Anthropic's amended S-1: $17 billion of quarterly revenue, a $567 million operating loss and $895.4 billion of compute commitments

On November 2, Anthropic filed an amended S-1 with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, filling in the fiscal third quarter (ended September 30) that the first filing left blank. Revenue was about $17 billion, up roughly 47% from $11.6 billion in the second quarter and more than tenfold from $1.4 billion a year earlier.

Profit flipped back into the red. The company posted a $567 million operating loss, one quarter after its first operating profit of $974 million. The cause is compute. Anthropic normally signs 12-to-18-month contracts to reserve capacity; demand in the quarter outran those plans, so it bought $5 billion of short-term compute at prices well above its long-term deals. Compute and infrastructure spending rose 77% from the prior quarter to $9.9 billion, and revenue per dollar of such spending fell from $2.08 to $1.72. Price cuts and contract discounts cost another $600 million of revenue, and a $2 billion non-cash charge tied to an employee donation-matching program pushed adjusted operating profit to about $2.5 billion, with margin down from 19% to 15%.

The amendment also discloses the total commitment for the first time: about $895.4 billion of purchase and lease obligations, including contracts signed after September 30, almost all of it for compute and data-center space, with payments stretching to 2045. Roughly $69.8 billion comes due within twelve months — more than a full year of revenue at the third-quarter run rate, and more than half of the $131.8 billion in cash and short-term investments the company held at the end of September.

That is not the ceiling. Anthropic plans to buy $125–150 billion of chips from Google and more than $20 billion from AMD; neither is counted yet because the amounts are not fixed, and the company estimates $150–175 billion of such future payments, which would take the total past $1 trillion. The money goes down the supply chain: Broadcom leads with about $161.2 billion of TPU equipment leasing, Google with at least $121.3 billion, SpaceX's AI unit with a contract capped at $120.5 billion, Amazon with at least $110 billion and Microsoft with $44.9 billion.

One more disclosure deserves attention: 5% to 7% of revenue in the first nine months came from customers who violated its usage policies or commercial terms, and the company has been tightening enforcement since August. Growth may take a hit as a result.

Why it mattersRead growth and commitments on the same page and the $567 million loss is not the story; the duration of the commitments is. A $17 billion quarter annualises to under $70 billion, yet $69.8 billion falls due in twelve months, and the full $895.4 billion is spread to 2045 — two decades of cash flow mortgaged to compute suppliers in advance. The cost lands in order: on shareholders, since IPO pricing must absorb an obligation that sits off the earnings line; on suppliers, whose capacity is locked up and whose bargaining power therefore rises; and on customers, whose prices are set by depreciation. Two numbers decide whether the loop holds: whether gross margin returns to 19%, and how the $69.8 billion due within a year actually gets paid.

Marvell buys Lumentum for $115 billion in stock, a 23.9% premium

On November 2, custom-chip maker Marvell said it would buy optical-components maker Lumentum in a $115 billion all-stock deal including debt, valuing the equity at $102.54 billion. It is one of the largest transactions of 2026 and one of the boldest bets yet on the AI build-out.

The terms: $1,132 per Lumentum share, a 23.9% premium to its last unaffected close on October 28, before deal talks surfaced. Lumentum holders receive 4.5 Marvell shares each and will own roughly 33% of the combined company on a fully diluted basis. Both boards approved unanimously; the deal is expected to close in the first half of calendar 2028. Marvell would owe Lumentum $5.8 billion if it walks away, Lumentum would owe $2.9 billion in the mirror case, and Marvell would pay $3.2 billion if regulators block the deal.

Investors were unimpressed: Lumentum fell 6.5% and Marvell 2.3% before the open, and both stocks have dropped sharply since talks were first reported. Nvidia, which invested $2 billion in each company earlier this year, declined to comment. Marvell's market value now exceeds $170 billion, helped by an August agreement to supply chips alongside Google's AI processors worth up to $120 billion in sales through fiscal 2033 and October guidance of $70–90 billion of fiscal 2031 revenue.

Lumentum separately guided second-quarter revenue to $1.58–1.63 billion, above the $1.48 billion analysts expected. CEO Matt Murphy framed the move as an architectural shift: "AI infrastructure is undergoing a fundamental architectural shift toward optical connectivity, creating an opportunity that comes along once in a generation." Marvell says the combined addressable market reaches $550 billion.

Why it mattersThis is not a purchase of current earnings; it is a purchase of a toll road. As AI clusters grow from hundreds of accelerators to tens of thousands, the bottleneck moves from compute to connection — optics, lasers, optical switching — and both price and volume rise. Marvell's $550 billion TAM is an admission that it is no longer merely a custom-silicon vendor. The price is 33% of the equity and a $115 billion balance sheet: if AI capex has peaked by the 2028 closing, the money reappears as goodwill and depreciation. The break-up fee structure is telling — a regulatory block costs $3.2 billion, far less than the $5.8 billion for walking away — which says the parties see demand, not antitrust, as the real risk.

11/03 08:33 Zhitong Finance ↗

Oracle moves to raise $20 billion in stock and bonds; shares fall about 6%

Oracle has disclosed a plan to raise $20 billion through a mix of stock and bonds, money earmarked for AI and cloud infrastructure and for repaying some debt, as it chases $225 billion of sales in fiscal 2030.

The bond sale spans six maturities from 2029 to 2046, one floating-rate and five fixed, with coupons and pricing still to be set. The stock fell about 6% on the news and is down 38% this year. Total liabilities stand at $125.34 billion, more than the $67.36 billion of revenue booked in fiscal 2026.

Management argues the turn is close. CFO Hilary Maxson expects cash flow to turn positive in the second half of fiscal 2028 — an outlook that lifted the stock about 11% in a single session on October 28. The company has pledged to cap AI-related additional financing at $20 billion through fiscal 2030, expects equipment spending to peak this fiscal year and next, and targets adjusted earnings above $21 a share in fiscal 2030.

Proceeds cover equipment, debt repayment, acquisitions, dividends and buybacks. What worries investors is the dependence on OpenAI, which is still losing money, and Oracle's smaller sales base relative to Microsoft and Amazon.

Why it mattersThis is the template for funding compute with debt: sign the customers, borrow to build the halls, then service the debt out of customer payments. That chain works only while customers pay on schedule and keep scaling; if their own funding slows, Oracle must fill already-broken ground with more expensive money. Half the $20 billion is equity, which puts the valuation on the table too — with the stock down 38% this year, issuing here dilutes far more than it would have a year ago. Watch two things rather than the revenue target: the size of capitalised interest, and how much of the fiscal 2028 cash-flow turn comes from collections.

11/02 09:28 Seoul Economic Daily ↗

Korea lifts its 2027 AI budget to 21.3 trillion won, nearly double this year's

In a budget address to the National Assembly on November 2, President Lee Jae-myung said his government will pour 21.3 trillion won into three flagship programs — semiconductors, physical AI and AI data centers — nearly double this year's level.

The split is explicit. A dedicated semiconductor account underpins 2 trillion won of capacity expansion, including earlier operation of fabs in the greater Seoul area and a new southwestern cluster, plus 2.1 trillion won for power, water and industrial-park infrastructure. Physical AI gets 500 billion won for field trials across eight sectors including manufacturing, smart factories and defense. Frontier AI receives 4.7 trillion won with the stated goal of pushing homegrown models into the global top tier, alongside public-facing AI agents that walk citizens from information to applications.

Research spending reaches a record. The NEXT 10 strategic technology fields get 13.8 trillion won, and the overall R&D budget rises to a record 39.5 trillion won. Renewables take 2.8 trillion won, the electric-vehicle subsidy quota rises to a record 430,000 units, and culture receives 11.6 trillion won.

The total budget is 820.9 trillion won, up more than 10% on this year, with the increase carried largely by tax revenue from the semiconductor upcycle. On talent, the plan trains 2,000 specialists for chip design and regional semiconductor industries and extends AI education from students to job seekers and incumbent workers.

Why it mattersThis is a budget that pledges tax receipts from one cycle to fund the next. The 820.9 trillion won total is financed by a chip boom, and 21.3 trillion won of the AI money flows mainly back into fabs, power and data centers — using today's chip profits to bet on tomorrow's chip demand. The structure is worth noting: physical AI receives 500 billion won, about 2.4% of the AI envelope, while hardware and capacity take the rest, which implies the government sees the binding constraint as power and production, not algorithms. The test arrives in two years: if memory prices roll over and the tax windfall disappears, what keeps this spending going?

Tencent Hunyuan ships Hyra 2.0, an AI research system that builds tools instead of solving puzzles

On November 2, Tencent's Hunyuan team released Hyra 2.0, an AI research system that shifts the emphasis from having an AI attack open scientific problems directly to having general agents build and upgrade the scientific models, workflows and software tools that later research reuses.

The previous version set the precedent: Hyra 1.0 drove an open model to solve an open problem in additive combinatorics that had stood for more than fifty years, validating the approach of handing an AI a problem with a clear scoring rule and letting it iterate.

The architecture splits research into three tiers. An Overall layer aligns with the human researcher; Mission layers own a single question over time and decide which routes to abandon; Workers read code, run experiments and diagnose failures independently, and are allowed to challenge Mission's judgement. Storage is likewise separated into project state, tiered agent memory and a shared knowledge base called Books. When a project is reviewed, transferable methods are written up as skills that later tasks can call directly.

The company says a batch of tools has already been built and upgraded, and that the results will be published in a GitHub repository for the community to test and reuse.

Why it matters"Intelligence compounding" is a plain accounting idea: if every project starts from zero, an AI's output is linear; if tooling accumulates, later projects get cheaper. Hyra 2.0 bets on the second, at the price of decomposing research into three manageable layers and keeping a human at the Overall layer. That also defines its boundary — problems with clear definitions and verifiable scoring rules suit it; open questions where the scoring rule itself is contested do not. For Tencent, this moves Hunyuan from model vendor toward research infrastructure. For everyone else, one measurement decides whether it works: how often the skills deposited in Books get reused on the next project.

DeepSeek launches Creator Mode: describe a plugin in one sentence, install a pomodoro timer in 5 minutes 24 seconds

On November 2, the DeepSeek Harness team launched Creator Mode, which turns a natural-language description into an installable custom plugin that extends Harness's tools, skills and interface. The tagline: "Your Harness. You Define It."

Seven bilingual demo prompts shipped alongside it, covering a 3D landmark gallery, an interactive Transformer visualisation, pixel-art app themes, a poster design workbench, a video editor, an academic paper reader and a session-statistics tool.

The mechanics: Harness loads its Cordis plugin-development skill, inspects the runtime and available interfaces, writes the configuration and code, installs the plugin through its own plugin manager and then checks that it runs. In the official demo, a floating pomodoro timer went from request to installed-and-verified in about 5 minutes 24 seconds.

The team notes that some demo plugins needed several rounds of conversation, so a single prompt may not reproduce the final result shown on video. The context: Harness is an open-source agent framework built on Cordis around the idea that everything is a plugin, available on desktop and web, and still in open beta with core plugins and APIs under active iteration.

Why it mattersThis pushes past "AI writes some code" to "AI rewrites the environment it runs in". The difference is permission: a plugin that installs can read session data, call tools and stay resident. A pomodoro timer is the harmless end of that spectrum; a session-statistics tool reads token spend and cost, and a paper reader reads whatever the user uploads — once such plugins are distributed by third parties, who can see what becomes a new question. Strategically, the plugin ecosystem matters more to DeepSeek than any single feature: it is the only moat that keeps an agent framework from being decided solely by whoever has the best model. What to watch is the signing and permission model, review for third-party distribution, and how a broken plugin gets rolled back. Five minutes twenty-four seconds is a good demo; running safely for 500 days is a different claim.

Goldman Sachs pulls AI coding tools from traders and bankers, keeps them for engineers

Goldman Sachs has cut most employees off from advanced AI coding tools including Anthropic's Claude Code and Microsoft's GitHub Copilot, leaving them to engineers, according to internal guidelines reported by Bloomberg. Traders and bankers had spent months using the tools to monitor trading flows and draft content; the rollback is reportedly unpopular internally.

The contrast with earlier messaging is sharp. In July, CIO Marco Argenti publicly praised "vibe coding" by non-technical staff: a trader or asset manager would prototype an idea with AI and hand it to an engineer to harden for production.

Spokesperson Nick Carcaterra attributed the change to "a variety of factors including productivity and risk management", adding that the experimentation phase is over and the tools will go to the people "best suited to leverage them for the long term". The reporting does not detail which roles keep access or how data access is tiered.

Why it mattersThis is a withdrawal of permissions, not a failure of the technology. Handing coding agents to traders buys prototyping speed and costs two things: models touching sensitive data such as trading flows and client information, and ambiguity over who owns code that non-engineers push into production. Saying the experiment is over is an admission that the first benefit did not justify the second cost. The phrasing matters too — productivity and risk listed side by side suggests this was not a compliance decision alone. For tool vendors the signal is that enterprise growth will follow permission tiers rather than seat counts. Watch the procurement structure next: per seat, or per invocation inside a controlled environment?

MIT researchers have models read and write weights directly: 0.016% of parameters takes Qwen's maths from 7.9% to 48.1%

Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang and Omar Khattab of MIT CSAIL published work on November 2 under the name Weight-Space Programming: general-purpose LLMs reading and writing a network's weights directly, without an optimizer and without gradient descent.

Reading comes first. Handed 17,253 weights as text, GPT-6 Astra named the game each of four identically shaped policies had been trained on — Breakout, Pong, Pac-Man and Freeway — without being told the candidates. Strip the input and output layers from three 7.2-million-parameter character transformers and give it only the four middle blocks, and it still identified the training corpus as Python, romaji Japanese or English, and named every induction head with no false positives.

Writing is more direct. In a Kinetix car-on-ramp task where PPO settled into a local optimum and failed all 256 evaluation episodes, Astra changed 3.5% of the weights and succeeded in all 1,024. In three MinAtar games it wrote networks from scratch, with no data and no gradients, that beat PPO trained for 100 million steps — a Breakout score of 517.4 against 15.8, where PPO at 500 million steps reaches 25.3.

The language-model result is the one that scales. Facing Qwen3.5-0.8B's tendency to loop in mathematical reasoning, Astra traced the failure to a single direction written by the MLPs in layers 15 and 19 and subtracted it from 64 output columns in each — 131,072 weights, or 0.016%. With a budget of 50 forward passes, MATH-500 rose from 7.9% to 48.1%, GSM8K from 13.8% to 67.1% and IFEval from 21.0% to 62.3%, while the share of answers hitting the 32,768-token limit fell from 93% to 33%.

The boundary is explicit. The same method applied to Gemma 3 270M, which rarely loops, produced no edit worth more than about one point on MATH-500. The authors' reading: editing weights can remove an obstacle hiding a capability, but cannot add one the model lacks. They also tried to identify, from weights alone, which of two models had been fine-tuned to be sycophantic; all four attempts failed.

Why it mattersThis moves the entry point to optimisation. Changing behaviour used to require data, a loss and gradients; now it can be a targeted edit — 0.016% of parameters for 40 points of MATH-500. Efficiency has a second face, which is auditability: behaviour that can be edited at a point can also be implanted at a point. Two of the uses the authors list are defensive — finding backdoors without running inference, and unlearning without collecting data — and both work just as well for an attacker. Before trusting the result, check two things: whether the logs reproduce outside the lab (the authors released every interaction and the edited Qwen weights), and, at larger scale, where the return on editing weights stops beating the return on editing prompts.

Embodied AI
11/02 10:23 DoNews ↗

Agibot's GO-3 Preview takes a convenience-store robot from 28.6% to 85.7% in five days

On November 2, Agibot introduced GO-3 Preview, an embodied model it says scales learning across pre-training, post-training and post-deployment. The architecture, called WRAM (World Reasoning Action Model), links task reasoning, future prediction and action generation.

Training runs on two data streams: first-person human video supplies object interaction and task procedures, while robot trajectories tie observations and actions to physical outcomes. Post-training uses a distributed mixed-environment system called Genie Evolver, built on a hundred real robots plus physics simulation and world models.

The strongest numbers come after deployment. A G2 robot running the Genie Master agent framework, placed in a working convenience store without human demonstrations or hand-tuning, went from a 28.6% full-task success rate on day one to a stable 85.7% after five days. Single-task success ran 17.7 percentage points above conventional supervised fine-tuning. Demos include tabletop curling — 4 hours of online iteration beat the measured human average — a 5-block Jenga pull that kept the tower standing, and long-horizon fine manipulation such as jianbing and Lego assembly.

Agibot says the model tops three benchmarks — RoboDojo, RoboTwin and RoboColiseum — and will be fully open-sourced by the end of 2026. For now it is a preview; the weights are not out.

Why it mattersThe benchmark rankings are not the interesting part; the timeline is. 28.6% to 85.7% in five days means post-deployment learning has moved into a real store rather than iterating inside a simulator. Three numbers have to be read together, though: 85.7% is full-task success, 17.7 points is a gain over fine-tuning, and 4 hours applies to one curling task — none of them is general capability. The divide comes after open-sourcing: if the community reproduces 85.7% in other stores, retail robots move from demonstration to shift scheduling; if not, this remains a video. Store work is also forgiving — a failure means handing over the wrong drink — whereas the same 14.3% failure rate in a pharmacy or a kitchen is an incident.
