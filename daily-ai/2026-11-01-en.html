DeepSeek open-sources a Super-agent design: agents that file their own pull requests

On the evening of October 31, DeepSeek published an engineering note titled “Super-agent: toward more autonomous agent systems” along with a prototype repository, dsh-superagent-prototype. The problem it targets is not whether a model can do a task, but that a person can only track three to five live sessions; context has to be carried by hand between chat, code and mail.

The design has two layers. A router receives messages, assigns work and keeps global context; a pool of workers executes in parallel without blocking each other. A worker can subscribe to a pull request, so later review comments go straight to it instead of back through the router. Incoming messages pass two filters: programmatic rules drop noise, then a model decides whether the rest deserves action.

The reported experiment simulated a workspace with roughly 300 messages and 20 work requests over five runs. Super-agent finished with a median of 12.4 minutes and a 95th percentile of 15.7 minutes; the single-session baseline reached 29.5 minutes at the 95th percentile under a neutral prompt. The cost is stated too: disabling event pass-through raised router decisions by about 30% and cached-token reads by about 37%.

Two engineering details are worth noting. An asynchronous ReAct loop already exists in DeepSeek Harness but is not on by default, and compute, state and execution environments are separated, with a watchdog that watches for loops, drift and failures. Beyond the note, only the prototype repository can be verified; there is no public product.

Why it mattersThe real change is who remembers progress. Instead of a person tracking where each thread stands, a router holds global context and workers subscribe to follow-ups. That saves attention and spends tokens and scheduling complexity. The 12.4-versus-29.5 comparison measures multi-task throughput, not per-task quality. When outsiders get access, the first number to watch will not be completion time but the false-positive rate of “acting on its own” — because when an agent finds a bug by itself, someone has to pay for the ones it invents.

Anthropic renames its top tiers and drops the five-hour window for Ultra

Anthropic reshuffled its Claude subscriptions on October 31. Max 5x and Max 20x become Max and Ultra at the same $100 and $200 a month. The substantive change sits in the $200 tier: verified Ultra accounts no longer wait for a five-hour window to reset and can work continuously within their weekly allowance.

Everything else holds. Weekly usage stays at ten times Pro, new models and features arrive first, and Claude Code plus long-running background tasks are supported. Existing subscribers migrate automatically; new accounts wait for verification, which is assessed from account and payment history.

Mike Overell, who runs subscription products, explained the rename. The original 20x referred to a 20-times allowance inside a single five-hour window, but once weekly caps arrived the weekly allowance was only ten times Pro. He apologised for the earlier framing.

The timing is awkward: OpenAI doubled Pro limits a day earlier. In June, the Wall Street Journal reported a consumer lawsuit over how the high-priced tiers advertised their limits, centred on the real usable volume of Max 5x and Max 20x.

Why it mattersRemoving the five-hour window looks like loosening, but it swaps time slicing for a weekly total. Users are interrupted less; the total is unchanged, so heavy users simply spend the same allowance in one continuous stretch. For Anthropic this answers a lawsuit and a long-running complaint, and it also clears the way for long-horizon agent work — being cut off mid-task is a product defect, not a UX preference. The place to watch is what happens when the weekly allowance runs out, because that is where the real price boundary sits.

Tencent’s Hy4 meets Alibaba’s Qwen4: same generation, same week

Writing in TMTPost on October 31, Zimubang reported that Tencent has just announced an imminent formal release of Hunyuan Hy4 while Alibaba signalled in Europe that Qwen4 arrives next week. The two companies usually staggered their launches; this time the version numbers, the window and the pitch — agents and real productivity — all line up.

Per the previews described in the piece, Qwen4’s architecture was first validated on Qwen3.8-Flash-Next: attention combining Gated DeltaNet with Qwen Sparse Attention, a 125B backbone plus a 51B N-gram embedding, 6B active parameters per token, and a training cost one-ninth that of Qwen3.7-Plus. The open question for Qwen4 is whether that architecture survives flagship scale.

The other thread is recursive self-improvement. Alibaba says Qwen3.8-Max can build training pipelines, generate data, design experiments and localise defects with almost no human involvement, sustaining more than a month and 33 effective iterations, lifting its Artificial Analysis score from 40 to 45. On inference it adapted itself to T-Head’s new architecture for a 96% gain in single-instance throughput; in chip co-design it iterated for 60 hours and made more than 10,000 EDA calls to cut physical implementation area by 42%.

On the Hy4 side, Tencent emphasises long-horizon and real productivity tasks, better chain-of-thought efficiency and its first visual understanding, alongside a Hy4 Flash tier for high-frequency calls. The organisational subtext is explicit: Qwen4 is Liu Dayiheng’s first full generation in charge, and Hy4 is the formal product of Yao Shunyu’s rebuild of pretraining and reinforcement-learning infrastructure.

Why it mattersThe useful part is not the benchmark table but whether the two firms define agent capability the same way. Alibaba is betting on long-horizon success rates and on self-improvement to dilute training cost; Tencent is betting on visual input and product feedback loops. Different definitions make leaderboards incomparable. What separates them is completion rate, rework rate and cost per finished task on the same real job. If the self-improvement numbers hold up, what changes is not one generation’s ranking but the engineering-hour budget of the next one.

OpenAI puts safety on a schedule: a Community Safety Report from early 2027

OpenAI said in a post dated October 30 local time that it will publish a Community Safety Report on a regular cadence, covering violence prevention, other community-safety topics and how well its safeguards actually work, according to IT之家 on October 31. The first edition arrives in early 2027 with data for the second half of 2026, then every six months.

One figure is already out: in the third quarter of 2026 alone, OpenAI referred more than 200 cases to law enforcement.

The product is being tightened in parallel. For past mass-casualty attacks, ChatGPT gives only a minimal account and withholds operational detail — weapon specifications, acquisition routes, and casualty comparisons that could feed notoriety — regardless of the user’s age or stated motive. Access to firearms and weapons information for teenagers faces a stricter threshold even when a question looks harmless.

The more consequential change is a circuit breaker. When a conversation shows fixation on violence or intent to seriously harm others, ChatGPT can end it; repeated interruptions for the same account can trigger a temporary freeze. OpenAI says it is building a way to notify parents when a teen account is frozen.

Why it mattersTurning safety into a twice-yearly report makes it auditable, and therefore comparable: termination rates, referrals and false positives will be lined up against other labs and against an experience where nothing is interrupted. Ending a conversation moves the decision from the model’s output to the account layer, which raises the question the report will not answer: when the system is wrong, does the user lose a chat or an account, and where is the appeal. Those details decide whether anyone trusts the numbers.

XHEART’s X7 Ultra: scoring an automotive chip with Momenta’s world model

XHEART, a chip company founded just over two years ago, made its public debut on October 31 with the X7 Ultra, an automotive-grade SoC. It quotes 700 TOPS, but the two numbers it leads with are not throughput: similarity between the quantised model’s output and an FP32 reference above 99.99%, and NPU utilisation of 98.7%.

Its proposed metric is “large-model compute”: rather than lab benchmarks, run the production autonomous-driving model on the silicon, with the constraints that the model architecture cannot change, precision cannot drop and driving quality cannot be traded away. The reference model here is Momenta’s R7 world model. The two firms have no direct equity link, but Momenta founders Cao Xudong and Xia Yan hold XHEART shares indirectly through a controlling shareholder; the earlier X7 is already in production in SAIC Volkswagen vehicles.

The hardware brings its own figures: a dedicated lidar accelerator processing 20 million points per second for thousand-line point clouds; camera-pipeline stability up 100% over conventional designs; memory-bandwidth consumption down 80% and energy efficiency up 100% against common products; an Arm v9 automotive CPU that the company calls Arm v9’s first landing in China, with 20% more core performance and 10% better efficiency; ASIL-B on the SoC and ASIL-D on the integrated MCU.

Cao’s framing is that large models now define chips: if driving models are to support L4 at scale, the vehicle side may need 10B or even 100B parameters, so silicon and model architecture have to evolve together.

Why it mattersChanging the unit of evaluation from TOPS to “how fast a production model actually runs” pushes responsibility from the chip to the software stack. If the model cannot be altered, performance has to come from compilers, kernel libraries and scheduling; one weak link and 700 TOPS buys nothing. Buyers gain a way to accept silicon per model version. Suppliers take on a harder obligation: they are no longer selling a die but a toolchain that must keep pace with model releases. The real test is not the launch but the next model version — and whether the toolchain has to be rewritten for it.

Nvidia shows optics-on-interposer prototype, and still no timeline

At SEMICON West 2026 in San Francisco, Nvidia displayed a prototype of its next co-packaged optics design, which it calls OOI — Optics-on-Interposer, per Shanghai Securities News via TMTPost on October 31. The company gave no commercial timeline and said only that it has prototype samples in hand.

The roadmap itself is clear. Rubin takes first-generation CPO from the second half of 2026 and scales in 2027; Rubin Ultra extends CPO to rack-to-rack links after 2027.

The report also notes that, in the near term, OOI will not affect China’s optical-communications industry.

Why it mattersMoving the optical engine onto the interposer shortens the last few millimetres of electrical signalling, which is where power and signal integrity get expensive as racks grow. Between prototype and volume sit packaging yield, thermal management and serviceability: a pluggable module can be swapped, while a co-packaged engine means replacing the board. Nvidia withholding a timeline suggests it is waiting on those three numbers itself. For the supply chain, the winner is not whoever makes optical modules but whoever can build a repairable interposer package.

Apple overtakes Nvidia: $5.03 trillion against $4.93 trillion

Reviewing Friday’s US session on October 31, Securities Times reported the Nasdaq Composite down 2.86%, the Nasdaq 100 down 3.46%, the Dow down 1.09%, the S&P 500 down 1.82% and the Russell 2000 down 1.66%. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 7.20%, its worst day since June, with Intel down more than 28% — its largest single-day drop on record.

Chip names fell almost across the board: Qualcomm down more than 11%, AMD more than 10%, Arm nearly 10%, Applied Materials more than 7%, ASML more than 6%. In storage, SanDisk fell more than 13% and SK Hynix nearly 8%; in optical interconnects, Coherent dropped more than 11% and Marvell more than 10%. Among mega-caps, SpaceX fell more than 6% and Nvidia more than 4%, while Microsoft, Meta, Google, Amazon and Apple each lost more than 1%; Tesla slipped 0.87%.

That left Apple at $5.03 trillion against Nvidia’s $4.93 trillion, back on top globally. The report attributed the selloff to disappointing results from large technology companies including Intel and Amazon, after Google and Meta had already underwhelmed.

There was a financing signal too. According to the Financial Times, as tech stocks slid and investors grew uneasy about the scale and payback period of AI spending, OpenAI cut its latest fundraising target by $10 billion, now seeking about $20 billion at a $1.25 trillion valuation, below last month’s $30 billion plan.

Why it mattersThe change at the top is not about technology roadmaps but about where cash flow sits. Apple sells stock already in people’s hands; Nvidia sells compute not yet built. When the payback period on capex lengthens, valuations shift one notch from compute narrative toward cash-flow narrative. OpenAI trimming its target by $10 billion is the private-market version of the same event: not a lack of need, but fewer buyers at that price. The next thing to watch is hyperscaler capex guidance, because that is the master valve for the whole chain.
