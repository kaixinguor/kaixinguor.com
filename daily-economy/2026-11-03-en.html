11/03 08:40 People’s Bank of China ↗

The PBOC lifts the FX deposit reserve ratio to 6% from 4%, effective November 15

At 08:40 on November 3, the People’s Bank of China said it would raise the foreign-exchange deposit reserve ratio for financial institutions by 2 percentage points, from 4% to 6%, starting November 15, "to strengthen the management of foreign-currency liquidity."

The instrument does not touch renminbi interest rates; it governs foreign currency inside the banking system. Banks must set aside a share of their FX deposits with the central bank, so a higher ratio leaves fewer dollars free to lend and raises the marginal cost of onshore dollar liquidity. It neither injects nor withdraws renminbi, which makes its effect on the exchange rate indirect: it changes bank FX positions first, then the price at which companies buy dollars or settle cross-border payments.

The statement is one sentence long. It does not disclose the deposit base, which institutions are covered, or whether the ratio is tiered; it applies to "foreign-exchange deposits at financial institutions" from November 15. That leaves banks less than two weeks to adjust their positions.

On the same day the central bank also convened a joint meeting with the financial regulator and the securities regulator on two capital-market facilities (next item). One action tightens foreign-currency liquidity; the other supplies cheap renminbi to the equity market. Opposite directions, same aim: keeping liquidity inside channels the authorities can see.

Why it mattersThis is a tool that moves foreign currency and leaves the domestic rate alone. Lifting the set-aside from 4% to 6% pulls a slice of freely usable FX out of the banking system, raising the marginal cost of buying dollars, paying abroad and borrowing in FX. The signal matters more than the size: the central bank chose a quantity instrument for FX liquidity instead of rates or verbal guidance. Watch three things — the spread between the onshore fixing and offshore renminbi, banks’ FX loan-to-deposit ratio, and whether FX deposits actually fall after November 15. If this is a hedge against seasonal dollar demand, it is technical housekeeping; if more follows, it is the first step of a change in direction.

11/02 20:06 Securities Times · People’s Financial News ↗

Buyback relending cut to 1% and made a permanent facility

On November 2, the People’s Bank of China, the financial regulator and the securities regulator held back-to-back meetings on two facilities that support the capital market: the swap facility for securities, fund and insurance companies, and the relending programme for share buybacks and share purchases.

The stock numbers: the swap facility, launched in October 2024 and rolled over several times, has an outstanding balance of 105 billion yuan. Buyback and purchase loans have signed about 440 billion yuan and disbursed more than 280 billion yuan.

Two substantive changes. First, buyback relending moves from a temporary arrangement to a permanent institutional one, with the relending rate cut from 1.25% to 1%; financial institutions may add a reasonable spread and expand lending. Second, the swap facility will be operated "in due course", with more participating institutions, an appropriately lower swap fee, and simplified collateral and operating arrangements.

Twenty-three securities and fund companies and 26 banks attended, with CICC, Guotai Haitong, CITIC Securities, E Fund, ICBC, CCB, China CITIC Bank and Industrial Bank among those speaking. PBOC Deputy Governor Zou Lan, NFRA Deputy Director Cong Lin and CSRC Vice Chairman Liu Haoling addressed the meeting.

Why it mattersCutting 1.25% to 1% and converting a pilot into a standing facility changes what a bank can put on its balance sheet. A "pilot quota" is reviewed annually; a permanent programme can be written into long-term products. The 440 billion yuan signed and 280 billion yuan disbursed show the facility has moved from window guidance into routine business. The binding constraint is not the cost of money but demand: whether a listed company buys back stock at this price depends on its share price and cash flow, not on 1%. For the market this is a floor, not a lever. For banks it is a thin-margin, capital-friendly business whose appeal depends on how the capital charge is computed.

11/02 Xinhua Finance ↗

Fed holds 10-2 with both dissents wanting a hike; markets price 85% for December

The Federal Reserve left the funds rate at 3.75%–4% on a 10-2 vote. Governors Cook and Cleveland Fed President Hammack dissented in favour of a 25 basis point increase. The statement upgraded its description of the economy to "strong expansion"; Chair Warsh stressed his anti-inflation stance, rejected a "soft inflation target", gave no clear December guidance, and announced fewer rate meetings from 2027.

Officials sounded tougher than the decision. Dallas Fed President Logan said at least two more 25 basis point hikes are needed before a pause; St. Louis Fed President Musalem called inflation "too high" and said action should come "soon"; Richmond Fed President Barkin said the direction is clear and only the speed is in question. Commerzbank notes the dollar barely moved after the decision while markets still assign about an 85% probability to a December hike.

Elsewhere: the ECB held its three key rates at 2.50%, 2.65% and 2.90%, with President Lagarde saying the council seriously discussed a hike; the ECB confirmed Lagarde will step down on February 7, 2027, nearly nine months early, and that the search for a successor has begun.

The Bank of Canada raised its policy rate 25 basis points to 2.5%, its first increase since July 2023, and projects inflation averaging 3.1% in 2027. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand held at 2.75% when a hike was expected. The Bank of Japan voted 8-1 on October 30 to keep its policy rate at 1.25%, while raising annual ETF sales from about 330 billion yen to about 860 billion yen starting January 2027.

Why it mattersThe global split is no longer about whether to cut; it is about who hikes first. Both dissents at the Fed wanted tightening, and an 85% market probability for December tells you this is not a tail scenario. For emerging markets this is the worst combination: high dollar rates, high oil, and pressure on local currencies — the PBOC’s FX reserve move the same morning belongs to exactly this backdrop. The data that decide it are US core CPI and payrolls for October. A surprise in either shifts the whole 2027 rate path, and emerging-market capital flows will reprice before the FOMC meets again.

Rebates, vouchers and car subsidies: many economies are handing out cash to consumers

Since the start of 2026, the United States, Japan, Europe and South Korea have rolled out measures to boost household spending, responding to falling real purchasing power and the risk of "low growth plus high inflation". A survey by Economic Information Daily finds the results hinge on energy prices, fiscal room and whether retailers pass anything through.

The pressure is concrete. US GDP grew 3.4% annualised in the third quarter, the fastest in a year, but the personal consumption expenditure price index rose 3.6% year on year in September and accelerated; regular petrol and diesel averaged about $1.19 and $2.65 a gallon above a year earlier, and the University of Michigan consumer sentiment index sits at the second-lowest level in its 74-year history. Euro-area inflation rose back to 3.8% in September, energy prices jumped 18.8%, EU GDP growth is expected to slow from 1.5% in 2025 to 1.1% this year, and French consumer confidence has fallen to a 13-year low.

The measures differ. The US tax package delivers its personal income tax relief as a one-off refund in early 2026 — a "delayed stimulus". France plans to raise electric-vehicle support from €5.5 billion to €10 billion a year by 2030 and to lease 100,000 EVs to low-income drivers; Italy offers up to €11,000 per car depending on income and scrappage; Germany pays €1,500 to €6,000 per vehicle. Japan will cut the consumption tax on food from 8% to 1% for two years from April 2027. South Korea is issuing vouchers of 100,000 to 600,000 won to the bottom 70% of earners and raising EV subsidies from 780 billion to 936 billion won.

The constraints are equally clear. The Bank of Japan estimates the food tax cut will subtract 1.3 percentage points from CPI in fiscal 2027, but how the annual fiscal gap gets funded is undecided; a former Shizuoka University professor notes that sellers are under no obligation to cut prices, and grocery chains already squeezed by rising costs may keep raising them. Europe faces high public debt and internal opposition; South Korea has cut its growth forecast and seen youth unemployment rise.

Why it mattersThese policies add demand, not supply. Rebates, vouchers and car subsidies put cash in households’ hands and can lift retail sales for a quarter, but they do not move energy prices or capacity. Whether Japan’s tax cut reaches the shelf depends on retailers; Europe’s EV subsidies are paid for by a tax base still built on combustion cars. The real cost sits in the fiscal accounts: the EU’s Social Climate Fund is €86.7 billion, France doubles EV support through 2030, and nobody has said where the money comes from. For investors the value is in timing — refunds land in the first quarter, vouchers in autumn — and that calendar is itself a trading signal.

Oil drops 7.36% in a night, chip stocks slide, China ADRs gain 2.25%

US equities split on November 2: the Dow rose 0.41%, the S&P 500 fell 0.51% and the Nasdaq lost 1.07%. Big tech weakened into the close, with Microsoft down more than 2% and Apple, Meta and Nvidia each off more than 1%.

Oil was the night’s biggest variable. December light sweet crude fell $7.66 to $96.46 a barrel, down 7.36%; January Brent fell $8.66 to $104.77, down 7.63%. The trigger was diplomatic: a senior US official said the United States would meet Ukraine and Russia in Abu Dhabi on November 10–11 to discuss ending the conflict, post-war security arrangements and de-escalation.

Haven assets rose in the same session. COMEX gold gained 1.83% to $3,956.90 an ounce and silver rose 3.31% to $50.72.

Chip stocks sold off late, with the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index down nearly 4%: Intel fell 8.80% after forecasting quarterly revenue below expectations and saying free cash flow would not be positive this year, and AMD dropped 7.52%. Chinese ADRs moved the other way — the Nasdaq Golden Dragon China Index rose 2.25%, with Bilibili up 6.55%, Alibaba 3.54% and JD.com 2.41%.

Why it mattersOil fell on a negotiation premium, not on demand. A 7.36% single-session drop took WTI from around $104 to $96.46, while gold and silver were bought at the same time — safe and risky assets together, which tells you the market bought lower volatility and lower inflation expectations, not peace. For inflation, oil is the only input that can move within two months; the 85% odds of a December hike may shift a few points on that alone. For chips, Intel’s cash-flow guidance matters more than the oil move: a company saying publicly that free cash flow will be negative this year is a company funding capex externally, the same problem the AI build-out faces. The 2.25% gain in Chinese ADRs is a third thread — money looking for assets uncorrelated with US tech.

11/03 07:48 Securities Times ↗

Apple posts $114.5 billion in quarterly revenue, 47.9% gross margin, and guides to 45–46%

Apple reported results for its fiscal fourth quarter ended September 26: revenue of $114.5 billion, up 12% year on year and above the $113.47 billion consensus, though slower than the 16% growth of the previous quarter. Diluted earnings per share were $2.10, up about 14% and above the $2.00 expected.

By segment, iPhone revenue was $57.58 billion, up 17%; Mac $10.79 billion, up 24%; iPad $6.23 billion, down 10%; wearables, home and accessories $8.3 billion, down 7.9%; services $31.57 billion, up 9.8%, roughly in line with the $31.58 billion expected.

Gross margin was 47.9%, above 47.2% a year earlier. The company said the figure included about one percentage point of benefit from tariff refunds; excluding that, margin was about 46.9%, slightly below last year.

The guidance matters more. Apple expects fiscal first-quarter revenue growth of 7% to 10% and gross margin of 45% to 46%. CFO Kevan Parekh said the forecast reflects memory-chip costs and the cost of Siri’s AI work, adding that memory purchase prices are still rising; operating expenses climbed 21% on higher research spending. The stock fell more than 4% during the earnings call.

Why it mattersThe story is in the gap between two lines: revenue beat, margin guidance falls. Of that 47.9%, about a point came from tariff refunds; strip it out and you have 46.9%, against guidance of 45–46% — the company itself expects further compression. The reasons are specific: memory costs and Siri’s AI costs. This may be the first time an "AI tax" shows up explicitly in a hardware maker’s margin guidance: models must fit into phones, memory gets dearer, operating costs rise 21%, and all three bills land on one number. Watch pricing next — hold prices and the margin absorbs the cost; raise them and demand elasticity gets tested immediately. Apple reclaimed the world’s largest market value from Nvidia on Friday; whether it keeps that title depends on which choice the market pays for.
