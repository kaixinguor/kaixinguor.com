PBOC to inject Rmb500bn of seven-day repos and Rmb800bn of MLF on November 25, draining Rmb200bn of long-term liquidity

The People's Bank of China said on November 24 that it will conduct Rmb500bn of seven-day reverse repos and Rmb800bn of one-year medium-term lending facility operations on November 25, both at fixed quantity with rate tenders and multiple-price allotment, Securities Times reported on November 25.

The demands all land on the same day. November 25 is a reserve-requirement settlement date, the week's peak for net government-bond payments, and the maturity date for Rmb1tn of MLF and Rmb80bn of treasury cash deposits. The seven-day line covers the month-end and payment gap; the Rmb800bn of MLF against Rmb1tn maturing is a rollover at a smaller size, draining a net Rmb200bn of medium- and long-term liquidity.

Prices have already moved. The DR001 central level has risen to around 1.3% this month and DR007 to around 1.4%, according to Ming Ming, chief economist at CITIC Securities, while outright reverse repos and MLF across both tenors have drained a cumulative Rmb500bn of medium- and long-term funds in November, per Shenwan Hongyuan.

The toolkit itself is changing gear. Since November 2 the central bank has tendered overnight reverse repos every working day and moved seven-day operations to fixed quantity, rate tender and multiple-price allotment, the format used here. Liu Yu, chief fixed-income analyst at Industrial Securities, expects funding to stay balanced and slightly loose if short-end support continues, with month-end demand lifting money rates only temporarily; Shenwan Hongyuan reads the shrinking MLF as a signal that Beijing wants to restrain a one-way rally in long bonds.

Why it mattersThis prints no money and drains none; it swaps maturities. Rmb800bn of MLF against Rmb1tn maturing cuts Rmb200bn of one-year liabilities, while Rmb500bn of seven-day repos adds Rmb500bn of seven-day liabilities. Banks plug the settlement and bond-payment hole with short money and the long hole stays open — so the tightness of funding is set at the short end, and the long-end yield is set by how much MLF is withheld. Who pays: banks short of reserves borrow at the tender rate and pay the month-end premium. Who earns: the central bank collects the allotment rate and turns the Rmb200bn gap into a constraint on duration. Why not a reserve-ratio cut: a cut delivers zero-cost, perpetual reserves and changes the quantity of liquidity, while repos and MLF are priced, dated liabilities that change its price and maturity — this time the second route was chosen. Two things to watch: whether December's bond supply and year-end precautionary demand require more seven-day volume, and whether long yields are being restrained or simply taken over after months of shrinking MLF.
Another angleFixed quantity with rate tenders and multiple-price allotment means the central bank sets quantity only: money of the same tenor clears at different rates and the marginal allotment rate is discovered by supply and demand. In engineering terms this is a multi-price auction, one dimension richer than a single-rate tender — you can see who wants money and at what price. The cost is a dispersed signal: the market cannot read one official rate out of it, least of all at the month-end turn.

EU to apply provisional safeguards on imported hybrid cars from December 1 as MOFCOM reserves WTO dispute rights

The European Commission has issued a preliminary ruling in its global safeguard investigation into hybrid cars and plans to apply provisional measures from December 1, Xinhua reported on November 24. A MOFCOM spokesperson said Beijing will follow the investigation closely and reserves all rights, including WTO dispute settlement and a request for trade compensation, to protect the legitimate interests of Chinese industry.

The timetable is tight: Brussels opened the global safeguard investigation on October 30 and decided on provisional measures less than a month later. The spokesperson said the two sides had held pre-initiation consultations under the China-EU trade and investment consultation mechanism and reached what he called a lawful, compliant, pragmatic and balanced arrangement on hybrid car trade.

Beijing's demands are procedural: honour the consensus, follow WTO rules to the letter, apply the principle of non-discrimination without exception, and conduct the investigation fairly. The spokesperson repeated that protectionism leads nowhere and that the two car industries are complementary and deeply intertwined.

The instrument matters. A safeguard is not an anti-dumping case: it does not require proof of dumping or unfair subsidy, only a finding that surging imports cause serious injury, after which tariffs or quotas can be imposed on all sources at once. It targets volume, not price, and not the conduct of any single company.

Why it mattersIf the provisional measures take effect on December 1, the first thing that moves is cash flow, not capacity: European importers and dealers settle at a higher landed cost and pass it down the chain to showroom prices, while Chinese exporters face orders postponed or rerouted rather than a finding of wrongdoing. Who collects: the EU budget, through the tariff, while European assemblers get a window of price protection. The real constraint is procedural — provisional measures usually last only the months before a final ruling, and consultations continue in the meantime; Beijing puts dispute settlement alongside trade compensation, the latter the specific remedy the Safeguards Agreement gives exporters, and the word used was "reserves", not "has filed". Two things to watch: whether the provisional measures turn into a long-term quota at the final ruling, and whether European capacity can cut costs inside the window — if it cannot, consumers there pay the rebound when protection ends.

11/25 08:20 People's Daily Overseas Edition ↗

Digital-economy plan targets 12.5% of GDP from core industries by 2030

The National Data Administration is implementing a digital-economy action plan for the 15th Five-Year Plan period, aiming for core digital industries to reach 12.5% of GDP by 2030, People's Daily Overseas Edition reported on November 25. The plan, which implements tasks set out in the five-year plan outline, is meant to make the digital economy a leading form of high-quality growth.

The tasks come in five groups. The first is foundations: rules for data circulation and use, a price-formation and revenue-sharing mechanism for data as a factor, a capability programme for data-circulation service providers, a national integrated computing network, national data infrastructure, and pilots where data can support coordination across the "six networks" of planning, standards, operations and services.

The second is industrial fusion: research and supply of shared data technologies and equipment, high-quality industry datasets, new delivery models for data and computing services built around scenarios, and contract-based digital services. A smart-manufacturing programme will guide priority industries and supply chains through digital transformation with a "one chain, one policy" approach.

The third is the rulebook: a digital-economy promotion law, better statistical monitoring, infrastructure for cross-border data-flow services, a tax regime suited to new digital business models, funding support and a cluster programme for high-skilled digital talent. The administration said the plan will be tied to its annual work priorities and advanced as one package.

Why it mattersThe 12.5% is value added over GDP: the numerator is core industry, the denominator the whole economy, so it is a structural target rather than a growth target — reaching it requires the numerator to outgrow nominal GDP year after year, which no single round of orders can deliver. Who pays: the computing network, national data infrastructure and industry datasets are all capital spending, fronted by the budget, telecom operators and cloud providers and recovered through contract-based digital services sold to data users. Who earns: equipment, computing and data-service suppliers. The genuinely new language is in two phrases — a price-formation and revenue-sharing mechanism, without which data cannot be capitalised, and a digital-economy promotion law, which moves the rules from departmental notices to statute and gives cross-border flows, statistics and tax treatment a stable footing. Two things to watch: whether returns on the computing network show up on users' bills, and whether cross-border data moves under a negative list or under security assessment once the service infrastructure is built.

11/24 21:28 Xinhua ↗

MIIT and eight provinces launch 6G pilot and issue a five-point Global 6G Development Vision

At the 2026 Global 6G Development Conference in Shanghai on November 24, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology and eight pilot provinces and municipalities — Beijing, Hebei, Shanghai, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Anhui, Hubei and Guangdong — jointly launched a 6G innovation pilot built on central-local coordination, Xinhua reported.

The model has three layers: national coordination, differentiated local implementation and broad participation. MIIT is running the programme as a special action to pool research, industry, application scenarios and talent, with the aim of making national 6G innovation one board rather than eight.

A text came with it. At the opening, the IMT-2030 (6G) Promotion Group, together with major international organisations, industry alliances and leading companies at home and abroad, issued a Global 6G Development Vision with five principles: cross-domain fusion built on native intelligence, green and sustainable development across the whole chain, security and trust on a foundation of full-domain safety, an open global industrial ecosystem, and innovation-driven enablement of the economy and society.

The conference runs in Shanghai on November 24 and 25 under the theme of global collaboration on innovation, and is focused on releasing the latest work on 6G technical standards. MIIT chief engineer Zhong Zhihong told the meeting that 6G has entered a critical phase in which technical work is deepening and standards are being drafted in full, and that global industry, academia and users need to align more closely.

Why it mattersA pilot of this kind does not hand out money; it puts standards, spectrum and scenarios at one table — the centre owns coordination and standards, localities own scenarios and supporting funds, companies own R&D and validation. Who pays: the eight provinces and municipalities put up use cases, trial spectrum and industrial funds while Beijing supplies the standards and the special action. Who earns: equipment makers, chip designers and test-instrument suppliers get the first validation orders. The investment sequence is the reverse of 5G, which built networks first and looked for applications afterwards; for 6G the language is scenario-first, which means proving that low-altitude, factory and maritime users will pay before deciding how the network gets built. The constraint is the calendar: standards work has only just broadened out, spectrum and terminals are unsettled, and what a pilot produces at this stage is a list of scenarios and validation data, not revenue. Two things to watch: whether local funding survives across budget years, and whether central-local coordination turns into eight trial networks that do not interoperate.

Digital China unit wins 2,048 of 20,480 cards in China Mobile AI supernode tender with Rmb11.666bn bid

Digital China (000034) said on November 24 that its controlled subsidiary, Beijing Shenzhou Kuntai Information Technology, had received a letter of acceptance from the tender agent China National Postal and Telecommunications Appliances Group, confirming it as a supplier for China Mobile's 2026-27 procurement of next-generation AI supernode equipment, Securities Times reported.

Three numbers belong together: the tender covers 20,480 cards in total; Shenzhou Kuntai's bid was Rmb11.666bn excluding tax; and its share is 2,048 cards. By card count that is 10% of the programme, so no single supplier takes it all.

The definitions matter too. The Rmb11.666bn is a bid price, explicitly excluding tax, and the 2,048 is a share measured in cards rather than in money; no unit price was disclosed, so dividing Rmb11.666bn by 2,048 cards does not give a card price. The procurement spans two budget years, and a win is not current revenue: production slots, delivery, acceptance and collection all sit in between.

Why it mattersThe information here is not the Rmb11.666bn but the 2,048-out-of-20,480 ratio: operator AI procurement is structured as a multi-supplier split, so the price is set by the marginal winner rather than by a single lowest bid taking everything. Who pays: China Mobile's capital spending, ultimately billed to enterprises and individuals who buy tokens or projects. Who earns: supernode integrators, and above them GPU, switch-silicon and optical-module suppliers. Where it is recorded: as an order backlog at Digital China, not current profit — between the award and revenue recognition sit production scheduling and payment terms, and operator procurement usually collects more slowly than it ships, so working capital is what gets consumed. Two things to watch: who takes the remaining 18,432 cards, which sets the price centre for this round, and whether domestic computing stacks can turn selling hardware into selling computing services — hardware alone means margins fall at the next tender, services mean renewals.

Chip rout: Philadelphia semiconductor index falls 7.88% and Anthropic closes below its $83 IPO price

US stocks closed mixed on November 25 Beijing time, 21st Century Business Herald reported: the Dow rose 0.85% to 50,018.7, the S&P 500 fell 0.50% to 7,201.09 and the Nasdaq fell 1.48% to 23,733.54.

The damage was concentrated in chips. The Philadelphia semiconductor index fell 7.88%, with all 30 members lower: Analog Devices lost 17.37%, Astera Labs 13.88%, Credo Technology 13.07% and Texas Instruments 11.25%, while Intel, ARM and AMD each fell more than 8% and Broadcom 3.98%. Memory and optical names went with them: Micron fell 10.97%, SK Hynix 9.9% and SanDisk 11.13%, while Lumentum lost 10.52% and Marvell 9.1%.

Nvidia fell 5.86% to $155.38, leaving a market value of $3.74tn, down about $233.5bn (roughly Rmb1.6tn) from the previous session and giving back part of its 31.48% surge on November 24.

The private-market mark was hit as well: Anthropic closed down 6.54% at $81.91, below its $83 IPO price, weighing on the wider market. Chinese ADRs were mixed, with the Nasdaq Golden Dragon index up 0.12%.

Why it mattersThe shape of the sell-off is the story: the headline indices fell 0.5% to 1.5% while the semiconductor index fell 7.88% with every member down — what got repriced was the valuation of AI hardware, not US risk appetite. Who pays: the $233.5bn that took Nvidia from $3.98tn to $3.74tn is a mark-down for holders plus realised losses for leveraged accounts and option sellers. Who earns: anyone who sold into the 31.48% gain the day before. Anthropic is the second thread: a $83 price set in the primary market was rejected at $81.91 in the secondary market, pulling AI valuations from private marks back to public ones, and that line runs backwards into the terms of the next funding round and into existing shareholders' paper. Two things to watch: whether a one-day 7.88% fall is valuation digestion, which repairs within a week or two, or a turn in orders, which shows up in next quarter's guidance; and the fact that memory, optical and analog chips fell together, which says the market is discounting AI capital spending as a whole rather than punishing one company's governance problem.
