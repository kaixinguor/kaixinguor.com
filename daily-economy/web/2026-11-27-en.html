Bank of England cuts bank capital requirements by 30bp and says it can free about £100bn for lending

The Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee cut system-wide Tier 1 capital requirements by about 30 basis points on November 26, and by 60bp for domestically focused systemically important banks, whose risk-weighted requirement falls from 13.6% to 13.0%. For the major banks in aggregate, the requirement drops from 13.1% to 12.8%, around the FPC's 13% benchmark.

The Bank said the package could free about £100bn of lending capacity, according to Xinhua Finance. It combines a lower Pillar 2A add-on, a recalibration of geographic concentration risk and a fuller offset for the 2019 increase in the countercyclical buffer, which stays at a neutral 2%. Consultations on leverage-ratio and Pillar 2A details are due in early 2027.

Bank shares rose about 3% for Lloyds, NatWest, Barclays and HSBC, and 3.6% for Standard Chartered, before giving back some of the gain. UK Finance had argued for a bigger release, saying that returning half of it to shareholders could support £250bn of lending; the Bank's package was less than half that.

The same report flagged AI financing as a new channel of contagion. Hyperscaler bond issuance is set to exceed $305bn in 2026, about 8% of global investment-grade supply, the Bank estimated, as AI funding spreads from equity into public debt, private credit and more complex structures, with some firms financing others against future revenue commitments. It warned such arrangements could “get into difficulty” if markets came under stress or AI revenue expectations were cut. According to Xinhua Finance, the report put large tech firms' AI-related financial exposure at more than $4tn.

The report also set out the price backdrop: Brent crude at about $92 a barrel, up from $75 at the July report, and European spot gas at about €81/MWh from €46. Energy costs have pushed up inflation and expected policy rates; sovereign yields are near two-decade highs and some households' financial headroom is eroding.

Why it matters A 30bp cut changes the price of bank lending before it changes risk. With one percentage point less Tier 1 capital, the same equity supports more risk-weighted assets; the £100bn is capacity, not credit already extended, and it becomes lending only if households and firms want to borrow. Who pays: the safety cushion is thinner, shareholders earn a higher return on equity, and depositors and taxpayers carry the tail cost of deposit insurance. The line to watch is AI: $305bn of hyperscaler bonds, 8% of global investment-grade issuance, turns AI capex from a profit-and-loss question into a credit-market one. If AI revenue expectations fall, the first move is in spreads, not chip orders. The Bank drew its own line: cutting much further would weaken banks' ability to absorb shocks. Two reversal conditions: credit losses rise, or supervisors call out circular AI financing.
Another angle  For AI companies, the binding constraint is shifting from “can we buy the chips” to “can we borrow, and for how long”. A 30bp capital cut lowers UK banks' funding cost; AI data centres are priced over a decade. The two curves do not share a time scale.

US proposes 301 tariffs on 14 economies, with China facing a stacked 20% that could rise to 40%

The US Trade Representative proposed new tariffs on imports from 14 economies on November 25 under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, citing “structural overcapacity” in manufacturing. According to Global Times, Chinese goods would face an additional 20% on top of existing duties, rising to 40% if talks fail; Vietnam would face 35% (20% with a reciprocal deal), India 25% (17%), and the EU 15% (up to 20%).

The tariffs are not immediate. They must go through notice, public comment and a hearing, and could take effect as early as January 2027. US officials said the new rates would generally not stack on existing duties and would amount to “a few percentage points” of adjustment for most economies; China is the exception, the only target of stacked tariffs.

The legal ground has shifted. In February 2026 the US Supreme Court struck down the “reciprocal” tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, and those duties lapsed. Washington then imposed a 150-day temporary tariff and opened the Section 301 probe in March against 16 economies, including China.

China's Ministry of Commerce said on November 26 that it urged Washington to correct its approach and return to dialogue, adding that it would watch developments and reserve the right to take necessary measures. The foreign ministry said a tariff war benefits no one and called “overcapacity” a false premise. As of July 2026, the average US tariff on Chinese goods was about 36.5%, according to the Congressional Research Service; the US International Trade Commission puts the effective rate at about 22.8%.

Why it matters Separate the announcement from the effective date. This is a proposal, with a hearing and an exemption list before any duty applies, so it moves expectations rather than this month's landed cost. For most economies, the new rate largely restores the gap left when the Supreme Court struck down the 2025 “Liberation Day” tariffs. China is different: 20% is stacked, and 40% is the failure-to-agree ceiling. Who pays: US importers upfront, then consumers through retail prices; Chinese exporters absorb part through order shifts and price cuts. Who collects: the US Treasury, and third-country producers that can replace Chinese supply. What changes: the legal instrument moves from emergency powers to Section 301, but the rate range does not return to pre-2025. Watch two things: the final rate and exemption list after the January 2027 hearing, and whether the roughly $30bn reciprocal tariff cut with China is extended — the only variable that offsets the rest.

11/26 22:56 CNR (CCTV News) ↗

China's big four banks sign over Rmb600bn of buyback and share-purchase loans as the relending rate falls to 1%

CCTV reported on November 26 that the People's Bank of China, the financial regulator and the securities regulator have made the buyback-and-increase relending facility a permanent arrangement and cut its rate to 1% from 1.25%. The four largest banks have now signed more than Rmb600bn of related loan contracts.

By bank: ICBC has served more than 200 listed companies with nearly Rmb180bn of contracts; Agricultural Bank has agreements with nearly 200 companies worth over Rmb150bn; Bank of China has committed more than Rmb180bn to nearly 200 companies, with manufacturing over 65% of deals; China Construction Bank has signed over Rmb100bn with more than 100 clients, over 70% in manufacturing and private firms.

The facility was launched in October 2024. The central bank provides low-cost relending to commercial banks, which then lend on market terms to listed companies and major shareholders for buybacks or share purchases. It covers 21 national banks and five systemically important city banks.

Why it matters The 1% is the banks' funding cost, not the borrower's rate. Banks add a spread, and that spread is the subsidy. The Rmb600bn is contracted, not drawn; the real impulse depends on drawdowns and maturities. Who pays: the central bank's balance sheet, backed by the joint credit of the fiscal and monetary authorities. Who benefits: companies and major shareholders that get cheap funding, and the share prices that buybacks support. What changes: buybacks move from “company cash” to “bank loan” — leveraged purchases of one's own stock, which magnify returns on the way up and debt service on the way down. Watch two things: the drawdown ratio and average tenor, and how much goes to companies trading below book with low payouts — that is the part the policy is really trying to support.

China cuts fuel prices for a seventh time this year: gasoline down Rmb440 a tonne

The National Development and Reform Commission said on November 26 that retail gasoline and diesel prices would fall by Rmb440 and Rmb425 a tonne from midnight. At the pump, 92-octane gasoline, 95-octane gasoline and 0-grade diesel fall by about Rmb0.35, Rmb0.37 and Rmb0.36 a litre.

A 50-litre tank of 92-octane saves about Rmb17.50. After the cut, diesel in most regions will sell for Rmb7.90-8.10 a litre and 92-octane for Rmb7.70-7.80.

It is the seventh cut of 2026 and the second in a row, after a Rmb645/Rmb625 cut on November 12. So far this year there have been 14 increases, seven cuts and two unchanged windows. The next window opens at midnight on December 10.

During the pricing cycle, stalled US-Iran talks, higher US crude and gasoline inventories and OPEC+ output increases reinforced expectations of oversupply; a later geopolitical rebound did not change the direction, according to Zhuochuang Information.

Why it matters The Rmb440 is a wholesale cut; at the pump it is about Rmb0.35 a litre after consumption tax, VAT and retail margins, which is why the tank feels less than the tonne price. Who pays: refiners and wholesalers give up margin first, then retail passes it down. Who benefits: drivers and truckers; a 50-litre tank saves Rmb17.50 a fill. What changes: two consecutive cuts trim the imported inflation pressure that built in October, but pump prices are still above the start of the year, with 14 increases against seven cuts. Watch two things: the crude change rate for the December 10 window, and refinery utilisation — if cheaper fuel lifts demand, refining margins may bottom before crude does.

11/26 18:09 Gov.cn (Ministry of Commerce) ↗

China's services-trade plan: 25 tasks, with AI services and cross-border data singled out for the first time

Eight Chinese departments, led by the Ministry of Commerce, published the 15th Five-Year Plan for services trade on November 26. The plan sets out seven areas and 25 tasks, with five boxes and 10 special actions, the ministry said in its explainer.

For the first time, the plan splits services trade into producer services and consumer services. Producer services — professional services, international transport, finance and insurance, construction and agriculture — are to be made more export-competitive; consumer services — travel, culture and traditional Chinese services — are to build new export advantages, while China imports more high-quality services it needs.

Two other firsts: AI services trade and green services trade get their own sections, and a dedicated chapter covers cross-border flows of data, people, capital and technology. The goals are a larger services trade share of total trade and knowledge-intensive services as a growth engine; by 2035, China wants the scale and competitiveness of its services trade among the world's leaders.

The base is significant: China's services trade has passed $1 trillion and ranks among the world's largest, while the country is already the largest goods trader.

Why it matters Services trade is not mainly blocked by tariffs but by behind-the-border rules: whether data can leave, whether people can come, whether money can settle, whether technology can be traded. The 25 tasks matter less as a push to “sell more services” than as an attempt to lower those four frictions. Who pays: firms handling data compliance, visas and settlement, especially smaller service providers. Who benefits: exporters in professional services, transport, finance, insurance, construction and agriculture that can win cross-border work. What changes: policy used to be spread across statistical categories; now it is organised around producer and consumer services, which should tilt resources toward producer services that pull through manufacturing exports. Watch two things: whether a negative list for cross-border data actually lands, and the knowledge-intensive share of services exports. Without those two, the $1 trillion base remains an appendage to goods trade.

Saudi Jizan refinery hit by attack; Brent rises 2.07% to $96.57

The Saudi-led coalition said on November 26 that the Jizan refinery in southwestern Saudi Arabia was attacked by Yemen's Houthi movement. A coalition spokesman said attacks on civilians and civilian facilities would draw a firm military response; the statement gave no casualty or damage details.

Saudi state news agency SPA reported that a hostile aerial object hit a refinery in Jizan, starting a fire that was later brought under control and temporarily halting part of its operations. One Nepali worker was killed and several workers of other nationalities were injured, according to Jinshidian, citing SPA.

Oil markets reacted at once. ICE Brent for January settled $1.96 higher at $96.57 a barrel, up 2.07%; NYMEX WTI had no settlement because of the US Thanksgiving holiday. Shanghai crude's night-session front month closed at Rmb588.1 a barrel, down 0.88%.

Separately, Iran's Revolutionary Guard navy said two tankers sailing improperly hit mines north of Bandar Khasab and caught fire, including a supertanker carrying more than 2m barrels that was still burning. Ship-tracking data showed more than 26 crude, fuel and LNG tankers a day transiting the strait between November 1 and 19, against a seven-day average below 25 on at least two-thirds of October days.

Why it matters The target was a refinery, not an oilfield, so the first loss is refined-product and export-loading capacity, not crude reserves. Brent's 2.07% gain is a transport-and-insurance premium: shipowners add war-risk cover, buyers switch loading ports, refiners repair units, and those costs land in the delivered price. Who pays: refined-product importers in Asia and Europe, and the insurance market underwriting the tankers. Who benefits: producers and refiners with spare capacity that can fill the gap. What changes: the market had been trading oversupply; the attack pulls attention back to strait and Red Sea transit risk. But unless the strait is actually closed and OPEC+ keeps raising output, the premium can fade within weeks. Watch two things: when Jizan restarts, and war-risk premiums — insurance will price the real tension before crude does.

Five limit-ups later, Kelai Mechatronics gets an SSE letter: 176.81x PE, robots under 1% of revenue

The Shanghai Stock Exchange sent a regulatory work letter to Kelai Mechatronics (603960) on the evening of November 26 over its share-price moves. The letter was addressed to the listed company. The company also published a risk warning.

The numbers sit side by side: five consecutive limit-ups, a 61.10% gain over the period, turnover of 24.76% on November 26, a static price-to-earnings ratio of 176.81x and a price-to-book ratio of 4.37x, both well above the industry. The company said the rise had detached from fundamentals and warned of overheating and irrational speculation.

The company also took apart the theme. Its industrial-robot product is an automated loading and unloading system for container trucks in logistics; sales were no more than 1% of revenue in both 2024 and 2025, and orders are not growing materially. It has no humanoid-robot products.

Earnings are weak: net profit attributable to shareholders was a loss of Rmb5.45m in the first three quarters of 2026, and the adjusted loss was Rmb7.36m. The stock rose by the daily limit on four straight sessions from November 20 to 25, triggering two abnormal-volatility notices, and hit the limit again on November 26.

Why it matters A regulatory letter is not a penalty. Its first job is to put the “robot” label and the revenue share on the same page. A 176.81x static PE belongs to a company that lost money in the first three quarters; a 4.37x PB belongs to a business where robots are under 1% of revenue. The gap is filled by a small float and sentiment, not orders. Who pays: buyers at the limit, especially those who enter in the last few sessions. Who benefits: early holders and funds that sell into the volatility. What changes: once the company says it has no humanoid-robot business, the theme's imagination is compressed to the loading-machine line. Watch whether the exchange demands more disclosure and whether speculative seats stay on the trading list. Reversal condition: if orders or a restructuring actually land, revenue share will move before the share price — that is the fundamental signal.

11/26 21:20 Sina Finance (Eagle Eye) ↗

China Merchants Shekou injects Rmb19bn of its own cash into wholly owned Nanyou

China Merchants Shekou (001979) said in a filing on the evening of November 26 that its board approved a Rmb19bn cash injection into wholly owned Shenzhen Nanyou (Group) Co, funded entirely from the parent's own cash. The vote was 9-0.

Nanyou was set up in November 1984 with Rmb499m of registered capital. It develops and operates the Qianhai Shenzhen-Hong Kong modern services park, runs property management and leasing, and provides logistics and freight forwarding. It is also a holding platform with 32.8874% of China Merchants Qianhai Industrial, 35% of Qianhai Pingfang Park, 55% of Shenzhen Longsheng Industrial and 15% of China Shenzhen Ocean Shipping Agency.

At October 31, 2026, Nanyou had Rmb27.679bn of assets, Rmb18.050bn of liabilities and Rmb9.629bn of equity, a 65.21% debt ratio. Revenue for January-October was Rmb114.5m and net profit Rmb129m; for 2025, revenue was Rmb188.8m and net profit Rmb490m.

The company said the injection is meant to tighten ownership layers and improve internal management and operating efficiency. It is not a related-party transaction or a major restructuring, and does not need shareholder approval. Nanyou remains 100% directly owned; registered capital and the consolidation scope are unchanged.

Why it matters The Rmb19bn moves from the parent's account to the subsidiary's. Consolidated cash and equity do not change; what changes is that Nanyou's Rmb9.629bn equity is padded toward Rmb28.6bn and its 65.21% debt ratio falls. Who pays: China Merchants Shekou's shareholders, through the opportunity cost of Rmb19bn of cash. Who benefits: Nanyou's creditors, whose claim now sits behind a thicker equity cushion, and the Qianhai project partners, whose project company has more capital. What changes: this is not an acquisition or new land, but an internal capital reshuffle; the gain is governance and financing capacity, not current profit. Watch two things: registered capital stays the same while capital reserve rises, so the Rmb19bn becomes the base for any future stake sale or spin-off; and Qianhai's 2027 rent and sales receipts — that is the answer to whether the money comes back.
