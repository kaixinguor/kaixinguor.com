11/19 03:00 Federal Reserve ↗

Fed minutes: almost every official saw further hikes ahead, and two floated more than 25 basis points in December

The Federal Reserve published the minutes of its October 27-28 meeting on the afternoon of November 18 in Washington. Almost all participants judged that further increases in the target range for the federal funds rate would likely be appropriate in coming months to return inflation to 2% in a timely way. The committee held rates 10-2, with Governor Cook and Cleveland Fed President Hammack preferring a 25 basis point increase, according to Xinhua Finance; after the September move the target range stands at 3.75-4.00%.

A larger step was on the table: two participants said that if incoming inflation data disappointed, the December meeting might need to consider an increase of more than 25 basis points. Governor Waller, speaking to CNBC before the minutes landed, said he would back a 50 basis point move in December and tied that to core CPI running at 0.3% month on month, which the latest reading did exactly. The two-year Treasury yield, the maturity most sensitive to the policy path, rose from about 4.80% to 4.84% after he spoke. New York Fed President Williams said the situation did not call for aggressive action.

The underlying numbers in the minutes: staff estimated September PCE inflation at 3.6% and core at 3.1%; unemployment was 4.2% in September, payroll gains averaged about 50,000 a month over the three months to September, and average hourly earnings were up 3% on the year. Expectations for the end point also moved up — the Desk survey's median estimate of the longer-run nominal federal funds rate rose to about 3.4%, with a median path of one hike in December, another in March 2027 and a cut in early 2028.

Markets are already ahead of the document. Fed funds futures fully price a 25 basis point increase in December and another in January, with a further move possible by April. The data calendar leaves a gap: October PCE lands on November 25, but November CPI is not due until December 10, after the December meeting.

Why it mattersThe minutes say "keep going"; the market is asking "how many times", and the gap between the two is the risk premium. Put three numbers side by side — core PCE at 3.1%, unemployment at 4.2%, a 3.75-4.00% policy range — and the real policy rate on core PCE is only 0.65 to 0.90 points. Policy is already tight, inflation is not falling, so tight has to continue: that is the weight carried by the word "almost". What actually decides December is the missing data. The Fed must choose with no November CPI in hand, which shifts the burden from data to communication. Waller wants a "credible signal"; Williams wants no theatrics. The disagreement is not about whether to tighten but about who pays for a failed explanation — if a 25 basis point move in December fails to convince markets that January follows, the two-year yield will finish the sentence for them.
Another angle The section most readers will skip sits in the financial stability discussion: staff noted that debt tied to the AI buildout has grown quickly, much of it through complex and opaque structures, and that off-balance-sheet leasing commitments and circular financing among AI firms could amplify losses if returns disappoint — a risk most participants flagged. For anyone building compute, that is less a warning than a calendar: once supervisors start reading financing structures, the terms and duration available to data centre projects over the next twelve months get tighter, shorter and more expensive.

11/18 Ministry of Finance ↗

Finance ministry clears Rmb150bn of seven-year recapitalisation bonds at 1.40%, just 15 basis points over three-year paper

The Ministry of Finance said in government bond notice No. 197 of 2026, published on November 18, that the second tranche of this year's special treasury bonds for recapitalising central financial institutions had been allotted. The issue was planned at Rmb150bn and sold at Rmb150bn of face value, with a seven-year tenor and a coupon set by auction at 1.40%. Interest accrues from November 19, distribution runs to the same day, and the bond lists on November 23.

The terms are plain: a fixed-rate bond paying annual coupons every November 19, with principal and a final coupon returned on November 19, 2033. At 1.40%, Rmb150bn costs roughly Rmb2.1bn a year in coupons, or about Rmb14.7bn across the seven payment years.

The price matters more than the size. The market-making sale recorded in the previous issue cleared two- and three-year paper at 1.25% on November 17. This seven-year deal came at 1.40% — 15 basis points over three-year bonds and 15 over the 1.25% rate on the central bank's overnight reverse repos. The market charged 15 basis points for four extra years of duration.

The purpose is in the name: the proceeds recapitalise central financial institutions. The chain runs from the ministry's borrowing to bank capital to credit, with banks expanding lending against that capital. For the government the cost is a fixed Rmb2.1bn a year; for the banks it is tier-one capital that never has to be repaid.

Why it mattersRmb150bn, seven years and 1.40% together say that the sovereign is using its own 1.40% funding cost to hand banks capital they do not have to return. Against October CPI of 0.7%, the real coupon is about 0.7 points; against October PPI of 5.7%, it is deeply negative. That five-point gap between the two price indices is precisely where this bond is contested: buyers are marking it to CPI, users are marking it to PPI. For banks, the injection lifts the capital ratio and supports a multiple of new lending, with the multiple set by risk weights. For the treasury, the obligation is rigid — coupons every year, principal in 2033 — and if rates rise over those seven years, this becomes cheap outstanding debt. Judge the deal not on 1.40% but on where the seven-year yield trades after the November 23 listing: if it clears above 1.40% in the secondary market, buyers are underwater on day one and the next recapitalisation bond has to be priced higher.

11/18 18:00 Eurostat ↗

Euro-area October inflation confirmed at 4.0% as energy runs at 21.0% and adds 1.89 points

Eurostat confirmed on November 18 that harmonised consumer prices in the euro area rose 4.0% in October from a year earlier, up 0.2 points from 3.8% in September. The EU as a whole also ran at 4.0%. A year earlier the two stood at 2.1% and 2.5%.

The breakdown points squarely at energy: energy prices rose 21.0% and contributed 1.89 percentage points to headline inflation, the largest single item. Services rose 3.3% for a 1.55 point contribution, non-energy industrial goods 1.2% for 0.30 points, and food, alcohol and tobacco 1.5% for 0.29 points, Xinhua Finance reported.

Dispersion across member states is wide. Sweden was lowest at 0.6%, with the Czech Republic and Denmark at 2.1%; Romania ran at 7.0%, Lithuania 6.1% and Bulgaria 5.9%. Against September, nine member states saw inflation fall, two were unchanged and 16 rose.

Forecasters split on second-round effects. MUFG's base case is 25 basis points from the ECB in December and again next March, taking the deposit rate to 3.0%. S&P Global Market Intelligence judged that the final reading gave no stronger case for a December move but that 25 basis points remains the likely option while commodity prices stay high. Goldman Sachs expects core inflation to peak at 2.7% in the first quarter of 2027 and return to 2% by the end of 2028.

Why it mattersWith 1.89 of the 4.0% coming from energy, the ECB's dilemma is arithmetic: rate rises cannot lower the price of oil, only the second round. Energy is an external shock, and monetary policy hits it with a long lag and a short lever; what it can compress is services at 3.3% and wages. So the hiking path is really a bet that high energy prices do not reach wage bargaining, and the test comes in the first quarter of next year — if services have not cooled by then, two hikes will not be the end of it. For China's export chain, expensive European energy keeps pushing chemicals, aluminium and glass capacity outward, and trade flows reprice faster than rates do. The energy bill itself lands on European households and firms, not on the ECB.

11/18 23:02 Ministry of Commerce ↗

China and South Korea conclude phase-two FTA talks after eight years and 17 rounds, switching to a negative list

The Ministry of Commerce's international department answered questions on November 18 about the substantive conclusion of the second phase of the China-Korea free trade agreement. Talks took more than eight years and have now been substantially completed, with a high-standard outcome. The phase began in December 2017 and has held 17 formal rounds since the first in March 2018.

What changed is not tariffs but access rules. The phase upgrades the chapters and annexes on investment, trade in services, financial services and economic cooperation, and commits to a negative-list approach for investment, cross-border services and financial services. Cooperation arrangements cover traditional medicine services, culture and online services.

Goods trade has eleven years of groundwork behind it: the original agreement was signed on June 1, 2015 and took effect on December 20 that year, and has run through 11 rounds of tariff cuts. Chinese statistics put 2025 bilateral trade at $331.24bn, up 1.2%; for January to October 2026 the figure is $440.9bn, up 63.7% — ten months already exceed the whole of 2025.

What remains is technical: the two sides will verify the details of the final text, translate it and complete legal review before signing. The text will be published on the free trade area portal after signature.

Why it mattersThe difference between a positive and a negative list is a default, not a length. A positive list means closed unless listed; a negative list means open unless excluded, which moves the negotiation from adding items to keeping them out. For services, that is harder to reverse than a tariff cut — tariffs can be renegotiated, but access rules become part of a firm's cost of establishment. How much of the 63.7% trade growth is energy and chip prices rather than volume is not broken out in the official data; what is clear is that the goods dividend is already compounding and services are what this round actually opens, with the payoff arriving in the statistics three to five years out. For companies, the practical meaning is that over the next two years the predictability of the rules for setting up in Korea matters more than the tax rate.

11/18 15:39 CNR ↗

Shanghai and Shenzhen turnover drops to Rmb1.37trn, a 16-month low and more than 65% below January's peak

Combined turnover on the Shanghai and Shenzhen exchanges was Rmb1,369.563bn on November 18, down Rmb88bn from the previous session, CNR reported, citing closing data.

The figure breaks two records at once: it is below the 2026 low of Rmb1,409.2bn set on September 29, and it is the weakest reading since July 7, 2025.

Against the all-time high of Rmb3,941.484bn reached on January 14, 2026, turnover has shrunk by more than 65% — barely a third of the January peak remains.

Why it mattersTurnover is the denominator. Rmb1.37trn implies a lower turnover ratio, meaning less money is needed to hold the index where it is. That is evidence of shrinking liquidity and simple arithmetic for a zero-sum market: with no new money arriving, any sector that rallies must pull cash out of another. A volume trough usually resolves one of two ways — sideways until policy adds money, or a slow drift lower until earnings arrive. What separates them is not turnover itself but margin balances: if turnover falls while margin debt holds, leveraged accounts are absorbing losses; if both fall together, the flush is real. For late November this number matters more than the index level, because it is the visible trace of fiscal spending and credit supply. Turnover bottoms before earnings do, but not necessarily before policy does.

11/19 07:19 Shanghai Securities News ↗

WTI settles at $83.26, down 3.2%, while Brent slips 1.28% to $91.95

Crude fell sharply on November 18, Xinhua reported from New York. December light sweet crude on the New York Mercantile Exchange dropped $2.75 to settle at $83.26 a barrel, a fall of 3.2%. January Brent lost $1.19 to $91.95, down 1.28%.

The spread widened within a day: on November 17 Brent closed at $93.14 and WTI at $86.01, a gap of $7.13; on November 18 the closes were $91.95 and $83.26, a gap of $8.69. WTI fell more than twice as much as Brent, putting the pressure on the US side of the market.

The fundamentals turned over the same week, Xinhua Finance reported: the IEA and the US Energy Information Administration both moved their fourth-quarter global oil balance from deficit to surplus; OPEC+ raised output by 2.04m barrels a day in October; and US commercial crude inventories jumped 4.657m barrels in a week, far above expectations. Crude in the Strategic Petroleum Reserve fell to 276.4m barrels, the lowest since August 1982, after IEA members released 400m barrels in response to the conflict with Iran.

The domestic contract moved in step: the front-month crude futures contract in Shanghai fell 6% on November 17.

Why it mattersThis is not the geopolitical premium disappearing; it is the balance sheet being rewritten. A single week of inventory can be reversed by one tanker, but two agencies flipping a quarterly balance from deficit to surplus is a slower and harder signal to undo — once the table turns, hedgers and trend funds add in the same direction, which is what an "oversupply trade" means. The $8.69 Brent-WTI spread still carries freight and Middle East risk premium, but WTI falling harder says the near-term pressure sits in US inventories and shale supply rather than in Hormuz. For Chinese prices, crude is the most direct line in PPI: October PPI rose 5.7% on the year, with oil and gas extraction up 35% and refining up 29.9%. A move from $93 back to $83 turns the month-on-month figure first; whether the annual contribution falls depends on how November prices compare with last November — the fastest route to narrowing the five-point gap between CPI and PPI, but one set by the base rather than by demand.

11/18 21:04 China Securities Journal ↗

Tencent grows revenue 8% to Rmb207.3bn as Rmb81.4bn of quarterly capex overtakes net profit

Tencent reported third-quarter 2026 results on November 18: revenue of Rmb207.336bn, up 8% from a year earlier; profit attributable to equity holders of Rmb63.612bn, up 0.8%; and non-IFRS profit attributable to equity holders of Rmb66.519bn, down 6%.

Capital expenditure was the number of the quarter at Rmb81.433bn, directed mainly at IT infrastructure, data centres, land use rights, office campuses and intellectual property. Capex exceeded net profit by Rmb17.821bn and equalled 39.3% of revenue.

By segment: value-added services brought in Rmb98.326bn, up 3%, with domestic games at Rmb47.6bn up 11%, international games at Rmb18.9bn down 9% and social networks at Rmb31.8bn down 1%; marketing services earned Rmb43.049bn, up 19%; fintech and business services Rmb63.655bn, up 9%, with cloud revenue up 35% year on year.

Gross profit rose 7% to Rmb115.9bn, a 56% margin against 58% in the previous quarter. Tencent also said its AI office workspace WorkBuddy has more than 10m daily active users and consumes over 100trn tokens a day.

Why it mattersRmb81.4bn of capex against Rmb63.6bn of profit means the gap comes out of operating cash flow and the existing balance sheet: this is the year the income statement moved onto the balance sheet. Gross margin slipping from 58% to 56% is compute and depreciation entering the accounts — AI spending is booked as capex first, depreciated over years, and lands in gross margin last, so the two-point decline is a starting point rather than a one-off. Investors should separate two things: cloud revenue growing 35% is revenue already banked, while WorkBuddy's 10m users and 100trn tokens are costs already incurred against revenue not yet priced. The test is two numbers — whether capex at 39.3% of revenue comes down next year, and whether the 56% margin holds.

11/18 22:45 Securities Times ↗

Gold, silver and copper slide after Waller's hawkish interview, with spot gold down 2.18% and LME copper off 3.91%

Precious and base metals fell together late on November 18 Beijing time, Securities Times reported. Spot gold dropped as much as 2.7% intraday and broke below $3,750 an ounce, while spot silver fell more than 4.7% to $45.1. LME copper, zinc and nickel all slid.

At the time of writing, spot gold was down 2.18% at $3,763 an ounce and spot silver down 3.54% at $45.69. LME copper, zinc and nickel were down 3.91%, 2.38% and 2.81% respectively. Among the industrial metals named that evening, copper fell the most, and by more than gold at 2.18%.

The trigger was Fed Governor Waller, who said he was very worried that inflation was moving in the wrong direction and broadening, and that bringing it down would require stronger measures. Without a signal that a January hike is coming, he said, the Fed should raise rates by 50 basis points in December — "you may need to shock the market".

Rate markets repriced immediately: CME's FedWatch showed futures fully pricing a 25 basis point increase at the December meeting, with another expected in January and at least one more before the end of 2027.

Why it mattersGold and copper falling together says the selling was about discount rates, not about safety. Gold is most sensitive to real yields; copper is sensitive to both growth expectations and the cost of financing. Falling in the same session leaves one common denominator: real rates rising, the only channel a hike expectation can hit both through. Silver deserves separate attention, down 3.54% and more than gold, because its industrial demand gives it two headwinds at once — a higher discount rate and weaker expectations for industrial use. Copper above $12,700 a tonne is still historically expensive, so 3.91% only removes part of this year's speculative premium. The real test is not the price but the order book: copper volumes for data centres are contracted, so price swings do not change the tonnage — they change who holds the inventory and who pays to finance it. For manufacturers downstream, a month of lower copper saves roughly 4% times copper's share of input costs, and that only becomes visible in fourth-quarter reports.
