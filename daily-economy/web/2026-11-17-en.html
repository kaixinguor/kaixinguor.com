11/16 10:15 National Bureau of Statistics ↗

October industrial output grows 4.8% while fixed investment falls 8.1%, and producer prices outrun consumer prices by five points

Industrial value added rose 4.8% from a year earlier in October and 0.21% on the month, with equipment manufacturing up 10.6% and high-tech manufacturing up 17.6%; the services production index gained 4.6% and the manufacturing purchasing managers' index stood at 49.3. Urban surveyed unemployment was 5.1%, down 0.1 percentage point from September.

Demand split in two directions. Retail sales of consumer goods came to Rmb4,646.3bn in October, up 0.4% year on year and 0.02% on the month, taking the ten-month total to Rmb41,646.4bn, up 1.0%. On the broader goods-and-services retail measure, sales rose 2.5% over the ten months, with services retail up 4.9% and goods retail up 0.9%. Trade was the strong leg: October goods imports and exports totalled Rmb4,868.3bn, up 31.2%, as exports rose 28.7% to Rmb2,790.5bn and imports rose 34.7% to Rmb2,077.8bn.

Investment kept contracting. Fixed-asset investment excluding rural households fell 8.1% to Rmb36,482.4bn in January-October, or 5.1% once real estate development is excluded. Infrastructure was down 5.1%, manufacturing down 2.9% and real estate development down 21.6%; private investment fell 11.4%, or 7.8% excluding property. In October alone investment fell 2.93% on the month. The offsetting lines are narrow but growing: intellectual property products up 9.2%, equipment purchases up 8.9% and information services up 20.5%.

Prices reach the same verdict. Consumer prices rose 0.7% from a year earlier and were flat on the month, with the core index up 0.8%; food, tobacco, alcohol and catering fell 1.6%, including vegetables down 16.9% and pork down 8.6%, while transport and communications rose 5.5%. Producer prices rose 5.7%, 0.7 point faster than in September and up 0.7% on the month, and producer purchase prices rose 7.9%. Over ten months producer prices are up 2.7%. Property volume keeps shifting to the second-hand market: new home sales came to 613.58m square metres, down 13.7%, worth Rmb5,855.0bn, down 14.3%, while notarised second-hand transactions reached 679.71m square metres, up 11.4%.

Why it mattersBreak the 5.7% into components and it stops looking like broad inflation. The statistics bureau attributes 3.63 points of it to five industries — oil and gas extraction at 35%, refining at 29.9%, chemicals at 16.4%, coal mining at 36.6% and gas production at 7.9% — and another 1.18 points to electrical machinery and to computers and electronics. That is 4.81 of the 5.7 points, or 84%, coming from imported energy and AI-linked equipment. For company accounts the arithmetic is unforgiving: input costs climb while consumer prices rise 0.7%, so margins are squeezed from both ends, and only 21 of 41 industrial sectors show prices higher than a year ago. Investment tells the second story: inside the -8.1% total, property is -21.6% and private investment -11.4%, while intellectual property products are +9.2% and equipment purchases +8.9%. The total is shrinking as the mix is being replaced, which leaves fiscal policy to answer who fills the gap. The third number is a definition. Retail sales are up 1.0% over ten months but goods-and-services retail is up 2.5%, and the 1.5-point gap is entirely services at 4.9%. Track retail sales alone and services disappear; track only the 2.5% and you understate how weak goods at 0.9% really are.
Another angle For monetary policy the two indices imply real rates of opposite signs. At 5.7% producer inflation, industrial revenue is rising faster than the stock of debt, so the real burden of that debt is falling; at 0.7% consumer inflation, households' real purchasing power barely improves. With the two ends pointing different ways, the price instrument is boxed in and only quantity is left, which is why November's operations have run through repos and outright purchases rather than a rate cut. The test is DR001 and one-year interbank certificate of deposit rates late in the month, not the next fixing of the loan prime rate.

11/16 State Administration of Foreign Exchange ↗

October cross-border inflows reach $76.9bn as firms settle 64.3% of receipts and buy foreign currency with 59.3%

The State Administration of Foreign Exchange published October data on bank foreign-exchange settlements and sales and on banks' receipts and payments for clients on November 16. Deputy administrator and spokesman Li Bin said cross-border receipts and payments by non-bank sectors — companies and individuals — came to $1.45trn in October, up 10% on September's daily average, while foreign-exchange market turnover reached $3.77trn, up 7% on the same basis. Over the first ten months, cross-border receipts and payments grew 20% and market turnover 9% from a year earlier.

Net cross-border inflows were $76.9bn in October, higher than in September. Li put the pattern down to the National Day holiday: companies paid earlier before the break and collected afterwards, so post-holiday inflows typically exceed the late-September level. Taken together, September and October averaged $58.8bn a month, 6% below August.

What stands out is that both sides of the market rose at once. Averaged over September and October, companies converted 64.3% of foreign-currency receipts into renminbi and bought foreign currency with 59.3% of their renminbi, up 2.8 and 1.5 percentage points respectively from August. SAFE's reading is that demand is diverse and expectations are broadly stable.

Why it mattersWhen the settlement ratio and the purchase ratio rise together, it is not a one-way bet on the renminbi — it is more trading in both directions. The 64.3% is concrete: of every $100 an exporter receives, $64 is converted into renminbi, and that conversion is where domestic dollar supply comes from. The $76.9bn net inflow is the result, not the cause. Compare with August to see what kind of number this is: a $58.8bn monthly average across September and October is 6% below August, which makes the inflow seasonal — post-holiday collection — rather than an acceleration. The months that matter are November and December, when exporters settle in earnest to pay wages, taxes and suppliers. If the settlement ratio pushes higher from 64.3%, domestic dollar supply loosens further and the cost of leaning against renminbi appreciation rises with it. The purchase ratio at 59.3% already says import payments and outbound investment demand are coming back. With both sides expanding, direction depends on which one moves faster, not on the net figure.

11/17 08:42 Shanghai Securities News ↗

Bonds sag on the tax deadline as the finance ministry tenders Rmb650m of market-making support for November 17

The Ministry of Finance issued a notice on November 16 opening a government bond market-making support operation. It covers two book-entry interest-bearing issues: Rmb320m of the 2026 issue 13, with a two-year maturity, and Rmb330m of the 2026 issue 17, with a three-year maturity. Competitive bidding runs from 11:05 to 11:35 on November 17, and the bonds sold will merge with the existing issues for trading from November 20.

The backdrop is a soft market. Government bond futures fell across the curve on November 16, with the longest maturities leading the decline. The day was the deadline for tax payments, and although the central bank injected a net Rmb96bn through open market operations, short-term funding rates rose broadly. Zhang Yi, chief economist at Financial Street Securities, told Shanghai Securities News that the marginal tightening in funding was a direct trigger for the selloff.

The mechanics are straightforward. Market makers must quote both ways continuously, but some issues are concentrated in a few hands, leaving too little free float, so quotes become discontinuous and bid-ask spreads widen. Support operations let dealers adjust inventory in specific issues, which improves quotes and makes a single large trade less disruptive to price. Zhang argues that continuous, adequately traded points across maturities let the curve reflect expectations for funding costs and term risk, giving corporate bonds a more reliable reference.

Why it mattersRmb650m does nothing to the price of a bond market measured in the hundreds of trillions. What it buys is quote continuity, not duration. The government curve is the pricing ruler for the whole credit market: if the two- and three-year points trade continuously, term spreads on corporate and local-government financing vehicle bonds are observed; if those points are illiquid, the spreads are merely estimated. The cost sits with the dealers, who carry a standing two-way obligation in exchange for underwriting and primary-market access, and the ministry's Rmb650m subsidises only a sliver of it. Judge the operation not by the tender result but by whether bid-ask spreads on those two issues narrow after they merge on November 20, and whether trade counts at those maturities recover. The more informative signal is elsewhere: the central bank injected a net Rmb96bn on the tax deadline and short rates still rose, which says the price of money is set by the tax calendar and the pace of credit expansion. Injections cushion that price; they do not set it.

11/16 China Association of Automobile Manufacturers ↗

China's car association opens a payment-term audit of 17 major automakers, sampling at least 15 suppliers each

The China Association of Automobile Manufacturers issued a notice on November 16 launching a review of how 17 major carmakers pay their suppliers. According to Securities Daily, the assessment runs once a year: carmakers report the previous year's payment record by January 31 and the first half's by July 31, and at least 15 suppliers are drawn at random from each company's supplier base, with document checks and site visits used to confirm that what companies report matches what suppliers actually received.

The tests are specific. For small and medium-sized suppliers, the questions are whether payment terms stay within 60 days and whether payment is entirely in cash. For larger suppliers, whether terms stay within 60 days and whether cash plus bank acceptance bills make up at least 80% of payment. The review implements a September notice from the industry ministry and the market regulator on standardising supplier payments across the auto industry. In June 2025, 17 major carmakers publicly pledged to pay within 60 days.

Supervision is moving toward verifiability. Securities Daily reported that the industry ministry will publish results every year and will jointly interview and require rectification from carmakers with large payables, deliberately stretched terms or a heavy volume of complaints. The same report noted that some companies start the clock differently, set vague or slow acceptance conditions and effectively extend terms, leaving some suppliers unconvinced.

Why it mattersPayment terms are an interest-free payable, which is to say a loan the carmaker takes from its supplier. Stretching 60 days to 120 days makes a parts maker fund two months of materials, labour and overhead at its own borrowing cost, booked in its finance expense rather than the automaker's — one of the quieter ways profit is redistributed along the auto chain. What the review changes is not the terms but their verifiability: self-reporting, supplier sampling and annual publication together turn a pledge into a record, and only then does the 60 days on a quotation correspond to a cash flow. The part that will actually move is the payment instrument. Cash plus bank acceptance bills must be at least 80%, and the discount cost on a commercial acceptance bill falls on whoever receives it, so a six-month bill is a hidden price cut. Two numbers will settle it: whether payables days fall in the 17 carmakers' 2026 annual reports, and how much of the sample is made up of small and medium-sized firms.

11/16 17:04 Xinhua ↗

Japan's third quarter shrinks 0.1% annualised as household spending falls for a second quarter

Japan's Cabinet Office said on November 16 in its preliminary estimate that real GDP was roughly flat on the quarter in July to September and shrank 0.1% at an annualised rate, the first contraction since the third quarter of 2025, Xinhua reported.

The drag came from the largest domestic component. Private consumption, which is more than half the economy, fell 0.3% on the quarter for a second consecutive decline. Capital spending rose 1.6%, but residential investment and public investment each fell 1.1%. Net domestic demand contributed 0.6 percentage point to growth.

External demand turned negative. Exports of goods and services fell 0.2% while imports rose 2.5%, leaving net exports at minus 0.6 percentage point. Economists had expected the Bank of Japan to raise rates in December; economist Takashi Kadokura said the data show the recovery remains fragile and that household living standards are barely improving, which may make the central bank more cautious about a December move.

Why it mattersThe -0.1% matters less than its timing: it arrives before a hike, not after one. Japan's policy sequence has been to wait for a virtuous wage-price loop before touching rates, and the third quarter offers the opposite evidence — consumption down for two quarters, domestic demand's 0.6 point almost entirely carried by capex at 1.6%, with housing and public works both falling. Net exports at minus 0.6 says trade is a drag: imports up 2.5% while exports fall 0.2% usually describes expensive energy and food imports alongside softer external sales. That leaves the central bank boxed in. A hike would support the yen and cut import costs but squeeze consumption further; standing pat lets a weaker yen push import costs up another round. For global markets the variable being priced is not Japan's GDP but the odds on the December meeting, and the size of the yen carry trade decides how fast a change in those odds travels.

Victory Giant seeks Rmb70.7bn of bank credit lines and launches a Rmb150m-300m buyback capped at Rmb240.49

Victory Giant Technology (300476.SZ) said in a November 16 evening filing that its board approved a credit-line package worth Rmb70.7bn with Industrial and Commercial Bank of China, China Construction Bank, Bank of China, Agricultural Bank of China, Shanghai Pudong Development Bank, Ping An Bank, China Guangfa Bank and China Bohai Bank. The lines cover working capital loans, trade finance, bills, supply chain finance, project loans, letters of credit and cross-border financing. The company said the facility size is not the amount it will draw, that drawdowns will follow order flow and project schedules, and that shareholders must still approve the plan.

Two other board decisions came with it. The company will inject capital into its third-tier wholly owned subsidiary in Malaysia, mainly for plant construction and equipment at its Malaysian base, to take overseas computing-power orders and relieve capacity constraints at home. It will also buy back shares on the Shenzhen exchange through centralised bidding, spending between Rmb150m and Rmb300m at no more than Rmb240.49 a share within 12 months of board approval, for equity incentives and employee ownership plans.

The stock closed at Rmb109.02 on November 16, giving the company a market value of Rmb107.1bn.

Why it mattersRmb70.7bn of credit lines against Rmb107.1bn of market value is 66% of the company's worth, and the point is not that it will borrow that much. The point is that the borrowing capacity now sits on the table as a channel it can draw on at any time to pre-fund materials and expansion for overseas capacity and computing-power orders. The cost is not in the facility but in the drawdown: these lines are mostly short-term working capital and bills priced off the market, so if capacity arrives ahead of orders, interest and depreciation hit the income statement together while revenue has not yet arrived. The buyback ceiling of Rmb240.49 is roughly 2.2 times the Rmb109.02 close, and that gap says something — a ceiling is an authorisation, not a commitment, and what actually gets spent sits somewhere in the Rmb150m-300m range depending on where the board is willing to buy. Two numbers will settle the account: the timetable for the Malaysian plant, and whether operating cash flow covers capital spending. Credit lines sit on the liability side; cash flow is the asset side.

11/16 20:05 Securities Times ↗

Jinke Property closes at Rmb0.97, below par, and warns it may be delisted

Jinke Property (000656) said in a November 16 evening filing that its shares closed at Rmb0.97 that day, below the Rmb1 par value, and that under the relevant rules the stock faces the risk of delisting because its price is under par.

The company has only recently come out of restructuring. Securities Times reported on October 30 that a year after its reorganisation plan was completed, Jinke had delivered 7,561 homes across 32 projects, or 1.12m square metres, taking its completion record to 99.2%. The company treats delivery as the first test of its credit repair.

The industry is still contracting. Statistics bureau data published on November 16 showed real estate development investment down 21.6% in January to October, new home sales of 613.58m square metres, down 13.7%, and Rmb5,855.0bn of sales value, down 14.3%, while notarised second-hand transactions rose 11.4% to 679.71m square metres. Volume is moving to the second-hand market, and the inventory and price pressure on new homes stays on developers' books.

Why it mattersA par-value delisting is not about how much a company loses; it is about the share price. Rmb1 is a hard line, and restructuring pushes a company toward it: debt-for-equity swaps turn liabilities into shares, the share count grows, and earnings and price per share are diluted together, so surviving and staying listed become two different tests. The first asks whether cash flow covers interest and construction; the second asks only whether the price clears Rmb1. The cost falls differently by holder. For creditors who took stock, a delisting moves the exit from auction on the exchange to negotiated transfers on the over-the-counter board, and the liquidity discount appears immediately. For homebuyers and local governments, delisting does not stop projects, but it shuts the window on equity refinancing and some bank lending, leaving the second half of delivery work more dependent on sales receipts. Watch not the Rmb0.97 but whether the company rolls out price-management measures such as share purchases or a share consolidation, and whether the 2026 annual report can get net assets back above zero. Delisting on price is one problem; sustained losses and negative equity are another, and either can arrive first.
