US core PCE rises to 3.3% in October, a near three-year high, as markets price a December hike

The personal consumption expenditures price index rose 0.3% in October from September and 3.8% from a year earlier, matching expectations, the Bureau of Economic Analysis said on November 25; September readings were 0.5% and 3.7%. Core PCE, which strips out food and energy, rose 0.3% on the month and 3.3% on the year, up from 3.1% and the highest since November 2023, against a consensus of 3.2%, Cailianshe reported.

Nominal and real figures tell different stories. Personal income rose 0.7%, or $201.8bn, and disposable income 0.8%, or $200.5bn. Nominal spending rose 0.3%, or $63.8bn, but real PCE fell $5.8bn, a decline of less than 0.1%. Personal saving was $1.00tn and the saving rate 4.1%. Goods spending rose $11.8bn and services $52.1bn.

BEA also revised third-quarter real GDP growth up to a 4.3% annual rate from an initial 3.4%, with the second quarter at 2.2%; investment, consumer spending and government outlays contributed, while exports fell and imports rose, Cailianshe reported. Faster growth and faster inflation landed in the same release cycle, and both point the same way: the economy is not giving the Fed a reason to stop.

Market pricing followed. Traders now almost fully discount a 25bp increase at the December 8-9 meeting and expect another in the first quarter of 2027; inflation has run above the 2% target for about five and a half years, Cailianshe reported. Money moved into bonds: the 10-year Treasury yield fell about 7bp to near 4.86%, an eighth consecutive session of declines and the longest run since 2024.

Why it mattersStart with the definitions. The 3.8% is year-on-year, the 0.3% is month-on-month, and core 3.3% is a year-on-year rate excluding food and energy — not "core rose 3.3 percentage points". The most informative pair is nominal spending at +0.3% against real spending at 0.0%: of the extra $63.8bn spent in October, prices absorbed nearly all of it, and the volume bought actually fell by $5.8bn. The resilience in consumption is nominal, not real. On growth, a 4.3% annualised rate removes the Fed's reason to pause, which is why December went from possible to near-certain. Who pays: households and firms with floating-rate debt, long-duration assets that get repriced, and emerging markets that borrow in dollars. Who earns: dollar depositors and creditors. Two things to watch: how long a 4.1% saving rate can carry consumption, and whether a second hike in early 2027 becomes the new anchor once the forward curve takes it in.
Another angleFor AI capex, the policy rate is the discount rate. Xinhua reported the same day that the five biggest US cloud providers spent $556.9bn on capital expenditure in the first nine months of 2026, more than the $542.9bn of operating cash flow they generated. A 25bp hike raises the hurdle rate on those projects, not the price of a chip: what sets the pace of build-out next year may be this yield curve rather than any capacity plan.

11/25 19:57 Yicai ↗

European Parliament approves the EU 2027 budget: €199.7bn in commitments, €211.5bn in payments

The European Parliament approved the EU's 2027 annual budget on November 25 by 406 votes to 184, with 69 abstentions, according to CCTV News. The Council had cleared the budget agreement on November 23, and the Parliament president signed it after the vote.

The two headline figures measure different things. Commitment appropriations of €199.7bn are money the EU commits this year and may pay out this year or later; payment appropriations of €211.5bn are what is actually disbursed during the year, including payment for commitments made earlier. Payments exceed commitments by €11.8bn.

Against the Commission's draft, the final budget adds €300m for priority areas: €22.5m for transport and energy networks, €21.5m for research, €14m for education exchange programmes and €13.5m for the EU civil protection mechanism.

It is the last annual budget under the EU's 2021-2027 multiannual framework. Once the framework expires, the overall spending envelope has to be renegotiated, and annual budgets only schedule what has already been agreed at that level.

Why it mattersPayments above commitments is an accounting signal that old promises are coming due: in the framework's final year, projects committed earlier are paid out, and little room is left for new commitments before the next round. The €11.8bn gap is not extra spending; it is earlier promises being cashed. Who pays: member states, through GNI- and VAT-based contributions plus own resources such as carbon levies. Who receives: transport and energy network projects, research programmes and Erasmus+ places. The €300m top-up is 0.15% of €199.7bn and sits in four presentable line items, which tells you where the marginal negotiation happens — not on the total, which the multiannual framework had already fixed, but on line items. Two things to watch: whether the next framework's size and own resources can pick up the slack, and whether payments running above commitments means the new framework opens in arrears.

Xinhua: five US cloud providers spent $556.9bn on capex in nine months, more than their $542.9bn of cash flow

Xinhua published an analysis on November 25 citing Reuters data: Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Oracle spent a combined $556.9bn on capital expenditure in the first nine months of 2026, more than the $542.9bn of operating cash flow they generated over the same period. S&P Global Ratings expects six large cloud providers to run negative free operating cash flow in both 2026 and 2027.

The scale keeps rising. Goldman Sachs forecast in November that annualised US spending on AI hardware and intellectual property will exceed $900bn by the end of 2026 and could reach $1.4tn by the end of 2027, close to 4% of US GDP; it estimates AI investment will add 0.6 percentage points to US growth in 2027, or about 0.2 points excluding semiconductor investment. The ECB's November Financial Stability Review estimates that large US technology firms could spend $5.5tn cumulatively by 2030.

The gap is funded with debt. Fed governor Michael Barr said on-balance-sheet debt at seven large AI companies is expected to top $800bn by the end of 2026; some of these firms invest in model developers and cloud startups, which then spend the money on their chips and compute — circular financing. The ECB review noted that free cash flow at most large US cloud providers already turned negative in 2026.

The costs spill outward. Research cited by the US Department of Energy suggests data centres could account for 11.8% of total US electricity consumption by 2030. A recent MIT study found that a data centre's arrival raised local average retail electricity prices by 2.7%, and Ali Pesco, director of Harvard Law School's Electricity Law Institute, notes that grid investment driven by a handful of large technology firms may be spread across all customers through tariff mechanisms.

Why it mattersThe $14bn gap between $556.9bn and $542.9bn is small, but the direction has changed: capex used to be covered by operating cash flow and now needs bonds, leases and multi-year purchase commitments. Where it sits: debt and right-of-use assets on the balance sheet, data-centre leases and procurement obligations off it. The first consumes interest, the second consumes duration — which is why credit spreads move before order books when rates shift. Who bears the cost: the 2.7% electricity increase is spread across every customer in the service area, not just the data centre, a transfer that requires no legislation. What it changes: AI capex has moved from reinvested profit to credit expansion, making the policy rate, rather than chip supply, the main variable of this cycle. Two things to watch: when free cash flow turns positive, and whether the average lease term is shorter than the depreciation schedule of the chips inside — if the lease expires first, the impairment lands in the year of renewal.

11/26 08:02 China Securities Journal ↗

China's listed public REITs reach 100 with Rmb264.27bn raised as the first private-developer retail REIT debuts

The Guangfa Xincheng Wuyue Commercial REIT listed on the Shanghai Stock Exchange on November 25, the first public REIT sponsored by a private developer, China Securities Journal reported on November 26. That took the number of listed public REITs to 100, with Rmb264.27bn raised across established products, including nine commercial property REITs worth more than Rmb30bn.

The underlying assets are two shopping malls — Changzhou Tianning Wuyue Plaza and Nantong Qidong Wuyue Plaza — totalling about 290,000 square metres of gross floor area. Both have operated for more than six years and kept average occupancy above 97% in 2023-2025. Projected net cash-flow distribution yields are about 5.61% for April-December 2026 annualised and 5.73% for 2027.

The secondary market gave it a small premium. It opened at Rmb3.120 per unit and closed at Rmb3.138 on November 25, up 1.03% on the offer price, with Rmb40.34m traded. The CSI REITs total return index fell 0.4% to 931.36 that day, having hit a year-to-date low of 911.18 on November 18 before rebounding over four sessions to 935.14 on November 24.

Over a longer horizon, losers outnumber winners. Of the 78 REITs listed before January 1, 2026, only 19 were up and 59 were down year-to-date as of November 25; over the past five sessions, however, 86 rose.

Why it mattersThe 5.61% is a coupon measure — projected net cash flow divided by the offer price, with no asset appreciation and no accounting profit — and its spread over government bonds is what the market charges for operating risk in commercial property. Who pays: Xincheng Holdings puts two malls into the REIT, gets cash back and hands part of the future rent to unit holders. Who receives: unit holders, through mandatory distributions of at least 90% of distributable amounts each year. Of the Rmb264.27bn total, commercial property REITs account for just nine funds and a little over Rmb30bn, about 11%: for developers to move heavy assets off their balance sheets at industry scale, the pool is still far too small. Two things to watch: 97% occupancy is history and 5.61% is a forecast, and the gap between them is the buffer against falling rents or rising vacancy; and whether falling rates keep compressing REIT discount rates, because if the long end turns, high-yield assets like these are repriced first.

11/25 21:38 Xinhua ↗

Five ministries tie aid-fund performance grades to next year's allocation, A through D

Xinhua reported on November 25 that the Ministry of Finance, the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs and three other departments have issued measures for performance evaluation of regularised assistance funds. Under the ministry's notice (Cainong [2026] No. 84, dated November 16), the former rural revitalisation subsidy becomes a regularised assistance fund from 2026, with the evaluation rebuilt on the experience of the transition period.

The evaluation covers five areas: target management (preventing a return to poverty, income growth); task management (funding, industrial support, employment support, support for less-developed areas); fund and project management (use of funds, project pipeline, performance targets, project quality, disclosure); effectiveness (project progress, budget execution, outcomes); and incentives, with add-ons for mechanism innovation, budget execution and funding effort, and deductions for in-year budget cuts, data quality and negative findings.

The scoring is explicit. Indicators are scored item by item, and task-specific scores are weighted by each task's share of funding. Results fall into four grades: A (90 and above), B (80 to below 90), C (60 to below 80) and D (below 60). Where inapplicable indicators leave a maximum below 100, scores are converted to a 100-point scale, and results are ranked separately for the eastern, central and western regions.

The results are then used: provincial scores feed into the factor-based allocation of the following year's assistance funds with corresponding incentives, and are circulated by the finance and agriculture ministries to provincial departments and provincial governments. Problems are referred back for rectification and reported up through the agriculture ministry. The measures repeal Cainong [2021] No. 122 and Cainong [2024] No. 50.

Why it mattersThis turns a special programme into a standing one. After the rename, the money's legitimacy comes from the annual budget rather than transition-period policy, and the exercise is relabelled from an assessment to a performance evaluation with results wired straight into the allocation formula — which writes provincial governments' marginal incentive into the grant function. Doing more is not a commendation; it is more money next year. Who pays: budgets at central, provincial, municipal and county level. Who receives: assisted regions, and the businesses that generate local industry and jobs. Ranking the three regions separately stops rich provinces from scoring high by default, but it also means the same grade is worth different amounts in different places. Whether this is a hard or soft constraint depends on two things: the weight performance carries in the factor formula, and whether grades C and D carry any cost beyond feedback and rectification — the measures specify feedback and rectification but no clawback ratio, leaving that to provincial rules.

S&P puts Nvidia's AA rating on CreditWatch Negative, flagging a possible one-notch cut within 90 days

S&P Global Ratings said on November 25 local time that it had placed Nvidia's AA issuer credit rating and its senior unsecured debt rating on CreditWatch Negative, citing questions about the effectiveness of the company's management and governance framework raised by recent widely reported events, Yonhap Infomax reported.

The conditions and the size of any cut are spelled out. If further due diligence finds the management and governance weaknesses persist, S&P could lower the rating by one notch within 90 days, and it expects the cut to be limited to a single notch unless investigations or disclosures lead it to conclude that Nvidia's reputation or business prospects have been damaged. The trigger was a personnel episode: Jensen Huang's brief departure and return, which S&P said did not adequately answer its questions about board oversight of the executive code of conduct, putting board independence and succession planning under scrutiny, Cailianshe reported.

The bond market moved first. Credit risk gauges for CoreWeave and Nvidia hit record highs on November 25: the cost of five-year default protection on CoreWeave debt rose as much as 2.09 percentage points, the largest one-day increase on record, to 15.34 percentage points a year, and swap contracts imply a better-than-70% chance of default within five years, according to ICE Data Services data cited by Cailianshe. Credit default swaps on Nvidia, Google, Oracle and SpaceX also set records the same day.

In equities, Nvidia fell 3.91% for a second straight session, Microsoft lost 1.25% and Amazon 3.14%; the Philadelphia semiconductor index slipped 0.65%, with 11 of its 30 members higher and 19 lower. The Nasdaq 100 at one point traded 20% below its record close, near bear-market territory, before paring losses in thin pre-holiday trade, Cailianshe reported.

Why it mattersA one-notch downgrade does not change the share price so much as the price of borrowing. AA is the second-highest investment grade; a cut would force a repricing of outstanding bonds, the coupon on new issues, and any collateral or guarantee terms tied to a rating. For a company generating this much cash the pain is not immediate, but it turns higher funding costs from an assumption into a covenant. Who pays: Nvidia as issuer, and the institutions holding its bonds. Who earns: buyers of CDS protection — 15.34 percentage points a year is the market's price for the proposition that an AI hardware company can default. What stands out is the reason: not orders, not margins, but governance. Succession planning and board independence are soft variables, yet ratings ask a hard question — will the money be repaid under stress? — and governance determines the quality of decisions made under stress. Two things to watch: the conclusion of the 90-day review, and which moves first, CDS or the rating. This time CDS moved first and the rating followed.

SpaceX cuts Blackwell cluster rents by 20% to 35% after Anthropic declines to renew 50,000 H200s

SpaceX has cut rents on small Nvidia Blackwell clusters, and AI model developer Anthropic has decided not to renew a batch of H200s, according to a SemiAnalysis note to institutional clients this week reported by Cailianshe on November 26.

The price move is specific. Rents for small Blackwell clusters fell from about $15 to $15.50 per GPU-hour to $10 to $12, a cut of 20% to 35%, bringing retail pricing roughly in line with the bulk rates paid by large customers such as Anthropic and Google. Small customers used to pay more than large ones precisely because cards were scarce, and the premium was a payment for that scarcity; with retail now at wholesale, the premium is gone — and only weeks ago, the same model showed rents still rising.

The non-renewal points the same way. SpaceX is seeking new tenants for a cluster of about 50,000 H200s that Anthropic will not renew, with delivery set for early 2027 and an asking price of about $5 per GPU-hour. Ornn, which tracks transaction prices, put H200s at $6.53 this Monday, so SpaceX is asking roughly 23% below market for compute that will not be delivered until 2027.

Transaction-based indices have already turned. Ornn's data show B200 rents down 8.8% from a November 8 high to $9.07 per hour, H200 down 16% from a November 11 high to $6.53, and B300 down 24% from a mid-October high to $10.87. Token prices fell further: B200 rent indices are still up 48% since late August, while Anthropic's token price dropped from $1.46 to $0.75 per million tokens, roughly halving.

Why it mattersRent is the spot price of compute and the only real-time signal in this business. Retail converging on wholesale says the premium for being able to get a card has evaporated; a 50,000-card fleet coming off lease and being marketed at a discount for 2027 delivery says suppliers expect next year's rents to be lower. Who pays: the second- and third-tier neoclouds that live on spot premiums and rent out older cards, often borrowing against those GPUs to expand — when rents fall, the same card collateralises a smaller loan. Who receives: buyers, who get the same compute at a lower unit price. What it changes: the Jevons argument — that cheaper units create enough extra volume to lift total spending — has been the core of the bull case for AI capex, but spending-management platform Ramp reports that over the past two months token prices kept falling while volume growth no longer compensated. Two things to watch: whether new cards hold the roughly $12 per GPU-hour hurdle that project returns rest on, and whether the depreciation schedules for older H100s and H200s have to be shortened — if so, the write-down lands on the lessor's books first.

11/25 20:38 China Securities Journal ↗

Poly Developments plans up to Rmb20bn of 10-year unsecured bonds, first to repay interest-bearing debt

Poly Developments and Holdings (600048) approved a corporate bond issue at a board meeting on November 25, with all nine directors in favour: up to Rmb20bn including the full amount, at Rmb100 par value, with maturities of up to 10 years and the option of a single tranche or a mix of maturities.

The terms are explicit: unsecured; a public offering to qualified professional investors, with no priority allotment to existing shareholders; underwritten on a firm-commitment basis by the lead underwriter or a syndicate it organises; the coupon set by the company and the bookrunner within a pre-set range after bookbuilding, on simple annual interest with no compounding; and, after CSRC registration, issued in one or several tranches and listed on the Shanghai Stock Exchange subject to eligibility.

The use of proceeds is ranked: repaying interest-bearing debt, replenishing working capital, project construction, acquiring property projects and other permitted uses, with amounts left to the board or its authorised persons under a shareholder mandate. The proposal still needs approval at an extraordinary general meeting on December 11.

The announcement also lists four creditor protections if the company expects that it cannot pay principal or interest on time: no profit distribution to shareholders; suspension of major capital expenditure including acquisitions; cuts to or suspension of directors' and senior managers' salaries and bonuses; and no transfer of the key responsible persons.

Why it mattersThree parameters set the cost: Rmb20bn, 10 years, unsecured. A 10-year tenor locks today's rate into a decade of interest expense, and the absence of collateral means pricing rests entirely on the issuer's credit. Where it sits: the first use is repaying interest-bearing debt, so this is not new investment but a liability swap — maturing short debt replaced with longer debt, extending duration and easing near-term cash flow while raising total interest cost over the life of the bonds. Acquiring property projects is the only expansionary line item, and its size will be fixed only after the shareholder mandate. Two things to watch: where the final coupon lands within the bookbuilding range, which decides whether the swap saves or costs money, and whether the issue is tranched, which sets the 2027 maturity profile. Of the four protections, no dividends and no capex is the hard one: it protects creditors, and shareholders bear the cost, which is exactly the division of labour between debt and equity on the same balance sheet.
