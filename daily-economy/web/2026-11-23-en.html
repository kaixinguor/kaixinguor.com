Money & Policy

货币与政策
11/22 18:52 Shanghai Stock Exchange ↗

Margin ratio cut back to 80% from November 30, with ChiNext market makers floated and Rmb2.758bn of new fee cuts

On November 22 the Shanghai, Shenzhen and Beijing stock exchanges, with approval from the China Securities Regulatory Commission, cut the minimum margin ratio for margin-financed securities purchases to 80% from 100%, effective November 30. In the exchanges' own terms the leverage ceiling goes from 1x to 1.25x: Rmb1m of collateral that used to support Rmb1m of borrowing now supports Rmb1.25m, 25% more buying power.

It is the fourth change to the ratio. It went from 50% to 100% in November 2015, back to 80% in August 2023, and up to 100% again on January 14 this year, according to Cailian Press. The exchanges said margin activity has cooled sharply and overall leverage is low, so a counter-cyclical cut would free up financing capacity; brokers may also recalculate available collateral on contracts opened before the change.

The size estimates depend on the denominator. The market carries about Rmb2.2tn of margin debt; available collateral is estimated at Rmb0.5tn to Rmb1tn, which at an 80% ratio supports roughly Rmb100bn of new borrowing. Applying the new ratio to the existing Rmb2.2tn of contracts releases about Rmb300bn more, for a theoretical total of almost Rmb400bn, Securities Daily reported, citing market participants. It is capacity, not capital already deployed.

Two other measures landed the same day. The Shenzhen exchange published draft rules for ChiNext market making, setting out registration, obligations and supervision, and opened them for public comment. The two exchanges also announced a new round of fee cuts worth Rmb2.758bn: Shanghai will waive the 2026 trading-unit fee, raise the data-centre cabinet discount from 30% to 60% and the office-support discount from 50% to 80%, and extend the exemption of listing fees and bond handling fees by two years to the end of 2028, while China Securities Depository and Clearing extended its waiver of some bond registration and settlement fees to the same date.

Why it mattersA margin ratio is the price of leverage, not a switch for credit. Cutting the floor from 100% to 80% raises the notional a given pile of collateral can carry by 25%, but it changes nothing about collateral quality, investor cash flow or a broker's willingness to lend. What decides how much of that Rmb400bn actually arrives is the margin rate — most brokers charge more than 4% — and the volatility of the collateral, not the ratio. Who pays: the levered investor pays interest, the broker pays capital and risk-control costs, the market pays in volatility. Durability rests on two things: whether brokers cut margin rates too, and whether the clause that applies harsher ratios to abnormal trading pulls the extra capacity back in. The exchange is loosening the lever and fitting a valve at the same time, which is the standard grammar of counter-cyclical policy: it changes the slope, not the direction.
Another angleMarket makers and fee cuts are institutional costs on the supply side. A market maker earns the spread and owes liquidity; the exchanges give up Rmb2.758bn of revenue. Neither creates funds directly, but both reduce friction, and narrower spreads mean more strategy capacity for quantitative and high-frequency money. For retail investors the real cost was never the commission but the impact cost and the bid-ask spread, and that is the part this package actually touches.

11/22 17:32 CNR ↗

Bank net interest margin holds at 1.41% as the NPL ratio rises 2bp to 1.54% and bad loans grow Rmb74.5bn

The National Financial Regulatory Administration has published its main supervisory indicators for banking and insurance in the third quarter. CNR reported on November 22 that banking institutions held Rmb503.1tn of assets in renminbi and foreign currency at the end of September, up 6.1% and above Rmb500tn for the first time; large banks accounted for Rmb225.9tn, up 8.5% and 44.9% of the total, and joint-stock banks for Rmb80.4tn, up 5.5% and 16%.

Asset quality moved in two directions. Performing loans stood at Rmb242.8tn. Non-performing loans rose Rmb74.5bn from the previous quarter to Rmb3.8tn, lifting the NPL ratio by 2bp to 1.54%. Buffers remain comfortable: loan-loss provisions of Rmb7.6tn, a coverage ratio of 199.49% and a provision-to-loan ratio of 3.07%, with a capital adequacy ratio of 15.40%, Tier 1 of 12.24% and core Tier 1 of 10.79%.

On earnings, commercial banks made Rmb1.9tn of net profit in the first nine months, a return on equity of 7.65% and a return on assets of 0.58%. The net interest margin was 1.41%, flat on the second quarter, after a 1bp quarter-on-quarter rise in the second quarter that was the first since 2022. By institution: large banks 1.31%, joint-stock banks 1.55%, city banks 1.40%, rural banks 1.59%.

Inclusive lending kept expanding, with small and micro business loans at Rmb38.8tn, up 6.4%, and inclusive agricultural loans at Rmb15tn, up 6.2%.

Why it mattersA stable margin alongside a rising NPL ratio says the banks are covering a falling asset yield with a falling funding cost. At 1.41%, every Rmb100 of earning assets produces Rmb1.41 a year; what holds profit together is scale — assets up 6.1% — and provisions, because a 199.49% coverage ratio can release profit when needed. A 2bp rise in the NPL ratio is small in absolute terms, but the direction is unusual for the past two years. Who pays: depositors, through lower deposit rates, fund half the margin; borrowers, through lower loan rates, get the other half; shareholders get a 7.65% ROE. Durability depends on how much deposit repricing is left. This round of stability comes from the delayed pass-through of successive deposit-rate cuts since 2024, and once that is used up, whether 1.41% holds depends on loan demand returning, not on pricing technique.

11/22 17:36 Wallstreetcn ↗

South Korea readies its stock stabilisation fund, eases buyback filing limits for three months and sets a short-selling watch

On November 22 Deputy Prime Minister and Finance Minister Lee Hyoung-il chaired an emergency financial-market meeting, known as the F4, and announced that the government would begin preparing its stock stabilisation fund for activation and deploy it whenever market conditions require. From November 24 it will also ease the daily filing cap on corporate buybacks for three months, while the Financial Supervisory Service and the Korea Exchange run a three-month special watch on illegal short selling, Wallstreetcn reported, citing Korean officials and Reuters.

The fund is Korea's version of a market-support vehicle. In October 2008 the Korea Exchange and other institutions raised KRW515bn during the global financial crisis and began buying monthly from November 2008 through March 2009. It entered preparation in 2020 and again in 2022 but was never actually deployed. This is the first genuine preparation since 2008.

The trigger was the tape. KOSPI fell more than 5% on November 20, the same day the Nasdaq lost 5.28%, and foreign outflows have run for months, with the cumulative total a record in LSEG data. The buyback change is specific: firms had been limited to filing no more than the larger of 10% of the planned buyback and 25% of one-month average daily volume, and no more than 1% of shares outstanding; the three-month easing widens that room.

Officials said the long-term growth case for AI remains widely accepted and that there is no need for panic selling, noting that Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix are posting record profits while their share prices diverge from fundamentals.

Why it mattersA stabilisation fund is a quasi-fiscal tool: it does not change corporate earnings, only the bargaining position of buyers and sellers. Three questions decide whether it backstops or absorbs — where the money comes from, what it buys, and how it exits. The 2008 fund was KRW515bn, a signal relative to market capitalisation at the time; the fact that it was prepared but not used in 2020 and 2022 says its main function is to move expectations rather than to supply bids. Easing the buyback cap hands the administrative bid to companies themselves, because corporate buybacks have been an important absorber of foreign selling, and large programmes are now nearing their end — which is precisely why the government moved. For an export economy with a heavy AI weight, this is a duration correction: foreigners are selling duration, not earnings, and a stabilisation fund can support the price but not the duration.

11/22 14:35 Xinhua ↗

Wearable device sales at above-scale retailers rose more than 70% in Jan–Oct, with the market reaching Rmb105.3bn in 2025

Xinhua reported on November 22 that National Bureau of Statistics data show retail sales of wearable smart devices at above-designated-size outlets up more than 70% year on year in the first ten months. A KPMG report on the 2026 China wearables market put the sector at Rmb69.5bn in 2021 and Rmb105.3bn in 2025, an average annual growth rate of 11%.

The product map has spread from the wrist to the body: AI glasses, smart rings, bone-conduction earphones, exoskeleton robots, with use cases running from sleep and fitness tracking to live translation and rehabilitation. The supply base is dense — more than 115,000 businesses in Shenzhen's Huaqiangbei sell over a million kinds of components, with a response time its promoters describe as "designed today, mass-produced tomorrow, shipped overseas within a week".

Policy is pulling in the same direction. An implementation opinion by eight ministries on "AI plus consumption" calls for cultivating the wearables market and building high-visibility scenes such as live translation and mobile payments.

The constraints are just as clear: performance, battery life and weight remain a trilemma, requiring coordination across chips, power, optics and sensors, while compliance costs for collecting, storing and sharing data rise with scale.

Why it mattersThe 70% figure covers above-scale retail only, excluding smaller merchants and unbranded sellers, so it is closer to a measure of brand concentration than of total demand; and Rmb105.3bn is the whole market in 2025, so the two numbers cannot be divided into each other. What is worth recording is the substitution: a smartwatch replaces a mechanical watch and a fitness band, AI glasses replace the combination of a translation app and earphones, and the increment comes from services — health management, live translation — rather than the hardware. Who pays: consumers pay for devices and subscriptions, companies pay for data compliance, and insurers or health systems may eventually pay for health data, which is the real second revenue curve. Durability depends on replacement cycles: wearables turn over faster than phones but carry lower unit value, so growth is driven by units rather than price, and once penetration passes the knee, volume growth fades before prices do.

11/23 07:22 Shanghai Securities News ↗

New fund launches top Rmb1tn for the year, with Rmb485.9bn of equity funds and Rmb187.3bn of ETF inflows last week

Shanghai Securities News reported on November 23 that 1,613 new funds had been launched in the year to November 22, raising Rmb1,000.111bn. Equity funds accounted for 1,069 products and Rmb485.9bn, with 121 equity funds raising more than Rmb1bn each — roughly 48.6% of the year's total.

Issuance is still accelerating: 146 funds are in the market and another 54 have published launch notices. On November 20 alone, 24 ETFs filed, including the first ten STAR market semiconductor ETFs, the first eight CSI Shanghai-Hong Kong-Shenzhen computing-power ETFs and the first six CSI shipping ETFs.

Existing money moved faster. Choice estimates put equity ETF net inflows at Rmb92.066bn on November 20, the largest single day of this inflow cycle and behind only October 8, 2024 and April 8, 2025. The week of November 16–20 drew Rmb187.318bn in total, with CSI 300 ETF Harvest taking Rmb22.651bn, SSE 50 ETF ChinaAMC Rmb21.583bn and CSI 300 ETF Huatai-PineBridge Rmb14.583bn.

Passive products are the main vehicle, and the two flows mean different things for the index: a new fund has a lag between launch and deployment, while an ETF subscription is immediate exposure.

Why it mattersA new fund is a contract for future money; an ETF inflow is money now, and only reading them together gives the increment. Less than half of the Rmb1tn is equity, with the rest in bonds and money market funds, so the recovery in household risk appetite is partial. The Rmb187.3bn that arrived in one week went into broad benchmarks such as CSI 300 and SSE 50, buying beta rather than alpha. Who pays: fund investors pay management and custody fees plus trading friction, and distributors take trailer commissions; who earns: fund companies and the exchange. Durability rests on two things — whether the deployment period turns into concentrated buying, which would support the index, and whether "counter-cyclical" positioning can survive redemptions when net asset values fall. Issuance heat is itself a contrarian indicator: the easier the fundraising, the higher the eventual cost of putting it to work.

About 94% of LME and COMEX copper stocks sit in the US as traders ship metal back to China and the Yangshan premium hits $500 a tonne

Wallstreetcn reported on November 22, citing the Financial Times and Bloomberg, that about six copper vessels had left Long Beach and Los Angeles for China; Bloomberg said on November 19 that bookings were already in place and that some metal originally bound for the US had been rerouted, involving tens of thousands of tonnes. The two-year rush into America is going into reverse.

The geography of inventories is the sediment of tariff expectations. Data from commodities house Marex put about 94% of London Metal Exchange and CME warehouse copper stocks inside the US, against 20% two years ago, and Bloomberg cited traders' estimates of roughly 2m tonnes of US inventory. As tariff expectations cooled, the CME premium over LME copper disappeared and turned into an LME premium.

The shortage in China pushed the premium to a record. The Yangshan import premium doubled in a week to about $500 a tonne, the highest on record, while LME copper fell from more than $14,670 a tonne in late October to below $12,600 this week. Traders have also started pulling metal from New Orleans and Baltimore, where the stockpiles are larger but must be bought at US prices to capture the wider spread.

Time and risk are asymmetric: the west coast takes about two weeks to reach China, while New Orleans plus warehouse-out takes six weeks to two months. Kpler data show November volumes into the US at less than half October's record, and Washington has still not formally settled whether refined copper will be taxed, leaving metal in transit exposed to policy risk.

Why it mattersCopper flows are set by three spreads: the Chinese physical premium, the US tax-inclusive price, and freight plus time. That 94% share is a reservoir built by two years of tariff expectations; once the expectation flips, inventory turns from an asset into tied-up capital and traders would rather take a discount than keep it. Who pays: Chinese buyers pay the $500 premium, US traders pay the mark-down and storage, and miners get a repriced premium. Durability depends on whether Chinese stocks can be rebuilt before the Lunar New Year — traders float tens of thousands of tonnes a month arriving, which is a forecast, not a fact — and on who carries the metal in transit if the US tariff does land. What is being priced is not copper but the probability that policy arrives.

11/23 Xinhua Finance ↗

A-shares add 334 buyback plans worth up to Rmb73.5bn since October 8, with cash dividends above Rmb2.3tn this year

China Securities Journal reported on November 23, in a piece carried by Xinhua Finance, that since October 8 Shanghai and Shenzhen listed companies had disclosed 334 new buyback plans with a combined ceiling of Rmb73.5bn as of November 22, along with nearly 1,200 updates on existing programmes. They also announced 109 new purchase plans by controlling shareholders, actual controllers and senior executives, worth up to Rmb17.2bn, plus almost 300 progress disclosures.

The headline numbers are large. Changxin Technology raised its buyback range to Rmb2.5bn–5bn from Rmb1bn–2bn; the chairman of Zhongji Innolight proposed Rmb2bn–4bn; Eoptolink, Sanhuan Group and Wus Printed Circuit each announced plans with Rmb1bn ceilings, Nexchip Rmb800m and AMEC Rmb500m.

Rules are moving too. According to China Securities Journal, the CSRC will revise the rules on share buybacks to give market-support buybacks more binding force, and until then will support and encourage index constituents and leading technology companies to buy back stock. On funding, the PBOC cut the relending rate for buybacks and share purchases by 25bp to 1% on November 2; Hongda Electronics has already obtained a Rmb36m facility from China Merchants Bank's Zhuzhou branch with a term of up to 36 months.

Dividends sit on the same timeline: more than 3,700 A-share companies have paid dividends this year, for a total above Rmb2.3tn, according to Wind data.

Why it mattersBuybacks and dividends are both cash outflows, but they differ in accounting and tax. A cancelled buyback shrinks the share count and mechanically lifts earnings per share, giving shareholders a capital gain; a dividend is cash now and is taxed. The Rmb73.5bn is a ceiling, not an executed amount — what changes per-share value is the cancellation ratio and the price paid — while the Rmb2.3tn of dividends is cash already out of the door. Who pays: retained earnings, which is to say future capex or research. When rates fall and project returns fall faster, returning cash beats investing it, at the cost of funding the next capacity cycle externally. The 2 percentage points between the 1% relending rate and the 3.0% one-year LPR is the bank's spread, and it is also the price the policy is paying to support the market.
