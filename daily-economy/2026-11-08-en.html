Two regulators target online financial black markets, naming debt-avoidance, insurance-refund and credit-repair accounts

On November 7, the National Financial Regulatory Administration said the Cyberspace Administration of China, working with it, had stepped up oversight of online financial information and shut down a batch of accounts promoting "malicious debt evasion", "improper anti-collection", "claims agency" and "credit repair" services. It published several typical cases.

Three playbooks were named. Accounts such as "Principal Zheng", "Principal's Breakout Circle" and "Just an Ordinary Person" sold anti-collection courses urging borrowers to avoid lawful debts. "Teacher Wu Refunds", "Teacher Li Dawei" and "Xiaolei Legal Consulting" advertised "full refunds" and "new refund rules", coaxing policyholders to invent insurer violations, file malicious complaints and pay high fees. "Self-Strengthening Brother Lao Xiang", "Apei Tells the Truth" and the "Minxing Micro-Academy" WeChat account fabricated successful credit-repair cases to sell paid services. The Douyin, Xiaohongshu, Xianyu, Kuaishou, Weibo and WeChat accounts involved were closed.

Why it mattersThe economics are simple: package "pay less", "get the premium back" or "clean the record" as a service, and the intermediary keeps the fee while the lender, the insurance pool and the credit system absorb the cost. Closing accounts cuts off customer acquisition, not demand. The real constraints sit at both ends — how lenders verify complaints, and whether the free credit-dispute channel is usable enough. For teams in financial content and advertising, this draws a clear line: charging for "agency rights defence" has moved from grey marketing to a named black market.

Africa-China trade forum signs 49 projects worth RMB 40.96bn

The 2026 Africa-China Economic, Trade and Cultural Forum was held in Yiwu, Jinhua, Zhejiang on November 7, drawing more than 400 guests from 39 countries and regions. During the event, 49 China-Africa cooperation projects were signed, worth RMB 40.96bn in total — including a Kinshasa market project in the DRC, a 105MW solar plant in Botswana, a cross-border logistics network and mineral imports — spanning trade, overseas investment, engineering contracting and third-party cooperation.

As a flagship side event under the Forum on China-Africa Cooperation, the gathering has helped sign more than 100 projects worth over RMB 120bn since 2024. On that basis, this year's RMB 40.96bn is roughly a third of the three-year total. Tariffs are the other backdrop: since May 1, China has applied zero tariffs to 53 African countries with diplomatic ties, and Jinhua's trade with Africa grew 35.3% year on year in the first three quarters, with 2,675 African-invested firms registered locally.

Why it mattersThe mix matters more than the headline: trade, overseas investment and contracting side by side shows the model shifting from building projects to producing, selling and servicing locally. Such projects are usually priced in dollars or local currency, with long payback periods, so FX and political risk land directly on contractors' and investors' balance sheets. For local firms, Yiwu's market-procurement channel and Jinhua's Africa trade links are ready entry points; the barrier is local operations and compliance, not the signing ceremony.

Shanghai state-owned buyers place $2.862bn of import-expo orders, up 3.1%

At the ninth China International Import Expo's Shanghai state-owned delegation signing ceremony on November 7, the delegation had placed 137 intended purchase orders worth $2.862bn, up 3.1% year on year, across commodities, medical devices and agri-food.

Since the first expo, the delegation has signed $25.2bn of intended purchases with companies in more than 20 countries and regions, ranking first among Shanghai's sub-delegations for nine consecutive years. Against last year's $2.776bn across 113 orders, this edition added 24 orders and about $86m.

Why it mattersThe 3.1% is growth in intended purchases, not final transactions or total imports; its signal is in the mix, with medical devices and agri-food taking a larger share as procurement shifts from raw commodities toward livelihoods and healthcare. For overseas suppliers, the expo remains a concentrated customer-acquisition moment; for buyers, the test comes after signing — fulfilment, FX hedging and inventory turns. Orders grew faster than value, meaning the average ticket is getting smaller and purchasing more fragmented.

Berkshire's operating profit falls 5.8% to $12.71bn as cash hits a record $377.4bn

On November 7, Berkshire Hathaway reported third-quarter results for the period ended September 30, 2026: operating profit of $12.71bn, down 5.8% year on year; net profit attributable to shareholders of $22.112bn, versus $30.796bn a year earlier; and net earnings per Class A equivalent share of $15,502. It repurchased $9.6bn of stock in the quarter, and cash reserves rose to a record $377.4bn.

Investment income was the drag: net investment income came to $9.407bn, against $17.311bn a year earlier — nearly halved. The gap between operating and net profit comes mainly from fair-value moves on the investment book.

Why it mattersA record cash pile plus continued buybacks is management saying it cannot find enough cheap assets. For the market, that is a valuation signal: even the buyer best known for acting in a panic is hoarding cash. For Berkshire itself, $377.4bn earning little is a drag on returns. The variables are when the next big deal appears and whether underwriting profit can offset the investment-income hole. Buybacks support per-share metrics; they do not reverse an operating-profit decline.

Economic Daily deflates solid-state battery hype: fastest route at TRL5, scale-up targeted for 2030

Economic Daily published an article on November 7 noting that several automakers had announced solid-state battery production lines, while Ouyang Minggao, an academician at the Chinese Academy of Sciences and professor at Tsinghua, said flatly that everything claimed as industrialised is a hybrid cell — no true all-solid-state battery has been industrialised. The distinction is the electrolyte: all-solid contains no liquid; semi-solid remains a mix, typically with 5–10% liquid electrolyte.

Progress is measured by technology readiness level. Of the three solid-state routes, the fastest — sulphide — sits at TRL5, "preliminary validation in a relevant environment". Mass production still requires TRL6 prototype demonstration, TRL7 real-environment validation, TRL8 system qualification and TRL9 actual deployment. A plan jointly issued by the industry ministry and six other departments targets initial scale application of all-solid-state cells by 2030.

The article also cites a valuation case: Finnish start-up Donut Lab unveiled a "production-ready" all-solid battery at this year's CES, claiming 400 Wh/kg, about five minutes of charging and limited degradation after 100,000 cycles; its valuation briefly exceeded $1bn. Months later it was alleged to be an ordinary lithium-ion battery, and Finnish financial regulators and criminal investigators stepped in.

Why it mattersOne word separates semi-solid from all-solid, and the valuation gap can be an order of magnitude. To judge a production claim, ask three things: is there still liquid in the electrolyte, are yield and cost disclosed, and is the cell a sample or a deliverable batch? The distance from TRL5 to TRL9 is an engineering problem, not a scheduling one — interface impedance, materials cost and scale-up processes can each turn a quarter into three years. For buyers, 2030 is a planning target, not a supply contract; for investors, treating "rolled off the line" as "scaled revenue" is the expensive mistake.
