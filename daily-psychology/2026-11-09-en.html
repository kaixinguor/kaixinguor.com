A 92-country survey of 53,799 people: mental wealth is lowest among the young and in richer countries

A study in Social Indicators Research (vol. 185, no. 11) measures mental health across 92 countries and 53,799 adults on three dimensions — emotional, social and financial — rather than counting depression or anxiety alone. The lead team is Kai Ruggeri's at the University of Glasgow, with co-authors from dozens of countries.

The picture is not flattering. Many groups are languishing: not ill, but not doing well either. The lows concentrate among younger adults and in higher-income countries. Education and income are generally improving, yet employed adults score only moderately — material progress is not converting into mental wealth.

The authors use “mental wealth” to ask whether a society, beyond GDP, employment and schooling, gives people emotional stability, relationships they can rely on and a sense of control. They identify economic and social indicators that policy could target, but the findings remain descriptive: they show where the lows are, not why they are there or how to lift them.

Why it mattersThe value of a survey like this is that it pulls mental health out of disease statistics and back into public policy. Its limits are equally clear: cross-sectional data cannot separate cause from effect, and the poor scores of young adults in rich countries may reflect relative deprivation, housing and job insecurity and social-media use at the same time. The test is whether governments put mental wealth into budgets and targets — or produce another survey with no sequel.

11/09 10:05 UC San Diego ↗

The full Instagram archives of 415 students: two kinds of loneliness leave two kinds of traces

Researchers at UC San Diego analysed the complete Instagram histories of 415 undergraduates — not just public posts but photos, captions, likes, comments and timing. More than 60% had private accounts. They separated emotional loneliness (missing a close, dependable relationship) from social loneliness (missing a group to belong to).

The two leave different traces. Students higher in emotional loneliness posted more between 11pm and 5am, used more negative language, chose profile photos with sadder or angrier expressions and mentioned family and friends more often — perhaps a longing for closeness. Those higher in social loneliness had fewer faces in their photos, more often tilted their heads upward in profile pictures, used more past-tense language, liked and commented less, posted less at weekends and used fewer words about care or communication.

The models predicted self-reported loneliness moderately well, but the size of people's real support networks poorly. The authors stress that these are group-level patterns, not a diagnostic test for an individual, and that the data cannot tell whether loneliness changes how people use Instagram or the reverse.

Why it mattersThe useful part is not that AI can “detect” loneliness. It is the distinction between two situations that need different remedies: people missing intimacy need deeper connection; people missing a group need low-friction ways in. If platforms act at all, the priority should be consent, opt-out and appeal — turning late-night activity, negative language and social withdrawal into a gentle prompt to talk to someone, not a label attached to a user. Otherwise care becomes surveillance.

Wuhan's “work-smell” clinic saw 30,000 visits in three months, turning exhaustion into a referral

Wuhan Seventh Hospital opened its “qu ban wei” — literally “remove the work smell” — clinic on 18 August, putting eight departments, including traditional Chinese medicine, orthopaedics, cardiology and its sleep centre, on one pathway. Patients pick a department by symptom online, are screened on site and referred to a doctor; complex cases go straight to a multi-disciplinary meeting. From August to October the clinic logged more than 30,000 visits, up 136.5% year on year, with the hospital saying most of the increase came from the new service.

The name is not a diagnosis. Experts at the Hubei Psychological Counselors Association map it onto burnout: emotional exhaustion, cynicism and a reduced sense of achievement, with the body signalling first — insomnia, headaches, digestive trouble, muscle tension. The clinic's own numbers are physical: orthopaedics accounted for nearly 51% of visits, including 30% neck and shoulder pain, 35% back and leg pain and 40–50% hand, elbow and wrist pain.

Two cases show how it works. A 53-year-old company owner with severe anxiety and insomnia was not simply given more sleeping pills; he received cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, physical treatment and a small amount of medication, and his sleep had improved noticeably 20 days later. A 21-year-old doctoral student who had not slept for 15 days and was assessed with severe depression and anxiety was first given anaesthetic-induced sleep to break the cycle, then CBT and stellate ganglion block.

Why it mattersThe 30,000 figure counts visits, not patients, and it is worth asking how much of the increase is people who would not otherwise have sought care. More important, burnout is rooted in hours, targets and management; a clinic can pull someone back from the edge, but the cardiology chief himself says relapse is likely if they return to the same job at the same pace. The service design — symptom-based triage plus multi-disciplinary review — is copyable. The luck of turning a hashtag into a waiting-room queue is not.

11/08 Pingdingshan Daily ↗

A county hospital opens its first psychiatry and psychology clinic, filling a gap rather than a waiting list

On 4 November, Yexian People's Hospital in Henan opened a psychiatry and psychology clinic, filling a gap in specialist mental health care at county level. It covers all ages, with sub-clinics for sleep, mood and anxiety, adolescent mental health and older adults, and also handles liaison psychiatry for physical-mental comorbidity and standardised management of severe mental illness.

Before the opening, the county lacked specialised, standardised provision; people with emotional difficulties had nowhere obvious to ask, or were passed between departments. The new team combines psychiatrists, psychotherapists, counsellors and integrated Chinese-Western medicine physicians, with private consulting, assessment and family-therapy rooms, one-to-one appointments and long-term follow-up.

Treatment is individualised: sleep problems get monitoring, physical therapy, psychological intervention and medication; adolescents get sandplay and family therapy; older adults are covered for memory decline, suspiciousness and late-life anxiety and depression.

Why it mattersOpening a clinic is the first step in supply, not the end. The binding constraint in county hospitals is rarely a sign over the door; it is people — psychiatrists, psychotherapists, clinicians able to do longer-term work. Payment is the second: psychotherapy is still often paid per session out of pocket, several hundred yuan a time, which is not trivial for a county household. Without referral networks, crisis protocols and insurance links, severe cases will still travel to the city and milder ones will still calculate whether it is worth it.

11/06 Medical Daily ↗

A 400-woman randomized trial: the Rosie chatbot did not ease anxiety or depression

A University of Maryland team published a 12-month randomized controlled trial in JAMA Network Open: 400 pregnant women and new mothers from 49 states and Washington, DC, all from racial or ethnic minority groups, 47% Black, with a mean age of 32.6. Half used Rosie, a generative AI chatbot answering questions about pregnancy, parenting and infant development; the other half joined a book club that mailed one children's book a month.

At 12 months, anxiety (GAD-7) and depression (PHQ-9) scores were essentially the same: the adjusted mean difference was 0.10 for anxiety (95% CI, −0.77 to 0.96) and −0.23 for depression (95% CI, −1.05 to 0.59). Emergency visits, postpartum check-ups, effective contraception and sleep duration also showed no differences. Rosie was an information tool, not a therapy: it drew on sources such as the CDC and children's hospitals and was funded with a $3.7m NIH grant in 2021.

Two secondary findings need caution. Among women pregnant at enrolment, the chatbot was associated with more self-reported pregnancy-related complications in the 12-month secondary end-point analysis, but not in the longitudinal analyses — this is not evidence that it caused complications. And women who used Rosie more often reported longer sleep and higher anxiety, but that was an exploratory within-group comparison; women who were already more anxious may simply have used it more.

Why it mattersA null result is worth more than a 90% satisfaction score. It tells hospitals, insurers and employers that convenient, cheap and always-on is not the same as effective, and that procurement of AI mental health tools should demand randomized evidence, a clear comparator and pre-specified outcomes. For product teams, the active ingredient may not be talking like a person; it may be routing someone to actual treatment, a hotline or a clinician when risk appears.

11/08 17:01 NetEase Hubei ↗

Stanford's AI robot-navigated neuromodulation lands in Wuhan: precision is easy to market, outcomes are harder

On 7 November, Wuhan hosted the launch of a neuromodulation system described as Stanford AI robot-navigated, and a clinical demonstration base — the first in central China — opened at Wuhan Youhao Hospital. The hospital says the system combines AI imaging, robotic-arm navigation and closed-loop algorithms to tailor stimulation to individual brain anatomy, reducing ineffective stimulation and improving comfort and response. It is aimed at depression, anxiety, sleep disorders and tics.

The launch brought in specialists from Tongji, Union, Hubei People's Hospital and the Wuhan Mental Health Centre. Discussion focused on safety, the boundaries of indications, long-term follow-up and multi-disciplinary care. The hospital says the base will host regular consultations, teaching and case reviews and will police clinical quality.

The distinction matters. Transcranial magnetic stimulation already has solid evidence in depression and some other conditions; AI plus robotic navigation is an increment in precision, not a leap from nothing to something. Better navigation should put stimulation closer to the intended target and reduce misses, but “more accurate” is not automatically “more effective”. That requires randomized or real-world studies, not expert endorsement at a launch.

Why it mattersFor patients, the questions are not whether the name Stanford appears but which indications, what course, what total cost, who delivers it and who is accountable when something goes wrong. For hospitals, buying a device is not building a capability: targeting, quality control, follow-up and outcome registries all have to be in place. Neuromodulation does not lack new concepts; it lacks clinical evidence reproducible enough to put “effective” into numbers.

11/09 10:09 Qilu Evening News ↗

Weifang hosts the first mental health and psychiatry development conference: 350 people on “treatment and prevention together”

The first Academic Conference on High-Quality Development of Mental Health and Psychiatry opened in Weifang, Shandong, on 7 November, with more than 350 specialists and practitioners from mental health, clinical psychology and public health. Its theme was “strengthening the base, improving quality, integrating treatment and prevention”, covering the social psychological service system, child and adolescent mental health, sleep and psychosomatic health, integrated Chinese and Western medicine and grassroots capacity.

Putting “treatment and prevention together” in the title reflects a policy shift over the past year. In March 2026, 25 departments including the National Health Commission issued an implementation plan for a social psychological service and crisis-intervention system, aiming by 2030 for provision covering the whole population and life course. In July, the State Council's 15th Five-Year Plan for National Health added the same goal to its prevention agenda.

A conference does not produce services. What decides whether integration works is more concrete: whether the grassroots have people — psychiatrists, psychotherapists, social workers; whether payment follows — who pays for therapy, screening and follow-up; and whether schools, employers, communities and hospitals have stable referral routes.

Why it mattersThe value of a gathering like this is that it pushes mental health from a specialty topic into a cross-department one. The risk is that it stays at the level of declarations: a conference every year, no baseline data every year. The thing to watch is not another consensus document but a replicable local pilot — annual visits at a county clinic, the share of severe cases in standardised management, successful adolescent referrals and the actual reimbursement rate once psychotherapy enters insurance.
