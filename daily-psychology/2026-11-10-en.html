11/09 UC Irvine News ↗

One hour less sleep moved simulated phishing clicks from 15.56% to 17.76%

A team at the University of California, Irvine, used the 2026 switch to daylight saving time as an unplanned sleep-deprivation experiment. The subjects were more than 11,000 employees of a large US institution whose IT department periodically sends out fake phishing emails and counts how many recipients click. Nobody was assigned to anything; the clock did the assigning. The paper appears in PNAS.

The result comes down to two numbers. On the Monday after the clocks moved, 17.76% of employees clicked a simulated attack, against 15.56% on the Monday before, with the same people and the same emails. The release describes that as a 13% increase; dividing the two rates gives roughly 14%, and the absolute gap is 2.2 percentage points.

This is a before-and-after comparison one week apart, not a randomised trial, so a bad Monday cannot be ruled out entirely. Two background figures make the finding easier to place: nearly a third of US adults do not get enough sleep, according to the CDC, and losses from cybercrime in the US alone exceeded $20 billion last year, per the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center. Two points of click rate lands on a real balance sheet.

The advice for employers is unusually concrete — add a security reminder before the clock change, and again after long holidays or big sporting events, or delay irreversible actions such as transfers and authorisations by a day. The researchers also stepped into the US debate over permanent daylight time: the House has passed the Sunshine Protection Act, but the sleep science they cite favours permanent standard time as closer to the body's internal clock.

Why it mattersThis is the cleanest causal evidence in the issue: the treatment was not assigned by researchers, but its timing was exogenous, and the population, task and outcome were all fixed. A 2.2-point shift is invisible to the individual — you will not feel more gullible today — yet across 11,000 people it is a budget line. That is the classic shape of a small effect: not worth a company-wide campaign, definitely worth two dates in the calendar.
The other angleIf employers act on this, the cost is roughly one email, and the benefit is booked by security while the fatigue is carried by staff — the kind of free-to-the-decider intervention that gets written into policy and then quietly skipped.

11/09 20:00 Child Psychiatry & Human Development ↗

14 countries, 865 toddlers: shouting tracks behaviour problems most closely — strongest in China

According to PsyPost's report on the paper in Child Psychiatry & Human Development (DOI 10.1007/s10578-026-02079-3), the data come from the Joint Effort Toddler Temperament Consortium: 865 children aged 17 to 40 months (about 27 months on average) and their mothers in Belgium, Brazil, Chile, China, Finland, Italy, Mexico, the Netherlands, Romania, Russia, South Korea, Spain, Turkey and the United States. Country samples ran from 49 families in Chile to 112 in the Netherlands, and children with clinical diagnoses were excluded.

Mothers reported how often they used five practices — telling the child to think about what they did, shouting or swearing, spanking or hitting, taking away privileges, and time-out — and completed the Child Behavior Checklist, which separates problems directed outward (aggression, hyperactivity) from problems directed inward (anxiety, withdrawal).

In standard-deviation terms, a one-SD increase in shouting went with a 0.34-SD increase in total problems; hitting with 0.25; removing privileges with 0.14. All three were linked to internalising problems, but only shouting and removing privileges to externalising ones. Telling a child to reflect, and time-out, showed no association with problems — and no association with fewer problems either.

The country contrast is the part worth reading closely. The links between shouting, hitting and internalising problems were strongest in the Chinese sample and weakest in the US and Finnish samples; the authors speculate that shame plays a larger role in Chinese socialisation, and flag that this needs testing. Use also varied: shouting was most common in Russia, China and Brazil and least common in Chile and the US; hitting was most frequent in Brazil and rarest in Finland; removing privileges was most frequent in Spain and rarest in Russia.

Why it mattersThis will be read as "shouting harms children", but the evidence runs only to correlation. A cross-sectional design cannot exclude the reverse path — a harder-to-manage child elicits more shouting — and one mother reported both her discipline and her child's problems, which inflates associations through shared method variance. Each practice was measured with a single item of unestablished reliability, and with 49 to 112 families per country, 14 countries cannot support much cross-cultural inference. The genuinely useful finding is the counter-intuitive one: removing privileges, the recommended alternative in many parenting programmes, travelled with more problems too — perhaps because parents reach for it once problems exist, perhaps because a delayed consequence teaches a two-year-old nothing. What should replace it is a question the study does not answer.

11/09 Pediatric Cardiology ↗

14 children after the Fontan operation, eight weeks of choir: feasible, and uncontrolled

A team led by Kendra Ward at Ann & Robert H. Lurie Children's Hospital of Chicago reports in Pediatric Cardiology (DOI 10.1007/s00246-026-04483-x) a choir intervention for 14 patients aged 8 to 16 born with a single ventricle, all of them past the Fontan operation. For eight weeks they received 30 minutes of individual voice coaching and 60 minutes of group instruction weekly, plus access to an online singing and breathing programme developed with Northwestern University's Bienen School of Music.

The clinical rationale is not music appreciation. Children with a single ventricle go through three operations, the last around age three or four, which route oxygen-poor blood straight into the pulmonary artery so the lungs are perfused passively by breathing and pressure rather than by a pump. The surgery is life-saving, but lungs are weaker, exercise tolerance is lower, and many children carry neurocognitive differences such as ADHD. Breathing training is already part of cardiac rehabilitation; the design problem is getting a child to keep doing it. Wrapping it inside a choir is the actual intervention here.

In the investigators' own wording, some breathing measures improved, self-reported depression and wellbeing scores rose, and parents reported better school function and less depression and anxiety. Note the composition: every improved outcome is either a child's self-report or a parent's observation. No objective functional endpoint — peak oxygen uptake on an exercise test, say — is reported as a primary result.

This is a single-arm pre-post feasibility study: 14 children, no control group, no randomisation, no blinding. Over eight weeks children return to school, seasons change, and being watched can move self-reported scores on its own; extreme values drift back toward the mean. The team says it hopes to launch a larger multicentre study in 2027, and has already started a non-research choir this autumn because the children and families asked to continue.

Why it mattersThe value here is not "singing treats depression". It is a template for psychosocial support in paediatric chronic illness that costs almost nothing: hide breathing training inside something children will show up for, so parents keep bringing them, and the hospital buys no equipment. To become a clinical recommendation it still needs three things — a comparator (a waiting list or usual follow-up would do), an objective endpoint such as lung function or exercise capacity, and six- to twelve-month follow-up. Until then it belongs in the "you can try it, you can run it, you cannot charge for an efficacy claim" bucket. For parents, the practical test is whether the child wants to go back a second time.

11/09 Frontiers in Psychology ↗

Kyoto University: some theft recidivists cannot learn from feedback, so deterrence may miss them

A Kyoto University team led by Yukiori Goto published the study on 9 November in Frontiers in Psychology (DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1997570). They recruited three groups — theft recidivists with a kleptomania diagnosis, theft recidivists without one, and people with no criminal record — and gave everyone a four-armed bandit task: four options whose reward probabilities shift over time, so the player must balance exploring unknown options against exploiting the one currently paying best.

While participants played, the researchers measured prefrontal cortical responses with functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) and fitted a reinforcement-learning model to estimate each person's learning rate — how efficiently they updated their choices after feedback. The result runs against intuition: the recidivists without kleptomania were the group unable to adjust behaviour to reward feedback, consistent with impaired reward-based reinforcement learning. The kleptomania group behaved differently, and the authors stress the two kinds of recidivism should be treated separately.

The practical weight comes from a background figure: theft recidivism runs at roughly 70% within five years, while judicial systems assume that the threat of punishment is enough to change behaviour. If part of the problem is an inability to learn which choice pays better, adding years to a sentence is adding weight to a lever that is not connected.

The caveats are equally concrete. The release does not report group sizes, only that there were three groups; fNIRS resolves cortical surface oxygenation, not deep circuits; and the design is cross-sectional, so the direction of causation is open. Taking this into a courtroom runs straight into two old problems: poor individual-level prediction, and how to define culpability when behaviour is partly a learning deficit.

Why it mattersThe easy misreading is "theft is a brain disease, so no one is responsible". What the data support is far narrower: attributing all recidivism to character and will leaves punishment spinning uselessly for some people. Clinically, it argues for making reward learning and executive function part of routine assessment in addiction and impulse-control services — it predicts behaviour better than asking someone whether they will steal again. For policy, the alternative on the table is treatment plus supervision rather than longer sentences: it costs money, needs staff, and shows nothing within a year, which is why it usually comes last.

11/09 Nature Communications ↗

One triage sentence predicts PTSD: AUROC 78.5% across 145 patients, all nine positives in a 30-patient review

A team at NYU Langone Health led by Katharina Schultebraucks published PREVIA online in Nature Communications on 9 November. The tool skips questionnaires. It reads the transcript of a single routine question — "what brought you to the emergency department?" — and forecasts whether the patient will meet criteria for post-traumatic stress disorder a month later. The work was funded by NIMH grant R01 MH129856.

The sample was 145 patients admitted to two New York City level-1 trauma centres with physical injury. Across the sample the model reached an AUROC of 78.5%, sensitivity of 95.4% and precision of 46.8%. In a head-to-head test on 30 randomly selected patients, nine of whom went on to develop PTSD, PREVIA flagged all nine (100%) while an emergency clinician specialising in PTSD identified eight (88.9%); precision was 50% for both. Outcomes were adjudicated against the CAPS-5 structured interview — the diagnostic gold standard, not a self-report scale.

It works in layers. The first scans the narrative for peritraumatic symptoms (loss of control, amnesia, depersonalisation) and early symptoms present in the ED; the second weights established risk and protective factors such as prior mental health problems, socioeconomic status, coping skills and social support; a third model synthesises these into a structured summary so no single model dominates, and a final layer combines that with age, sex, trauma type and base rates to produce a risk score with a plain-language explanation. The authors say the three-layer design beat simpler configurations on most metrics, and that it was built on open-source models with no training or fine-tuning, so other hospitals can test it without proprietary software or their data.

It targets a real bottleneck. About 43 million patients a year are admitted to US emergency departments with injuries, and more than a quarter develop PTSD, yet only about 28% of departments screen for it at all, and only two-thirds of those use validated instruments — because clinicians have roughly 15 to 20 minutes per patient. Letting one sentence at intake produce a risk score costs almost nothing in time, which is the whole commercial pitch.

Why it matters"100%" is the most dangerous number in the story: the denominator is nine cases, inside a 30-patient review drawn from a 145-patient sample at two centres, with no external validation reported. Missing one of those nine would drop sensitivity by 11 points, and real emergency departments are messier — more languages, more fragmented speech, worse audio. The harder question comes after the flag: does the department have follow-up, a trauma-focused treatment to refer into, and someone to pay for it? Last issue we covered the null trial of the Rosie chatbot, and the lesson repeats — detection and companionship are not the same as changed outcomes. For buyers, the three questions are external validation, the downstream cost of false positives, and the specific pathway that opens when the score is high.
