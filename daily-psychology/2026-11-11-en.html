1,651 Ethiopian adolescents: most direct paths from gender norms to distress did not hold

Anita Alaze and colleagues at Bielefeld University used round two of the Gender and Adolescence: Global Evidence (GAGE) data from Ethiopia (2020): 1,651 adolescents (901 girls, 750 boys, median age 13), psychological distress measured with the GHQ-12, and structural equation models testing 14 pathways linking sex, gender norms, psychological competencies, gender attitudes and gender roles to distress.

The results sit some distance from intuition. Most significant associations were among the gender constructs themselves (sex to attitudes; norms to competencies, attitudes and roles). Only two paths actually reached distress: psychological competencies to distress, and sex to distress. The mediated paths through gender attitudes and gender roles did not hold. The authors put it bluntly: the constructs were associated with each other rather than with distress. The limits are stated plainly: one country, one round, cross-sectional, so no causal ordering; the GHQ-12 is a screening instrument, not a diagnosis; and the gender-norms scale was newly validated here.

Why it mattersWhat makes this paper useful is what it failed to find. Inside the widely cited frame that gender norms damage adolescent mental health, pulling the mediation chain apart leaves most of the middle links broken. For anyone running adolescent mental health programmes, the implication is not to treat "change gender norms" as an intervention aimed at symptoms: it more plausibly moves proximal variables such as psychological competencies, and that segment needs its own design and its own measurement.

11/10 14:32 Frontiers in Neuroscience ↗

29 roommate pairs, four nights of actigraphy: sleep fragmentation is shared, but modestly

A University of Louisville team analysed baseline data from a 2017 mindfulness programme: 29 undergraduate roommate pairs (58 people, aged 18-19) wore wrist actigraphs for four nights and completed one-week versions of the PSQI and the Perceived Stress Scale. On average they slept 6.98 hours, spent 66 minutes awake after sleep onset (WASO), and logged 18.6 actigraphy-detected awakenings per night.

Roommate similarity was clearest for awakening frequency: ICC = 0.38 (95% CI 0.04-0.68), above the 0.09 average for pseudo-dyads of non-roommates. Centring on the data-collection wave cut it to 0.25 (-0.11 to 0.59), an interval spanning zero. Night-to-night deviations in WASO correlated at r = 0.29 (0.07-0.44) and survived adjustment for day of week and week of semester; same-night covariation in awakenings did not. The authors keep the conclusion low: roommates show "modest" similarity, no environmental exposure was measured at all (the motorway, freight rail and overnight air-cargo hub nearby are context, not variables), and with 18 pairs sharing a bedroom versus 10 not, the comparison was, in their words, uninformative.

Why it mattersThis is exactly the kind of finding that gets written up as "sleep is contagious", but 29 dyads, confidence intervals that cross zero and a correlation only 0.29 above pseudo-dyads all point somewhere duller: the similarity most likely comes from a shared schedule in the same building, not from physiological synchrony. Dorm policy implications are thin. What would actually help is pairing dyadic sleep monitoring with synchronised environmental monitoring of noise, temperature and light, the only way to separate a roommate effect from a room effect.

115 trials, 30,000 people: escitalopram, agomelatine and duloxetine beat fluoxetine over the long run

A team at Radboud University Medical Center published a systematic review and network meta-analysis in The Lancet Psychiatry: 115 randomised trials, more than 30,000 participants and 20 antidepressants, all compared with fluoxetine as the reference drug, assessing acute response and long-term sustained response at once. The authors call it the first large-scale study to put short- and long-term performance on the same network.

Three drugs came out ahead over the long run: escitalopram, agomelatine and duloxetine. The costs are stated in the same breath: agomelatine carries a risk of liver failure, and duloxetine is rarely a first-line choice. Eric Ruhe stresses this does not mean everyone with severe depression should switch to escitalopram; prescribers still weigh benefit against side effects person by person. Nearly 6% of people worldwide have depression, per the release. One caveat: the release gives only the ranking, not effect sizes such as standardised mean differences or risk ratios, and a network meta-analysis depends on how comparable the trials are in population, dose and follow-up length.

Why it mattersThe live clinical argument is not which drug works fastest but whether, and to what, one should switch after response. By folding maintenance into the initial choice, this analysis supplies a ranking for picking a drug you can stay on. A ranking is not a prescription: liver risk, discontinuation effects, price and availability can all reverse it. The practical corollary: a patient stable on fluoxetine has no reason to switch on the strength of this ordering alone.

11/10 18:05 EurekAlert (Uppsala) ↗

PMDD is not one condition: two-month diaries from 90 women reveal three symptom subtypes

Erika Comasco's group at Uppsala University reports in Nature Communications: 90 women with premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD) and 40 healthy controls kept a daily diary for two months, rating anxiety, depression, irritability and mood swings, and latent class growth analysis was used to fit each symptom's rise and fall across the cycle.

Three subtypes emerged. Severe PMDD with early onset begins roughly two weeks before menstruation and peaks about five days before. Severe PMDD with late onset starts rising about ten days before and peaks around the onset of bleeding. Moderate PMDD with late onset also starts about ten days before but peaks around four days before, and is milder. The authors stress that within one diagnosis the onset, peak and offset all differ, and that different symptoms in the same woman follow different curves. There is also a temporal order: a rise in anxiety is followed by more irritability, depressive symptoms and mood swings. In the network, anxiety is the central node, depression predicts subsequent severity of the other core symptoms most strongly, and irritability predicts depression at the longest lag.

Why it mattersThe clinical takeaway is about measurement more than about labels: whether symptoms start two weeks or ten days before bleeding changes when to schedule follow-up, when to time medication, and whether "premenstrual worsening" is even the right description. Two months of daily diaries is impractical in a clinic, but a one-page symptom calendar that patients fill in themselves is cheap. For research, trials that treat PMDD as one homogeneous sample may average away subtype-specific effects.

An app trial with 126 unemployed adults: 68.3% found work, versus 50.8%

Stewart Shankman's team at Northwestern ran the DRIVEN trial: 126 unemployed US adults (mean age 33.6, 49.2% women, never having earned more than $100,000 a year, with elevated depressive symptoms) randomised to a six-week app condition or a control. The app delivered weekly self-guided cognitive behavioural exercises in a behavioural-activation spirit (start with something finishable in 15 minutes) plus three one-hour video job-coaching sessions; controls received curated job-search links by email each week.

The primary outcome was job-search behaviour. Preparatory search rose by an estimated 0.053 units per day (95% CI 0.031-0.076) versus 0.011 (-0.011 to 0.033), P=.008; active search rose 0.061 (0.038-0.084) versus 0.025 (0.001-0.047), P=.03; depression fell further, -0.214 versus -0.122, P=.002. The headline number is employment: 68.3% versus 50.8%, P=.046. The discounts are just as concrete: by the 8- and 16-week follow-ups the self-reported outcomes no longer differed significantly; employment was self-reported in a sample of 126; the app, the coaches and the video contact were bundled, so no component can be credited; and the paper was published online in JMIR on October 20, with this item covering the November 10 institutional release.

Why it mattersFor anyone building digital mental health products, the useful lesson is not the 17.5-point gap but the framing: attach the psychological intervention to the outcome users actually want, a job, rather than to treating depression. That lowers the stigma of asking for help and turns behavioural activation into a deliverable task list. The fading follow-up effects point the other way, though: this looks like a starter, not a maintainer. Selling a long subscription requires answering why users come back after week eight.
