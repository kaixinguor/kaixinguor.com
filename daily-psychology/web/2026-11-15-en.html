11/13 Florida International University (EurekAlert) ↗

255 children with ADHD, randomised: extended time, frequent breaks, standing desks and quiet corners did not improve classroom behaviour or quiz scores

The Center for Children and Families at Florida International University ran a randomised clinical trial inside its own Summer Treatment Program, published online in JAMA Pediatrics on November 13, 2026 (DOI 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2026.5973). The study enrolled 255 children with ADHD in kindergarten through sixth grade between 2022 and 2025; 235 completed it. It tested four accommodations that are routinely written into individualised education programs (IEPs) and Section 504 plans: extended time on assignments, frequent breaks, standing at a desk, and a low-distraction workspace. Outcomes were classroom rule violations, academic productivity and quiz scores.

None of the four significantly improved classroom behaviour or quiz scores. Some were associated with reduced academic productivity, and stacking an accommodation on top of behavioural intervention or stimulant medication did not change that. Within the same data, behavioural intervention — structured points, clear expectations, immediate feedback, praise and rewards tied to individual goals — reduced rule violations by 37% relative to a general classroom setting. Stimulant medication, against placebo, cut violations by 61% and increased the rate of correctly completed assignments by 22%.

Lead author Gregory Fabiano, a professor of psychology at FIU, said the team was surprised that none of the accommodations produced a significant effect, and argued that these approaches "fail to build skills or address motivation". The authors are explicit about the boundary: the trial ran in structured summer treatment classrooms rather than ordinary schools, so whether the findings hold in everyday classrooms needs separate study. The late William E. Pelham Jr., who founded the center, helped design and conduct the work. The team's recommendation is to shift emphasis toward daily report cards, clear expectations, consistent feedback and rewards for individual goals.

Why it mattersWhat this trial changes is not which treatment wins but whether a practice almost every school uses has ever been tested on its own. An accommodation lowers the task threshold; a behavioural intervention changes the immediate consequences of behaviour. The first costs almost no staff time, the second costs a great deal, and budget incentives point the opposite way from the evidence, which is why the default has been the first. Changing that needs an evidence page at the IEP meeting, not another awareness campaign. Read the effect sizes on their own scales: 37% and 61% are relative reductions in rule violations, 22% is a gain in correctly completed work, all measured short-term inside structured classrooms. This design cannot speak to long-term achievement or quality of life.
Another angle If these accommodations stay in 504 plans and IEPs at their current scale, the cost lands on district meetings and teachers' implementation time, and no mechanism currently triggers a review of existing clauses when a null result like this appears.

11/13 Psychophysiology (PubMed record) ↗

151 adults in the lab: chronic loneliness goes with heart-rate variability that fails to withdraw during cognitive work, and the link survives adjustment for depression and anxiety

A study published online in Psychophysiology on November 13, 2026 split loneliness along two time scales: chronic loneliness over the past five years and situational loneliness over the past 24 hours. One hundred and fifty-one participants completed attention, perception, short-term memory, working-memory and executive-function tasks while continuous electrocardiography was recorded; vagally mediated heart-rate variability (vmHRV) reactivity served as the index of autonomic flexibility. Charlotte M. Roddick is first author and Frances S. Chen of the University of British Columbia is senior author.

Linear mixed-effects models showed that higher chronic loneliness was robustly associated with blunted vmHRV reactivity during attention, perception, working-memory and executive-function demands — that is, cardiac vagal withdrawal that should have happened did not. The associations persisted after adjusting for depressive symptoms, anxiety, perceived stress, emotion regulation, structural features of the social network, health behaviours and demographics. Situational loneliness showed weaker and less consistent associations. Exploratory analyses suggested that people high on both chronic and situational loneliness had especially blunted reactivity during working-memory demands, which the authors describe as a possible additive burden.

The design limits matter: this is a cross-sectional association, not a trial or a longitudinal cohort, so it cannot establish whether loneliness came first or an existing difference in autonomic regulation did. Participants were adults recruited into a laboratory, and the demands of five short tasks are not the demands of a working week. The authors' own conclusion is that chronic loneliness is an independent correlate of disrupted neurovisceral regulation — a much narrower claim than "loneliness damages health".

Why it mattersThe most useful move here is the split by time scale: loneliness over 24 hours and loneliness over five years behave differently on a physiological measure, while most questionnaires ask only whether someone has felt lonely lately. In practice that means a high score on a single screening should not be read as chronic exposure; what tracks with autonomic regulation is the version that has lasted years. What is missing is direction and dose. Longitudinal data would need to answer how long loneliness must persist before vmHRV changes, and a trial would need to show whether reducing loneliness restores the response. Until then this belongs in the mechanism column, not in causal health messaging.

11/13 Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics (PubMed record) ↗

266 autistic adults in a multicentre trial: guided self-help adapted for autism cut depression scores by 5.05 points at 16 weeks, and the gap held at one year

ADEPT-2 was a two-arm, multicentre, randomised phase 3 trial published online in Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics on November 13, 2026. Adults aged 18 or older with a confirmed autism diagnosis and depressive symptoms at PHQ-9 of 10 or above were randomised to guided self-help (GSH) adapted for autism (n=132) or treatment as usual (n=134). The intervention combined psychoeducation and behavioural activation with nine meetings with a coach; the trial was registered on August 9, 2022 (ISRCTN 17547011).

The primary outcome was the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) at 16 weeks after randomisation, analysed on an intention-to-treat basis. The estimated between-group mean difference was -5.05 points (95% CI -7.85 to -2.24, P<0.001, d=0.47) in favour of adapted GSH, and the difference was sustained at 52 weeks. On dose, 110 of the 132 participants in the GSH arm (81%) completed the minimum six sessions, five never started and 28 discontinued. Anxiety, well-being and service use were measured at 16 weeks, with follow-up at 32 and 52 weeks.

The team named the intervention AASH-DEP (Autism Affirming Accompanied Self-Help for depression) and argue in their conclusion for roll-out, on the grounds that autistic people carry a heavier burden of depression while standard first-line guided self-help was not designed around their needs.

Why it mattersAn effect of d=0.47 sits in the usual moderate range for psychotherapy, so three design details carry more weight than the number. The comparator was treatment as usual rather than a waiting list, so the intervention beat existing care. The gain held at 52 weeks, so it is not a short-lived placebo response. And 81% reached the minimum dose, which says something about whether a low-intensity, coach-supported format is workable for this group. What remains is scalability: who pays for nine coach sessions, what training the coaches need, and whether the result replicates outside specialist clinics. For policy this is a more specific route than "expand psychotherapy capacity" — adapt the intervention to the population, then deliver it with lower-intensity staff.

11/13 WHO Regional Office for Europe ↗

WHO Europe and UNICEF publish a mapping tool for child and youth mental health: 12 blocks across seven sectors

The WHO Regional Office for Europe and UNICEF published the Multisectoral mapping tool for child and youth mental health services and systems on November 13, 2026. The 76-page document (WHO/EURO:2026-11519-51291-78214) is organised into 12 blocks: context; leadership and governance across sectors; financing; the specialised workforce; health-sector services; education-sector services; social welfare; criminal justice and law enforcement; accommodation services; workplace services; data collection and research; and a closing block on next actions.

The regional picture it cites: one in seven children and adolescents in the European Region lives with a mental health condition, and suicide is the leading cause of death among 15- to 29-year-olds. Many children face barriers to health services, educational opportunity, social support, family stability and community resources. The tool is an output of the WHO-UNICEF joint strategy for 2026-2030, A healthy start for a healthy life, aimed at policy-makers, health professionals, educators and social service providers mapping gaps together.

Methodologically it is a checklist, not a standard: the document states that users may adapt it to their own context and needs, and that mapping results are meant to identify priority areas for improvement rather than to produce comparable scores or rankings across countries.

Why it mattersThe value of a tool like this is not in a number but in how it breaks "mental health services" out of a single clinic room and into items each sector can be asked about: how many counsellors a school actually has, whether the justice system has a referral route, whether accommodation services accept adolescents with behavioural problems. The weakness is equally clear. Self-assessed, adaptable and scoreless means results cannot be compared across countries or used to hold anyone to account. Judging whether a mapping exercise changed services requires a second measurement later, and the document sets no interval for repeating it.
Another angle For finance ministries the most consequential block may be the third: it asks for cross-sector spending to be itemised, which is exactly where national mental health budgets are usually blended into one opaque line.

11/13 Nature Neuroscience ↗

UCLA turns up the surviving copy of a gene with enhancer-targeted CRISPR: phenotypes reverse in organoid models of two autism-linked genes, and only in mutant cells

A team led by Daniel Geschwind at UCLA Health took a different route to conditions caused by losing one working copy of a gene, publishing in Nature Neuroscience on November 13, 2026. Rather than replace or edit the faulty copy, the researchers used a modified form of CRISPR to act on enhancers — the dials that set when and how strongly a gene is expressed — and raise the activity of the healthy copy that remains. The work focused on two genes strongly tied to autism: CHD8, which regulates other developmental genes, and SCN2A, which is needed for neurons to fire. The authors note that roughly 15% of autism's genetic risk comes from mutations that appear for the first time in a child rather than being inherited, and that many of these disable one of the two gene copies.

The experiments used two human stem-cell-derived models: cultures of excitatory neurons and cortical organoids. In CHD8 models, boosting the healthy copy reduced abnormal organoid overgrowth, helped cells mature and shifted gene activity closer to typical levels. In SCN2A models, the treatment restored much of the neurons' normal electrical activity, although some finer electrophysiological properties normalised only in certain cell lines. The effect appeared only in cells carrying the mutation: healthy cells showed no increase in expression and neighbouring genes were unaffected. Timing mattered, suggesting the approach may work best at particular stages of development.

The authors describe the work as a proof of concept rather than a treatment. Everything was done in cell models, the size of the shift from mutant to normal expression varied between cell lines, and delivering the approach safely to the brain will require better delivery methods plus an answer to whether it works after the early developmental window has closed. First author George Chen, formerly a postdoctoral fellow in the Geschwind lab, is now an assistant professor of neurology at Children's Hospital Los Angeles.

Why it mattersThe real selling point is specificity: acting only in mutant cells and leaving neighbours untouched addresses the objection most often raised against gene therapy, and targeting enhancers rather than the gene itself should in principle let expression follow the rhythm of normal development instead of overshooting into harmful overexpression. But between an organoid and a patient stand four gates — delivery, dosing, timing and long-term safety — and any one of them can erase the effect. The finding that some SCN2A electrical properties recovered only in certain cell lines already signals sensitivity to genetic background. For industry this is a route measured in years, and one likely to reach rare single-copy conditions first; it should not be read as autism being on the verge of a cure.
