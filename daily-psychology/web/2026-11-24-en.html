11/23 JAMA Pediatrics (paper) ↗

Three years, 10,048 Chinese adolescents: every extra year of both heavy viewing and compulsive use raises the risk of a suicide attempt by 63 per cent

JAMA Pediatrics published the study online on November 23, 2026 (DOI 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2026.5235). Y. Duan is first author and R. Chen corresponding author, both at the Vanke School of Public Health at Tsinghua University, with co-authors from Peking University Sixth Hospital, the Peking University Institute of Mental Health, the National Clinical Research Center for Mental Disorders, the PKU-IDG/McGovern Institute for Brain Research and the Department of Psychology at Beijing Forestry University. The data come from a three-wave prospective cohort in southern China running from 2022 to 2024, with annual follow-up; the analysis was carried out between November 2025 and April 2026.

The methodological point is not the phrase “short-form video” but the two-by-two: one axis is viewing time, the other is addictive use, and crossing them yields four states — low time and low addictive use, high time and low addictive use, low time and high addictive use, and high on both. Year one held 10,048 adolescents with a mean age of 13.9 (SD 1.3); 5,431 were girls (54.1 per cent). A total of 1,025 (10.2 per cent) sat in the joint high state and 2,592 (25.8 per cent) were high on either dimension. The gap between those two numbers is the paper's reason for existing: measure only time, and the 15.6 percentage points in between get classified wrongly.

Outcomes were collected in year three: suicidal ideation (SI), suicide attempts (SA), non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI), and the internalising and externalising problem scales of the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire. Estimation used modified Poisson regression with robust variance, which keeps a common binary outcome from inflating an odds ratio.

The results come in two layers. On transitions: compared with adolescents high on neither dimension, those who moved into the joint high state were at higher risk across all five outcomes, whereas those who moved back out of it were no longer at significantly higher risk of SI, SA or NSSI — though internalising problems stayed at aRR 1.63 (95% CI 1.31 to 2.04) and externalising problems at 2.20 (1.74 to 2.79). Stepping back is not a reset. On accumulation: each additional year in the joint high state carried aRR 1.26 for SI (1.20 to 1.32), 1.63 for SA (1.47 to 1.81), 1.33 for NSSI (1.22 to 1.46), 1.47 for internalising (1.39 to 1.55) and 1.66 for externalising problems (1.58 to 1.76).

The caveats need room. The design is observational and both exposure and outcome are self-reported; the sample comes from southern China and does not generalise to other countries or to northern China; the three waves straddle the later pandemic years, so period effects cannot be separated from platform-algorithm changes; and aRRs are associations whose arrows may run both ways, since adolescents whose mood is already poor are the ones most likely to slide into compulsive use. If you or someone near you is having thoughts of suicide, China's national psychological assistance hotline is 12356 and the US line is 988.

Why it mattersThe contribution is not “short videos are harmful” but the separation of two exposures that usually travel together. Measure time alone and you merge the adolescent who watches a lot and can stop with the one who watches less and cannot — and the second carries the steeper gradient: 1.63 for attempts against 1.26 for ideation is a 0.37 gap, which says cumulative exposure pushes mainly towards behaviour, not towards thoughts. On evidence, three waves, ten thousand adolescents and intervals this tight (1.47 to 1.81 for SA) make this dependable for risk stratification; self-reported exposure, a single region and no randomisation mean it establishes neither causation nor whether limiting time helps. The cost lands where the levers are: if compulsive use predicts risk independently of hours, the modifiable variables sit in product defaults — autoplay, infinite scroll, night-time pushes — rather than in the 40-minute rule a parent administers. This study measured exposure and tested no intervention.
Another angle A school screening model built on hours would miss the adolescents in that 15.6-point band whose problem is stopping rather than watching; asking one extra question about compulsive use costs almost nothing.

11/24 Journal of Gerontological Nursing (paper) ↗

Seven years, 5,623 older Chinese adults: caring for grandchildren tracks a 10 per cent lower risk of depression, which reverses once the load gets heavy

The Journal of Gerontological Nursing published the study online on November 24, 2026 (DOI 10.3928/00989134-20261116-01). Yang H, Du Y, Wang M, Chen C, Ye Z and Zhu X wrote it with corresponding author Guo C, all at the Institute of Population Research at Peking University. The data are the 2011 to 2018 waves of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), covering 5,623 participants.

The method is a Cox proportional hazards model for incident depression over follow-up, with grandchild caregiving as the exposure and caregiving intensity examined alongside it. CHARLS draws nationally across regions and across the urban-rural divide, which is what gives this result more weight than a single-site survey would carry.

Two sentences hold the result. First, caregiving was associated with a lower risk of depression, hazard ratio 0.90 (95% CI 0.83 to 0.97, p = .012) — roughly 10 per cent lower. Second, and more interesting: as caregiving intensity rose, that protective association weakened and turned negative. The curve is not monotonic.

Caveats: this is an observational cohort in which caregiving is neither randomly assigned nor exogenous. Adults willing and able to mind grandchildren are plausibly healthier, better supported and on steadier terms with their children — textbook selection. The reversal at high intensity points the same way as the role-burden hypothesis, but cohort data can only suggest it and cannot supply a dose threshold in hours per week. CHARLS covers middle-aged and older adults, so the finding does not extend to the whole working-age population, and the outcome is a screening scale rather than a diagnosis.

Why it mattersThis converts a moral argument about whether grandparents should mind grandchildren into a measurable dose question. The average association is protective at HR 0.90, but the curve is not monotonic: at low intensity the work brings structure and contact, at high intensity it brings the absence of an exit, and the word “caregiving” fuses the two. On evidence, seven years, 5,623 people and a Cox model are the hard part; the soft part is self-reported intensity, no randomisation, and a protective effect of 10 per cent that sits near the minimum clinically important difference on a symptom scale. The cost lands at the seam between family and public budgets: if heavy caregiving really does raise risk, the effective intervention is not telling grandparents to rest more but supplying childcare places and respite care — neither of which appears in a framework of filial duty, and both of which decide where the curve bends.

11/23 Psychological Medicine (paper) ↗

Nine randomised trials, 4,290 people: baseline psychomotor retardation means 26 per cent lower odds of remission, until full baseline severity is controlled

Psychological Medicine, volume 56, article e366, published the analysis on November 23, 2026 (DOI 10.1017/S0033291726106448). J. B. Badenoch is first author, at the Institute of Psychiatry, Psychology and Neuroscience at King's College London and the South London and Maudsley NHS Foundation Trust; J. P. Rogers is corresponding author, with co-authors from the CORE Data Lab at University College London, the North London NHS Foundation Trust talking-therapies service, and neuropsychiatry at the National Hospital for Neurology and Neurosurgery.

The design is a secondary analysis of individual participant data from Dep-GP: nine randomised trials of unipolar depression run in UK primary care, covering pharmacological, psychological and exercise treatments. Baseline psychomotor retardation was self-reported on the Revised Clinical Interview Schedule — one symptom item on a structured interview, not an instrumented measure.

Outcomes and estimation: the primary outcome is the standardised score (z) of each trial's validated self-report depression measure three to four months after randomisation, and the secondary outcome is remission. Within-study regressions adjust for age, sex, ethnicity, marital status, employment and allocated treatment; estimates are then pooled with random effects and heterogeneity is reported as I².

Of 4,290 participants, 2,754 (64.2 per cent) reported psychomotor retardation. Pooled, they scored 0.184 standard deviations higher at follow-up (95% CI 0.059 to 0.309, p = .004, I² = 68.7 per cent) and had lower odds of remission (OR 0.737, 95% CI 0.577 to 0.941, p = .015, I² = 57.7 per cent). The robustness sequence matters most: the association survived adjustment for core depressive symptoms, and did not survive adjustment for full baseline severity.

Reading limits: this is a secondary analysis, not a new trial; z = 0.184 is a small effect and OR 0.737 is a 26 per cent shift in odds. With I² at 68.7 and 57.7 per cent, the nine trials disagree substantially, so the pooled figure is an average and not anyone's individual effect. And the disappearance under full adjustment is not a flaw — it is the most common outcome in prognostic-factor research, and the one most often left out of abstracts.

Why it mattersThe paper takes half its own conclusion back: retardation tracks a worse prognosis until you put baseline severity in the model, at which point the signal is gone. That makes retardation look like a marker of severity rather than an independent prognostic factor, and it means there is no support for intensifying treatment on the strength of it. On evidence, individual-participant secondary analysis is hard currency among observational designs — nine trials, person-level data, uniform adjustment — but the effect is small, heterogeneity is high, and the vanishing act under adjustment is itself the finding worth carrying. The cost is where the cheap move lives: asking in clinic whether getting up, speaking and walking have slowed costs nothing and beats adding another scale; what the paper rules out is treating the answer as a basis for choosing treatment or setting its length. What would move this forward is a reproducible measure — gait or speech tasks, say — followed by prospective testing.

11/23 Complementary Therapies in Medicine (paper) ↗

A 42-patient randomised pilot: Tuina buys one extra median HAMD-17 point, and cortisol explains 19.4 per cent of it

Complementary Therapies in Medicine published the trial online on November 23, 2026 (DOI 10.1016/j.ctim.2026.103494), article 103494. Zhang SX is first author and Tao JM corresponding author, in the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine at Shuguang Hospital, affiliated with Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. The trial is registered as ChiCTR2400085211.

The design is a two-week, randomised, assessor-blind, active-controlled pilot: 42 patients with post-stroke depression (PSD) were assigned to Tuina (n = 21) or cognitive behavioural therapy (n = 21). The primary outcome was change on the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17); secondary outcomes included the modified Barthel Index, the Fugl-Meyer Assessment, the MMSE, and serum ACTH, cortisol and inflammatory cytokines. Analysis was by intention to treat, with non-parametric tests and bootstrap mediation.

Both arms improved significantly on HAMD-17 and the Barthel Index (p < .01). Tuina produced the larger HAMD-17 reduction (median change −5.00 against −4.00, p < .001) and larger falls in ACTH (p = .023) and cortisol (p = .021). The Fugl-Meyer score, the MMSE and every inflammatory cytokine showed no between-group difference — which matters, because it rules out the popular account that Tuina works through anti-inflammatory action.

On mechanism: the drop in cortisol mediated 19.4 per cent of the HAMD-17 improvement (indirect effect 0.2867, 95% CI 0.0337 to 0.5975). The lower bound sits close to zero, so this is a fragile signal; put the other way, more than four-fifths of the improvement ran through pathways the trial never measured.

Limits: 42 people, two weeks, one centre, a pilot. Assessor blinding is not participant blinding — Tuina cannot be disguised, so expectancy and contact time remain uncontrolled — and if assessors could infer allocation from scheduling, bias can still enter the ratings. A mediation estimate of 19.4 per cent from a sample this size is not a stable number.

Why it mattersThe number to remember is the size, not the verdict: one extra median point on a scale that runs from 0 to 52, which falls below the minimum clinically important difference of roughly three to four points. Statistical significance and clinical importance part company precisely here, and p < .001 is easy to misread as the latter. The mechanistic lead is more interesting than the efficacy claim: the HPA axis made it into the conclusion, yet mediation was only 19.4 per cent and every cytokine came back flat, which is what closes the anti-inflammatory story. On evidence, randomisation, an active comparator and a registration number put this near the well-run end of traditional-medicine trials; 42 patients, two weeks, one site and no participant blinding confine it to a source of endpoints and dose ranges for a larger study. The cost is symmetrical: a Tuina session consumes a trained practitioner, a room and about forty minutes, roughly what a CBT session consumes — and at one extra point, no payer has a reason to put it on a depression pathway unless a bigger trial shows comparable remission rates at eight weeks or beyond.

11/23 Brain, Behavior, and Immunity (paper) ↗

A 6,064-person US sample: the customary CRP cut-off of 10 mg/L removes nearly 10 per cent of the people who are depressed

Brain, Behavior, and Immunity published the study online on November 23, 2026 (DOI 10.1016/j.bbi.2026.107290), article 107290. J. O'Shields is first author with M. Macaluso, J. Cranford and M. Eason, in the Department of Social Work and the Department of Psychiatry and Behavioral Neurobiology at the University of Alabama at Birmingham. The data are the 2021 to 2023 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), n = 6,064 adults aged 20 and over, 51.53 per cent female and 60.15 per cent non-Hispanic White.

The approach splits the sample at CRP of 10 mg/L, defines probable depression as PHQ-9 of 10 or above, and runs weighted multinomial regressions on demographic, behavioural, biomarker and health-condition predictors of elevated CRP, depression, and their co-occurrence. The 10 mg/L line is the object of study because inflammation-and-depression research has long used it to screen out acute illness.

About 7.11 per cent of US adults had CRP at or above 10 mg/L. Probable depression ran at 12.51 per cent, and nearly 10 per cent of that group also had CRP at or above 10 — so the customary exclusion deletes close to a tenth of any depression sample, and the tenth it deletes is not missing at random.

Who gets deleted: co-occurring high CRP and depression was associated with higher BMI, lower socioeconomic status and sleep disturbance, and non-Hispanic Black participants were more likely to reach CRP of 10 mg/L despite similar odds of depression. The authors' recommendation is not a higher threshold but stratification — by inflammatory burden, or with multi-marker profiles — to keep the sample's diversity.

Limits: cross-sectional, with a single CRP measurement. Acute infection and chronic low-grade inflammation cannot be told apart in one draw, which is exactly why the 10 mg/L line was adopted and exactly why it must misfire. Weighted NHANES represents the community-dwelling US population, not inpatients or specialty samples, and probable depression is a screening result rather than a diagnosis.

Why it mattersThis is a methods story whose costs land on patients. A convention that drops everyone above 10 mg/L systematically removes the depressed people with higher BMI, lower socioeconomic status and worse sleep — not noise, but the more clinically complicated end of the distribution. On evidence, NHANES sampling and weighting are solid; a cross-sectional design can only show what appears alongside what, not whether inflammation or depression came first. The cost is external validity for research and interpretation for clinic: a depressed patient with CRP of 12 mg/L should not be read as “probably just an infection”. The cheaper alternative is already in the paper — stratify rather than exclude.

11/23 Military Psychology (paper) ↗

Fifteen trials, 2,462 veterans: digital therapeutics cut PTSD symptoms by 0.47 standard deviations, with 62.5 per cent completing

Military Psychology published the review online on November 23, 2026 (DOI 10.1080/08995605.2026.2753623). Chen X, Zhang L, Ren H, Dong N and Li N, of the Department of Physiological Psychology at the Xingcheng Special Duty Sanatorium of the Joint Logistics Support Force, pooled randomised and pilot-randomised studies of digital therapeutics and mobile health interventions for military veterans with post-traumatic stress disorder or depressive symptoms.

Methods: random-effects pooling, Hedges' g for continuous outcomes and risk ratios for dropout, with risk of bias assessed on RoB 2 and certainty graded with GRADE. Doing all three means the authors attach a confidence label to the pooled number rather than only computing it.

Results: 15 studies, 2,462 participants. PTSD symptoms fell by g = −0.47 (95% CI −0.71 to −0.23, p < .001) and depressive symptoms by g = −0.42 (−0.65 to −0.19, p < .001). Dropout did not differ significantly from control (RR 1.18, 0.94 to 1.48), pooled completion reached 62.5 per cent, and users generally rated acceptability and usability favourably. Certainty of evidence was low or very low.

Limits: g = −0.47 is a moderate effect, roughly what low-intensity face-to-face psychotherapy reports; GRADE low to very low means the figure cannot be treated as settled, since most included trials were pilots with short follow-up and varied control conditions. Completion at 62.5 per cent is the hard adherence number — close to four in ten did not finish. The dropout risk ratio has an interval crossing 1, so the worry that digital care retains people worse is neither confirmed nor excluded by these data.

Why it mattersSplit “can digital therapeutics substitute” into the two quantities that can be measured and the answer separates: the effect (0.47 standard deviations) is presentable, and the attrition (62.5 per cent completion) eats the real-world version of it — and g = −0.47 is already an intention-to-treat figure, so the share who download and finish in ordinary use will be lower still. For purchasers, cost per completed course therefore sits an order of magnitude above cost per issued account, and the two land in different budget lines. For clinicians, the unanswered question is who finishes eight to twelve weeks without a therapist, and almost none of the 15 trials treated adherence as a primary endpoint. The cost falls on the tightest constraint in veterans' care — therapist supply and geography: digital delivery saves clinician hours rather than total cost, unless completion can be engineered upwards with reminders, human coaching or stepped-down scheduling.

11/23 PNAS (paper) ↗

Two experiments, 1,514 Americans: LLM dialogue lowered belief in conspiracies while they unfolded, with only partial persistence at one to two months

PNAS, volume 123, issue 48, article e2628011123, published the study online on November 23, 2026 (DOI 10.1073/pnas.2628011123). T. H. Costello, of Social and Decision Sciences at Carnegie Mellon University, wrote it with N. Rabb of the MIT Sloan School of Management, M. N. Stagnaro, G. Pennycook of the Department of Psychology at Cornell University, and D. G. Rand of MIT Sloan and Cornell's departments of psychology and information science.

Timing is the design. Experiment 1 (N = 472) ran within days of the July 2024 assassination attempt on Donald Trump; experiment 2 (N = 1,042) ran after the September 2025 assassination of Charlie Kirk. Both samples were US adults holding conspiratorial views about the event, and the treatment was a multi-turn conversation with a large language model prompted to reduce those beliefs.

Two controls: a conversation with an LLM about an unrelated topic, and a static fact sheet. In both experiments the treatment group ended significantly lower on conspiracy belief than either control, which implies the dialogue did work that the facts alone did not.

Follow-up ran one to two months out, where some analyses showed reduced belief in various conspiracies and some did not. The authors' own phrase is suggestive evidence; durability was not written up as a conclusion.

Limits: the treatment is goal-directed dialogue, not companionship and not therapy; the outcome is a self-reported belief scale, not behaviour; both samples are US adults and both events are American political ones, so nothing here generalises to other countries or to health rumours. The abstract reports direction and significance rather than effect sizes, which sit in the full text, and the follow-up window is a month or two — a single conversation's effect on long-run attitudes is not settled here.

Why it mattersThis supplies an inverted reference point for the question of whether LLMs can stand in for human mental-health care: they work on persuasion tasks that are goal-directed, fact-checkable and confined to a few turns — the opposite of what therapy needs, which is a long relationship with no preset conclusion. The same capability, aimed at two different tasks, yields opposite ethical verdicts. On evidence, two experiments run around real events with two control arms are strong for the persuasion literature; the weak points are partial persistence, a self-reported outcome and effect sizes absent from the abstract. The cost lands on platforms and public communication: if one conversation can push a belief down, the same capacity can push one up, and what decides direction is the prompt and whose budget pays. Regulators who watch only whether an AI is offering therapy will miss this more ordinary risk.
