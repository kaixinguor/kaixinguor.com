11/22 Journal of Adolescent Health (paper) ↗

Suicide among Americans aged 10 to 24 rose 33.6 per cent from 2000 to 2019, and rates climbed in 3,055 of 3,079 counties

The Journal of Adolescent Health published the study online on November 22, 2026 (DOI 10.1016/j.jadohealth.2026.09.008). S. G. Rahman is first author, joined by A. S. Hinerman, L. Dwyer-Lindgren, E. J. Rodriquez, P. D. Strassle, C. J. L. Murray, A. H. Mokdad and E. J. Pérez-Stable, from the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute and the National Institute on Minority Health and Health Disparities at the NIH, together with the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation at the University of Washington. The input is the National Vital Statistics System file of every death in the United States from January 1, 2000 to December 31, 2019, plus matching population estimates.

Rates were estimated by county for three age bands (10 to 14, 15 to 19, 20 to 24) and five groups (American Indian and Alaska Native, Asian, Black, Latino, and White), using small-area estimation to cope with thin counts, and correcting for misclassification of race and ethnicity on death certificates — not an optional step, since misclassification has historically run high for Native and Latino decedents.

Three layers of result. The national trend: the age-standardised rate for ages 10 to 24 rose from 7.7 per 100,000 (95% UI 7.4 to 8.0) to 10.3 (95% UI 10.0 to 10.6), an increase of 33.6 per cent (95% UI 27.9 to 39.9). The group gap: American Indian and Alaska Native youth were highest in both years, at 16.1 (13.0 to 19.7) in 2000 and 27.2 (22.0 to 33.4) in 2019. The geography: of the ten counties with the highest rates in 2019, seven were in Alaska, two in South Dakota and one in North Dakota.

The sentence that best makes the case against averages is this one — 3,055 of 3,079 counties (99.2 per cent) saw rates rise among 10- to 24-year-olds, and 61.4 per cent of those rises were statistically significant. This is not a handful of hot spots; it is a near-national lift, with hot spots where it lifted furthest. The caveats are firm: the series stops in 2019 and cannot speak to the pandemic years; the outcome is suicide as recorded on a death certificate, and misclassification correction reduces but does not remove under- and misreporting; small counties remain unstable, which is why the paper reports uncertainty intervals that carry both sampling and model uncertainty rather than confidence intervals. If you or someone near you is having thoughts of suicide, China's national psychological assistance hotline is 12356 and the US line is 988.

Why it mattersWhat changes here is the answer to “where is the problem”. A single national curve flattens the steep parts: opened up by county, almost every county is rising (99.2 per cent), while the highest ten sit in Alaska and the Dakotas — which means allocation by state misses counties, and messaging by national average misses people. On evidence, death registration is the hardest data in this domain, and the paper does the 20-year span, three age bands, five groups and the misclassification correction, so it is dependable as a description of the distribution; it is descriptive epidemiology, however, and explains neither why rates rose nor which intervention works. The time scale is the binding constraint: the curve ends in 2019, and the whole post-2020 argument about adolescent mental health — social media, the pandemic, changing help-seeking — happens outside it, so the series cannot be used to grade any recent policy.
Another angle For county-level prevention budgets, a nearly threefold gap between 27.2 in Native youth and 10.3 nationally means the same money buys very different returns depending on where it lands, while “99.2 per cent of counties are rising” warns that a high-risk-county screening model would miss most deaths.

11/22 International Journal of Audiology (paper) ↗

120 studies, 109,707 people with hearing impairment: loneliness is 0.39 standard deviations higher, and 0.97 among deaf participants against 0.29 among those hard of hearing

The International Journal of Audiology published the meta-analysis online on November 22, 2026 (DOI 10.1080/14992027.2026.2702206). Martin Pinquart and C. Pusch, of the Department of Psychology at Marburg University in Germany, searched Google Scholar, Medline, PsycInfo and Web of Science for two kinds of quantitative study: those reporting loneliness among people with hearing impairment against test norms or against people without hearing impairment, and those reporting a correlation between hearing impairment and loneliness.

The pool is 120 studies covering 109,707 people who are deaf or hard of hearing, combined in a random-effects multilevel model so that multiple effect sizes from one study are nested rather than counted as independent samples. The overall effect is g = 0.389 (95% CI 0.296 to 0.483).

Splitting the pool opens a wide gap: g = 0.971 among deaf participants and g = 0.291 among those who are hard of hearing. The moderators point the same way. Elevations in loneliness are larger when participants are younger and more likely to be unpartnered or unmarried; smaller in community-based probability samples than in convenience samples; and smaller in studies from high-income countries than elsewhere. The authors conclude that measures to prevent and reduce loneliness remain generally warranted, but that deaf and younger people should be the priority.

On interpretation: g is a standardised mean difference, and 0.39 is small to moderate. Most pooled studies are cross-sectional and correlational, so direction is unresolved — loneliness may reduce uptake of hearing aids and rehabilitation, and under-used hearing technology may deepen loneliness, with both arrows plausible. The larger effects in convenience samples suggest the published literature may overstate the real gap.

Why it mattersThis takes the everyday sentence “they cannot hear, so they are isolated” and splits it into two magnitudes. At 0.389 the overall effect is unremarkable for the loneliness literature, but the distance between 0.971 and 0.291 says that what cuts a person off is not the decibel threshold on an audiogram but whether the channel of communication is severed outright. The service implication is direct: hearing aids and cochlear implants address audibility, while deaf people need signing environments, live captioning and peer community — different budgets, and not deliverable by the same clinic. The 120 studies and 109,707 people are the hard part, and the multilevel model handles the nesting properly; the soft part is that the primary studies are largely correlational, convenience samples are over-represented and cross-country comparability is limited. The cost lands at the seam between rehabilitation and social insurance: hearing-aid fitting has a named payer and billing codes, interpreting and community services do not — so allocating by “strength of evidence” would systematically under-serve the group with the largest effect.

11/22 International Archives of Occupational and Environmental Health (paper) ↗

An MRI study of 110 workers: above 52 hours a week, the grey-to-white signal ratio differs in seven right-hemisphere cortical clusters

The International Archives of Occupational and Environmental Health, volume 99, issue 10, article 68, published the study online on November 22, 2026 (DOI 10.1007/s00420-026-02258-8). Jonghun Lee, Wonpil Jang, Youjin Kim, Wanhyung Lee and Joon Yul Choi split 110 participants by weekly hours: 32 worked more than 52 hours a week and 78 worked 52 or fewer.

The measurement deserves a sentence of its own. From 3.0 T three-dimensional T1-weighted images, the team sampled grey- and white-matter signal intensity along the cortical surface with FreeSurfer to compute the grey-to-white matter signal intensity ratio, or GWR — a signal-based marker of cortical microstructure, not thickness, not volume, and not a clinical diagnosis. Statistics used vertex-wise general linear models adjusted for age, sex and education, with cluster-wise Monte Carlo simulation for multiple comparisons.

Seven right-hemisphere clusters differed between groups (cluster-wise corrected p<0.05). GWR was higher in the overworked group in the precentral, postcentral, supramarginal and middle temporal cortices, and lower in the superior frontal cortex. In exploratory analyses on the same clusters — selected from the same sample — associations with continuous weekly hours ran in the same direction as the group differences (FDR-corrected p≤0.004), and GWR in the right precentral and supramarginal clusters was negatively associated with regional white-matter fractional anisotropy (FDR-corrected p<0.05).

The limits are structural: cross-sectional, 110 people, one centre, with 52 hours as a grouping threshold rather than a dose. GWR shifts with scanner, sequence and cortical modelling, so cross-site comparison needs extra calibration. The authors attach no direction to “higher” or “lower”, writing only that the ratio is associated with cortical integrity — so “overwork makes you dull” has no support here, because there is no cognitive testing and no clinical outcome.

Why it mattersThe value is not the verdict that overwork harms the brain but the instrument: signal sampled along the cortex instead of a questionnaire asking whether you feel tired. The usual failure mode in imaging studies of this size is endpoint proliferation, and here the seven clusters do not even agree in direction — mostly higher, lower in superior frontal — and the authors report them together rather than promoting one to a headline, which is more honest than the alternative. On evidence, cluster correction and FDR are both applied, which is decent practice for a small imaging sample; but with no follow-up and no cognitive or functional endpoint the paper supports “worth pursuing” and nothing about working-time law. The cost sits between employers and workers: 52 hours is a looser line than the 48-hour weekly average in the EU Working Time Directive, and this study cannot supply a medical basis for any statutory limit — it can only give the question “why 48 and not 60” a candidate measurable marker, provided someone runs the longitudinal, multi-site, cognitively endpointed study.

11/22 Discover Mental Health (paper) ↗

221 of Japan's 335 secondary medical areas have no psychiatric emergency bed at all, and 30.7 per cent score zero on accessibility

Discover Mental Health published the study online on November 22, 2026 (DOI 10.1007/s44192-026-00623-0). Yuki Egashira and Ryo Watanabe, of Kanagawa University of Human Services, applied the enhanced two-step floating catchment area (E2SFCA) method on a 500-metre grid to measure spatial access to psychiatric emergency care across all 335 secondary medical areas in Japan.

The grid holds 422,031 inhabited 500-metre cells. Demand is census population adjusted for age and sex; supply is the count of certified psychiatric emergency beds. Each area yields two numbers: a median accessibility score (MAS) per million population, and a Gini coefficient describing how unevenly that access is spread inside the area.

The results come in three layers. Supply: 221 of 335 areas (66.0 per cent) hold no psychiatric emergency bed at all. Level: the median MAS across areas is 18.7 (interquartile range 0.0 to 43.7) and the median Gini coefficient is 0.30. The floor: 103 areas (30.7 per cent) have a MAS of zero, and in 38 of them (11.3 per cent) no grid cell has modelled access at all, meaning the entire area sits outside the reach of any psychiatric emergency bed.

The counter-intuitive result is in the regression. The strongest correlate of MAS is the share of psychiatric beds allocated to emergency care (β = 0.476), followed by the ratio of uninhabitable land (β = −0.220) and psychiatrist density (β = 0.174); the share of psychiatric beds among all hospital beds is not significant. In the Gini model, uninhabitable land (β = 0.228) and the emergency share of psychiatric beds (β = −0.206) carry comparable weight, and the overall bed share is again non-significant. Cluster analysis sorts the areas into six regional types. The article is labelled an early-sharing accepted manuscript — citable, with a permanent DOI, but still subject to edits before the final version of record.

Why it mattersThis rewrites a planning metric: adding psychiatric beds and re-designating existing beds for emergency work are two different acts, and only the second tracks accessibility. For any system still building out psychiatric emergency capacity, the money belongs in re-designation and in overnight staffing rather than in a bed-count target; a plan built on total beds per capita will score “has beds but no emergency pathway” as compliant. On evidence, 335 areas, 422,031 grid cells and two inequality measures that corroborate each other make this dependable as a description of supply. But it is a supply map, not a demand map: no visits, no transport times, no patient outcomes, so what it demonstrates is poor reachability, not untreated patients. The cost lands on prefectures, because converting a bed to emergency use means giving up a chronic-care bed, and that trade is negative in a total-bed count.
Another angle The method travels to any place with gridded population and a bed list — and the binding question is not how many beds exist but whether anyone answers the phone there at night and at weekends.

11/22 Quality of Life Research (paper) ↗

Fifteen studies of rural community social support, exactly one randomised trial: the evidence stops at “may help”

Quality of Life Research, volume 35, issue 12, article 343, published the review online on November 22, 2026 (DOI 10.1007/s11136-026-04448-w). Fatamatuj Jahura Shuha and Leena Eklund Karlsson followed the Arksey and O'Malley framework, reported to PRISMA-ScR, searched four databases to 1,767 records, removed duplicates, screened in two phases and included 15 studies.

Of the 15, ten are quantitative, two qualitative and three mixed-methods; exactly one is a randomised controlled trial. The interventions include peer group support, social prescribing, volunteering and structured community participation. Most studies concern older adults, with little evidence on working-age adults.

The conclusion is deliberately modest: these interventions were linked to better mental wellbeing and quality of life, alongside more social interaction and stronger social ties, but the evidence remains limited, methodologically heterogeneous, with small samples and short follow-up. The authors call for stronger designs, larger samples and longer follow-up before policy leans on it.

What a scoping review can and cannot do: it maps a field rather than pooling effects, and with 15 studies and one trial there is no pooled effect to report and no way to rank one model of support above another. “Linked to” also cannot rule out reverse selection — people who already have more social capital are the ones who sign up to volunteer and to community activities.

Why it mattersThis gives social prescribing an honest coordinate on the evidence map. England's NHS committed in its 2019 Long Term Plan to 1,000 link workers to make referrals into community activity, and the randomised base for that kind of intervention remains thin — one trial out of 15 studies means we mostly know that participants did better later, not that participating made them better. The front-line read: if a budget buys only one thing, peer groups are cheaper and more scalable than one-to-one therapy, but nothing here says they are equivalent, and treating them as a substitute rather than a complement is unsupported. On evidence, PRISMA-ScR and two-phase screening make the search transparent; it cannot substitute for effect sizes. The cost lands at the local level, where the binding constraints in rural areas are transport, a room and somebody to run the group — none of which appear in the phrase “social prescribing”, and all of which are exactly what makes a randomised trial hard to run.

11/22 Pakistan Journal of Positive Psychology (paper) ↗

Two surveys of the same 300 students: the more a machine seems to have a mind, the stronger the bond — but that explains only 14 per cent of the variance

The Pakistan Journal of Positive Psychology published two surveys from one research group on November 22, 2026 (volume 3, issue 11, pages 33 to 39, DOI 10.67785/pjpp.11.473; volume 3, issue 12, pages 7 to 13, DOI 10.67785/pjpp.12.474). Zarafsha Akhtar, Naila Batool, Ifra Khan and Laiba Tahir, of the Department of Psychology at Kohsar University Murree, draw both on the same 300 university students (158 men, 142 women), recruited by convenience sampling and surveyed cross-sectionally by self-report.

The first paper measures AI anthropomorphism — the extent to which a person perceives AI as having a mind and feelings — against the human-AI relationship, using the Scale of AI Anthropomorphism (SAIA; Xu et al., 2026) and the Experiences in Human-AI Relationships Scale (EHARS; Yang and Oshio, 2025), with internal consistencies of α = .91 and .83. The two correlate at r = .38 (p<.001), anthropomorphism explains roughly 14 per cent of the variance in the relationship score, and men score slightly higher on both (d = 0.28 and 0.34).

The second paper swaps the predictor for reliance on AI (RAI-15; Yap et al., 2023; α = .76). Reliance correlates with the human-AI relationship at r = .23 (p<.001) and explains about 5 per cent of the variance; men report higher reliance (d = 0.32) and a stronger relationship (d = 0.34). Seeing a mind in the machine predicts the bond better than leaning on it for tasks — but neither effect is large.

The caveats need spelling out: convenience sample, one country, one university, 300 students, self-report instruments, cross-sectional design — none of which supports a causal claim. Both papers draw on the same sample, which is one dataset published twice and limits the value of either as independent confirmation. Fourteen and five per cent of variance leave most of the story elsewhere. SAIA and EHARS are both 2025 to 2026 instruments whose cross-cultural equivalence has not been tested, and no data yet show whether these coefficients replicate in Chinese or any other language.

Why it mattersThis is useful for the AI-companion argument, but in the opposite direction from the headline: it measures how human the machine seems and how close the reported relationship is, both by self-report, correlating at .38. What is worth noticing is the gap between the two papers — perceiving a mind (14 per cent) predicts attachment better than relying on the tool (5 per cent), which matches the anthropomorphism literature: emotional investment follows “seems human”, not “is useful”. For products, making the interaction more person-like raises attachment and therefore both stickiness and ethical risk; for clinicians, there is still no evidence that such attachment substitutes for human support, and none that it harms — what is needed is longitudinal design with clinical samples and behavioural endpoints, not another cross-sectional questionnaire. On evidence this is weak: a small journal, a single-university convenience sample, two papers from one dataset, no invariance testing. The cost lands on where regulation points: if the degree of anthropomorphism is the main driver, what needs disclosure and default settings is not whether AI is involved but how human it is built to seem.
