11/21 12:25 Xinhua ↗

About 11 per cent of 110,000-plus schoolchildren warrant particular attention, and the programme trained 3,730 peer supporters, who referred 306 students

Xinhua filed the story from Nanning at 12:25 Beijing time on November 21, 2026, against the backdrop of World Children's Day on November 20 and a Beijing event called “Choose a future for children” held as UNICEF approaches its eightieth anniversary. At the centre of the piece is a programme with three sponsors: China's National Health Commission, the Communist Youth League's central committee and UNICEF.

Three sets of numbers carry the story. The first is screening: a national survey released in May 2026 covered 31 provincial-level regions and collected more than 110,000 valid responses, finding that the mental health of about 11 per cent of primary and secondary school students warranted particular attention. The second is programme capacity: running from 2021 to 2025 across 10 provincial-level regions, it reached 329 schools and nearly 1.45 million student attendances, and trained 3,730 students in 58 schools as peer supporters, who identified and referred 306 students experiencing mental health difficulties. The third is local capacity: a pilot in Hubei launched in 2025 by the provincial civil affairs department with a public welfare foundation, where one county counselling centre delivered more than 500 individual interventions and organised more than 1,000 activities in a year, including group counselling, teacher training and parenting guidance.

The peer-support method is described concretely — look without judging, listen without rushing to solutions, then link the person to somewhere that can hold them. Trained students are not expected to manage cases; when something exceeds their ability they hand it to a school counsellor or to professional services. UNICEF's framing is that peer supporters help with early identification and with reducing stigma.

The caveats matter. Eleven per cent is where a screening instrument trips, not a prevalence rate for disorder. The 306 and the 3,730 are process measures: the report gives no figure for how many of those referred actually entered professional care, how long they waited, or whether their symptoms improved. The 2021–2025 programme has ended and no funding line for the next phase is named. There is a policy timetable, though. In September, Vice Minister of Civil Affairs Hu Haifeng said the authorities would build a sound system for early detection and intervention within three years, and that during the 15th Five-Year Plan, 2026 to 2030, civil affairs departments would strengthen mental health services for children in difficult circumstances.

Why it mattersWhat changes here is where the capacity sits. Once screening and referral become a national routine, the bottleneck moves from noticing to holding — and the ratio inside the programme makes the point: 3,730 trained students producing 306 referrals is about 8.2 per cent, a narrow entry point rather than a substitute for services. What decides the outcome is the segment after the door: whether a county can take the referral, how long the wait is, and who pays. The evidence has to be separated into two parts. Programme counts — schools, trainees, referrals — are solid records. The claim that peer support works needs a controlled chain from referral to first appointment to follow-up, and this report does not contain one. Budgeting from 11 per cent would systematically overstate clinical demand and understate waiting time. The cost lands at county level, where one centre's 500-plus interventions in a year were funded by a foundation and by government purchase of services; whether that money becomes a routine line in the 15th Five-Year Plan decides whether the model is a showcase or a standard.
Another angle On the time axis, a three-year deadline for an early detection and intervention system means delivering not just software by 2029 but people and payment at the receiving end — and the receiving end in this report is one county, one centre, one year of numbers.

11/21 Psychopharmacology (paper) ↗

Nine near-daily cannabis users, tested double-blind: alcohol wrecks lane control in a simulator, cannabis reduces brake force, and every active dose lowers confidence in your own driving

Psychopharmacology published the study online on November 21, 2026 (DOI 10.1007/s00213-026-07202-9). Maribeth Stafford is first author, with Hannah M. Harris, Paul A. Nuzzo, Michelle R. Lofwall, Laura C. Fanucchi, Sharon L. Walsh and Shanna Babalonis. The design was a within-subject, randomised, double-blind, double-dummy, placebo-controlled outpatient laboratory study in nine healthy adults who used cannabis regularly.

Each participant completed five sessions covering inhaled cannabis at 0, 15 and 30 mg THC, 15 mg THC plus 7.5 mg CBD, and oral alcohol at 0.8 g/kg, reduced by 15 per cent for women. Measurement ran at baseline and for 7.5 hours after dosing, on simulated driving and on subjective ratings.

The results come in three layers. Alcohol produced robust impairment on simulated driving, with lateral control among the measures reaching significance (p<0.05). Cannabis did not produce impairment of that depth, but it was not harmless: it reduced brake force and altered headway distance while navigating obstacles (p<0.05). Subjectively the picture was broader and more consistent — every active dose of cannabis and alcohol lowered driving confidence, including willingness to drive a real vehicle (p<0.05), and raised abuse-potential ratings such as drug liking (p<0.05). The 30 mg dose and alcohol also raised self-reported impairment and sedation.

The limits are plain. Nine participants, a simulator rather than a road or a crash database, and a sample of near-daily users whose tolerance will understate impairment in people who use occasionally. Turning brake force and headway on a simulator into crash risk requires extrapolation. Even the headline finding is easy to misread: the authors write that cannabis did not produce profound alcohol-like impairment, not that it produced none.

Why it mattersThis lands on the assumption that enforcement and legislation lean on hardest: that blood concentration and impairment sit on an alcohol-like dose–response curve, which is what makes a legal threshold meaningful. The difference visible here is specific. Alcohol degrades lateral control; cannabis shifts longitudinal control, braking and following distance. Those failures map onto different crash types, so they call for different detection and different training. It also explains why roadside cannabis testing is harder than a breathalyser: there is no general threshold to quote. The evidence is strong for its class — randomised, double-blind, double-dummy, seven and a half hours of follow-up — but nine people, one site, no effect sizes and no confidence intervals leave a long way between “headway changed in a simulator” and “crashes rose”. The cost lands in two places: detection, and risk communication. “I am not drunk” and “I am not driving well” are different sentences, and this study finds the second one at 15 mg of THC.

11/21 The European Journal of Health Economics (paper) ↗

A Greek panel study: income losses tracked mental health deterioration through the pandemic, while changes in weekly hours did not

The European Journal of Health Economics published the paper online on November 21, 2026 (DOI 10.1007/s10198-026-02016-0). Athina Raftopoulou and Nicholas Giannakopoulos examine the link between pandemic-era income disruption and deteriorating mental health in Greece, where job-retention schemes preserved employment relationships while workers could still lose earnings, have contracts suspended or see hours change.

The methodological move is to run two measures of income side by side: respondents' own reports of household income change, and measured changes in personal and household income, with labour and non-labour income separated. Changes in weekly working hours enter as the comparison. The point is to pull apart two things that usually move together — money falling and hours changing.

The results line up. Reported income decreases are consistently associated with a higher probability of COVID-related mental health deterioration, and the association holds among continuously employed workers and among people who stayed in the same occupation: no job loss required. Measured income shows the same direction, particularly for labour income, whereas changes in working hours are generally not statistically significant. The authors report that the findings survive a wide range of robustness checks.

The caveats: observational and longitudinal, with income declines not randomly assigned, so this is association, not causation. “COVID-related mental health deterioration” is a self-reported survey item, not a clinical diagnosis. And the data come from one country in one period, with comparatively strong job-retention policies — precisely what to keep in mind when carrying the result somewhere with different policies.

Why it mattersThe blunt claim that losing work damages mental health gets taken apart here. What appears to matter is the level and predictability of income rather than the number of hours or the mere fact of holding a job. The policy implication is direct: a retention scheme that protects contracts but not earnings gives back part of its mental health dividend, which makes the replacement rate in short-time work schemes a public health parameter with a measurable outcome, not only a fiscal one. The evidence is moderate to good — a panel design with two income measures corroborating each other beats a single cross-section — but it stays observational, and both measures ride on the same questionnaire, so shared reporting bias cannot be ruled out. The cost is split between the Treasury and employers: each step down in the replacement rate saves cash and spends health, and this paper is an estimate of the distance between the two.

11/21 Supportive Care in Cancer (paper) ↗

16 randomised trials, 1,127 lung cancer patients: exercise moves depression by 0.63 standard deviations, but the dose has to reach about 602 MET-minutes a week

Supportive Care in Cancer, volume 34, published the study online on November 21, 2026 (DOI 10.1007/s00520-026-11497-x). Bingyi Li is first author, with Yifeng Quan and Zhaoxiang Meng. It is a systematic review with three meta-analyses stacked together — pairwise, network and dose–response — drawing on PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials through April 2026.

Sixteen randomised controlled trials covering 1,127 patients with lung cancer were included. The pooled effect is a reduction in depression scores of SMD −0.63 (95% CI −0.88 to −0.38; I² = 72%, p<0.00001). Interventions were split by how closely they followed American College of Sports Medicine recommendations: the high-adherence group improved more, at SMD −0.81, against −0.38 in the low-adherence group. Anxiety, fatigue and sleep were analysed alongside depression.

The network analysis ranks exercise types, with aerobic exercise showing the largest effect on depression at SMD −1.30 (95% CI −2.08 to −0.51) — but rated low certainty under the CINeMA framework. The dose–response analysis finds that a significant improvement in depression becomes visible once total exercise volume reaches 601.72 MET-minutes per week, and that the relationship is non-linear.

The caveats: 16 trials is still a modest pooled sample, and I² of 72 per cent signals substantial between-trial heterogeneity. High versus low ACSM adherence is a post-hoc split, not randomisation, so it cannot be read as “follow the guidelines and you gain another 0.43 standard deviations”. Outcomes are depression scale scores, not diagnostic remission or response. And the network ranking that puts aerobic first is labelled low certainty by the authors' own framework, which rules it out as a prescribing rule. Their conclusion keeps the same distance: aerobic exercise may be an effective treatment for depression in lung cancer patients, on low-quality evidence.

Why it mattersPsychosocial oncology has long been missing the dose dimension: guidance says exercise is advisable, rarely how much or for how long. This paper offers an operational threshold, about 602 MET-minutes a week, and is honest enough to stamp “aerobic is best” as low certainty. What actually changes a clinical judgement is the gap between 0.81 and 0.38: structured adherence versus vague encouragement is roughly a doubling of effect, which means that writing “be more active” is, in effect, prescribing the low dose — and the low dose is worth 0.38 standard deviations here. The 16 randomised trials are the hard part of the evidence; the 72 per cent heterogeneity, the post-hoc split and the low-certainty ranking are the soft parts. The cost is easy to locate: exercise for lung cancer patients has to fit between treatments and needs a therapist, a space and scheduling, and most oncology departments have none of the three. The bottleneck is not the evidence but turning a dose into a referral pathway — and deciding who pays for it.

11/21 Journal of Immigrant and Minority Health (paper) ↗

47 studies, 35 health-literacy instruments for migrants: not one reports measurement error, and three test whether scores mean the same thing across groups

Journal of Immigrant and Minority Health published the review online on November 21, 2026 (DOI 10.1007/s10903-026-02035-2). Isaac Amankwaa is first author. The team searched seven databases plus supplementary sources for English-language quantitative and mixed-methods studies published between February 2015 and March 2025, and appraised measurement properties using COSMIN methodology.

The scale of the literature: 47 studies across 18 host countries, examining 35 instruments. Twenty-one were dedicated instrument-development or measurement-property studies; 26 were applied studies. In other words, most published work uses somebody else's instrument rather than demonstrating that it measures correctly in migrant populations.

The evidence is thin in a very specific pattern. Among the 21 dedicated studies, internal consistency was reported in 20 (95 per cent), structural validity in 17 (81 per cent), content validity evidence in 13 (62 per cent) and hypothesis testing in 14 (67 per cent). Test–retest reliability appeared in four studies, responsiveness in one, and measurement error in none. Three studies examined measurement invariance or differential item functioning, and only four instruments had measurement-property evidence from more than one study. Translation and cultural adaptation were judged to improve accessibility and conceptual relevance without establishing that scores are comparable. Affordability, documentation and legal worries, and trust or discrimination — the conditions that matter most in a migration context — were rarely documented. Every host setting was high-income or upper-middle-income.

What the review can and cannot say: it evaluates other people's studies and generates no new data. English-only inclusion may miss instruments developed and published in local languages, and COSMIN appraises what was reported, which cannot distinguish under-reporting from never having been done. The authors' recommendation is concrete — match the instrument to the intended use of the score, with tailored assessment for local service design and a shared construct plus comparability evidence for cross-group comparison.

Why it mattersThis presses on the everyday habit of scoring vulnerable groups with questionnaires. With measurement error reported nowhere and invariance tested three times, the same score may be good enough for local service design, but the moment it is used to compare groups — “migrants have lower health literacy” — the conclusion rests on an untested assumption. The diagnostic detail is telling: internal consistency is easy and reported by 95 per cent, while responsiveness and measurement error, the properties that decide whether a change in score means a change in a person, are almost absent. COSMIN is the accepted framework and 47 studies across 18 countries make this a dependable description of the field; what it cannot tell you is whether any single instrument is accurate, only that nobody has shown it is. The cost lands on the agencies and payers who use these scores to allocate: a scale without comparability evidence writes its error into a budget in the form of “this group has low need”, and that line is very hard to overturn later.

11/21 Clinical Oral Investigations (paper) ↗

96 children aged 6 to 8, randomised three ways: augmented reality improves cooperation in the dental chair, while the anxiety and stress markers disagree with each other

Clinical Oral Investigations, volume 30, published the trial online on November 21, 2026 (DOI 10.1007/s00784-026-07286-9). Erenay Alpayçetin is first author, with Yelda Kasımoğlu, Yeliz Güven, Şule Batu, Kerem Gavcar, Bora Şenceylan, Meral Kuyucu, Gökhan İnce and Elif Bahar Tuna İnce. Ninety-six children aged 6 to 8 who needed a pulpotomy were randomised into three groups of 32: routine care, cartoons, and augmented reality.

The AR arm folded the tell–show–do routine and positive reinforcement into an application the child watched during treatment. Five measurement lines ran in parallel: the Wong-Baker FACES scale, the Modified Child Dental Anxiety Scale or MCDAS(f), the Frankl behaviour rating scale, pulse rate, and three salivary stress markers — cortisol, alpha-amylase and chromogranin A.

The results disagree with each other, which is what makes the paper useful. In AR's favour: post-treatment Frankl scores were higher than routine care (p=0.011) and than cartoons (p=0.041), Wong-Baker scores were lower than routine care (p=0.034), cortisol fell further than in routine care (p=0.030) and cartoons (p=0.020), and alpha-amylase fell further than in routine care (p=0.001). Against: MCDAS(f) scores were already lower in the AR group at baseline, so after adjustment AR differed only from the cartoon group (p<0.001) and not from routine care (p=0.121); pulse rate changed within the AR group (p=0.001) but not between groups; and chromogranin A fell further in the cartoon group than in the AR group (p=0.005).

The authors' conclusion is restrained: AR was associated with better cooperation, but its effects on anxiety and physiological stress markers were inconsistent and no overall superiority was demonstrated. On design, 96 children, one centre, three randomised arms and objective salivary endpoints is not weak. What is missing matters commercially: the baseline MCDAS(f) imbalance, no follow-up to the next appointment, and no measurement of chair time or sedative use — the two quantities a clinic would actually count.

Why it mattersThis is a clean demonstration of the failure mode that digital mental health studies fall into most often: choose enough endpoints and one of them will be significant. Three of six measures favour AR, two do not, one reverses — and the authors decline to pick a winner, which is more useful than a tidy p-value. The industry read follows from what AR is actually doing. If it improves cooperation rather than reducing anxiety, its payer is not psychological treatment but paediatric dentistry's throughput: one visit instead of two, less sedation. Those are different economics and different reimbursement codes. The evidence is stronger than most “app improves mood” papers because of the three-arm randomisation and the salivary markers, but the baseline imbalance and absent follow-up keep “AR is better” at the level of direction. The cost sits in the clinic: a tablet and a content licence are cheap, and the real expense is the extra minutes of a nurse's time — minutes for which fee-for-service systems have nowhere to bill.
