11/25 Journal of Addiction Medicine (paper) ↗

Ten million US parents met criteria for a substance use disorder last year, and only 16 per cent were treated

The Journal of Addiction Medicine published the analysis online on November 25, 2026 (DOI 10.1097/ADM.0000000000001806). McCabe SE, Pasman E, McCabe VV and Schepis TS are the authors, and the paper carries the copyright line of the American Society of Addiction Medicine.

The design is a secondary analysis of weighted 2023 National Survey on Drug Use and Health data, restricted to the US civilian, non-institutionalised population aged 12 and over, with parents who live with their children carved out as the group of interest. The outcomes were past-year DSM-5 substance use disorder across alcohol, cannabis, cocaine, hallucinogens, heroin, inhalants, methamphetamine and nonmedical use of benzodiazepines, opioids and stimulants, together with severity, treatment receipt, perceived problem and self-identified recovery.

Scale comes first: about 10,440,020 parents living with their children met past-year criteria, and 4,318,360 of those cases were moderate or severe. The gap comes second: only 16.0 per cent of affected parents received any substance use treatment in the past year, more than seven in ten had neither treatment nor recovery on their record, and many did not see themselves as having a substance problem at all.

Recovery is where the definitions split. Of 5,288,968 parents who described themselves as in recovery, just 31.5 per cent were abstinent; 68.5 per cent were not. In this population, being better usually does not mean being off everything.

Limits: a single cross-sectional self-report wave, with disorder assigned from survey items rather than clinical interview. Recovery is self-defined and biologically unverified. Institutionalised and unhoused people sit outside the sampling frame, and parents living with children are not all parents. Treatment is any past-year substance use treatment, with no check on whether it was evidence-based.

Why it mattersThe 84 per cent figure is usually read as a supply problem, and the authors point somewhere else: people who do not think they have a problem do not queue for beds, so adding capacity along the current pathway may not pull them in. The second mechanism hides in the recovery numbers. If payers and courts keep abstinence as the only mark of success, two thirds of the people who count themselves recovered get scored as failures, and the programme that treated them looks ineffective. On evidence, a weighted national probability sample makes the magnitude credible; the soft parts are the single time point, the self-report and the absence of clinical verification, plus the fact that the parental subsample was drawn by the analysts rather than by the sampling frame. The cost lands on children as much as on parents, and child welfare, addiction treatment and school mental health are three separate budgets, none of which pays for prevention.
Another angle A 16.0 per cent treatment rate next to 68.5 per cent non-abstinent recovery suggests this population already defines improvement as reduction and control, while the system only counts two things: abstinence and enrolment.

11/25 PLoS One (paper) ↗

Forty-three studies, mostly cross-sectional: mindfulness travels with healthier social media use, cause unresolved

PLoS One published the review on November 25, 2026 (DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0337349). Jones CA and Tibber MS wrote it at the Research Department of Clinical, Educational and Health Psychology, University College London.

The design is a systematic review. PsycINFO, Medline and Web of Science were searched from inception to January 2026 for quantitative studies measuring mindfulness, social media use and mental health together, and 43 met criteria. The trouble starts with the modelling: mindfulness appears as an independent variable, a dependent variable, a mediator and a moderator across the included work, which means the studies are not answering one question.

Results ran overwhelmingly in one direction. Higher mindfulness went with better mental health and with more beneficial patterns of social media use, and wherever mediation or moderation was tested, mindfulness tended to buffer the link between social media use and distress.

The authors are blunt about what that supports. The literature is dominated by cross-sectional designs, non-WEIRD samples are scarce and participants skew young, so “mindfulness protects” stays a correlation. It does not license the claim that practising mindfulness offsets the harm of social media, and reverse pathways — people who are doing better find it easier to stay mindful — cannot be ruled out.

Why it mattersThe useful finding is the muddle, not the conclusion. When one variable is treated as cause, consequence, mediator and moderator across 43 studies, all four models can produce a positive story while giving opposite answers to the only question a buyer has: if I teach this, does anything change? On evidence, a systematic review without meta-analysis or pooled effect sizes, resting mostly on self-report scales, can organise a literature but cannot produce a dose. The cost falls on procurement: mindfulness apps and school programmes are already on purchase lists on the strength of cross-sectional data, and a budget allocated by evidence grade would not fund them. That is precisely why they land under employee wellbeing rather than treatment.

11/25 JAMA Psychiatry (paper) ↗

25,665 first-episode psychosis patients: intake records predict later clozapine, with a C of 0.71 and a PPV of 0.15

JAMA Psychiatry published the study online on November 25, 2026 (DOI 10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2026.3943). Pettersson-Yeo W is first author and Taquet M corresponding author, with affiliations spanning the Department of Psychiatry at the University of Oxford, Akrivia Health, Oxford Health NHS Foundation Trust and the NIHR Oxford Health Biomedical Research Centre.

The clinical problem is timing. Treatment resistance (TR) is normally established only after two or more antipsychotic trials fail at adequate dose and duration, which usually takes years from first episode, and clozapine — the only approved treatment for TR psychosis — arrives at the end of that wait. The question is whether the ordinary record written at first presentation already contains the answer.

This is prognostic model development and validation on electronic health records from 10 NHS mental health trusts in the Akrivia Health database. Everyone diagnosed with first-episode psychosis between March 1998 and December 2014 was included, with follow-up to December 2022 and up to eight years of records after diagnosis. A total of 179 predictors were taken from first presentation — symptoms, sociodemographics, psychiatric comorbidity and psychotropic prescribing — and leave-one-trust-out nested cross-validation provided within-network external validation. Clozapine initiation within eight years served as the proxy for TR, modelled with penalised logistic regression.

Of 25,665 patients (mean age at onset 25.51, SD 5.54; 12,007 female, 46.8 per cent), 2,133 — 8.3 per cent — received clozapine within eight years. Discrimination across trusts reached a mean C statistic of 0.71 (95 per cent CI 0.68–0.75), with a positive predictive value of 0.15 (0.12–0.17) and a negative predictive value of 0.96 (0.95–0.97). Under a broader definition of TR — clozapine or three or more non-clozapine antipsychotics — prediction held but discrimination fell to 0.68 (0.64–0.72), while PPV rose to 0.27 (0.24–0.31) and NPV settled at 0.89 (0.88–0.91).

Limits: the outcome is a prescribing decision rather than a symptom scale, so it drifts with local habits; an 8.3 per cent base rate caps the positive predictive value almost by arithmetic; retrospective records carry missingness that varies by trust and by decade; and no trial has yet shown that earlier prediction produces earlier clozapine or better outcomes.

Why it mattersA PPV of 0.15 beside an NPV of 0.96 makes this a rule-out instrument, not an alarm: it can say with reasonable confidence that a patient probably will not end up on clozapine, and cannot say at the individual level that one will. Turning prediction into earlier treatment needs three things this study does not test — that flagged patients get reassessed sooner, that reassessment turns into an earlier prescription, and that the earlier prescription changes the course. On evidence, twenty-five thousand patients, ten trusts and leave-one-site-out validation are solid for a psychiatric prognostic model; the soft spots are the low base rate, the prescribing proxy and the absence of any interventional data. The cost lands on hospital IT and clinic workflow if such a model is embedded, while the benefit — earlier clozapine — lands on the patient, with a follow-up appointment and a prescription in between.

11/25 Journal of Nervous & Mental Disease (paper) ↗

18.4 per cent of US outpatient mental health visits are paid entirely out of pocket, against 9.5 per cent of other visits

The Journal of Nervous & Mental Disease ran the study in its November–December 2026 issue (volume 214, issue 6, pages 149–152) and posted it online ahead of print on November 25, 2026 (DOI 10.1097/NMD.0000000000001890). Gao YN and Olfson M are the authors, at the Department of Psychiatry in the Vagelos College of Physicians and Surgeons at Columbia University, the New York State Psychiatric Institute and the Department of Epidemiology at Columbia's Mailman School of Public Health.

The design is descriptive. Using a nationally representative sample of US outpatient visits from 2018 to 2021, the authors estimated the share of visits where insurance covered no part of the bill and the patient paid in full, comparing mental health visits with all others, then repeated the comparison at patient level and by insurance type.

Mental health visits came in at 18.4 per cent fully self-paid (95 per cent CI 15.9–20.8), against 9.5 per cent for non–mental health visits (9.0–10.1) — close to double. The patient-level gap is wider still: 46.8 per cent of privately insured people (44.9–48.7) had at least one fully self-paid mental health visit, compared with 26.7 per cent under Medicare (24.0–29.4), 17.8 per cent under Medicaid (15.4–20.2) and 39.5 per cent under other insurance (31.8–47.2).

Limits: visit-level billing and self-report cannot separate “in network with a high deductible” from “entirely out of network”, the window spans the pandemic and its shift in visit mix, and full self-payment is not the same as going untreated — it says only that this bill bypassed insurance. The authors note that despite recent legislation, coverage for mental health care and for other care can still diverge sharply for people with private insurance.

Why it mattersThe ordering is the counterintuitive part. Privately insured patients were the most likely to hit a fully self-paid mental health visit — close to half — while Medicaid sat at 17.8 per cent. The gap is therefore not about having coverage but about network depth and cost-sharing design: the thinner the psychiatric network, the more easily a visit slips outside insurance and onto the household's current cash. On evidence, a national probability sample with two levels of estimation and tight confidence intervals is descriptive work of the hard kind; what it cannot show is anyone skipping care because of the bill. The cost falls on the insured: premiums are paid either way, and the out-of-network portion comes out of pocket, so the same illness carries two prices in the same city depending on the card.

11/25 Nicotine & Tobacco Research (paper) ↗

A 137-person randomised trial: cash for quitting among homeless adults ends at 5.8 per cent against 4.4 per cent

Nicotine & Tobacco Research published the trial online on November 25, 2026 (DOI 10.1093/ntr/ntag246). Baggett TP is first author and Rigotti NA corresponding author, with affiliations including the Division of General Internal Medicine at Massachusetts General Hospital, Harvard Medical School, the Boston Health Care for the Homeless Program and the Department of Psychiatry at the University of Vermont.

The design is a mixed-methods randomised controlled trial. For 12 weeks the incentive arm received escalating payments of $25 to $70 each time a point-of-care saliva cotinine test came back negative; the control arm received $10 per visit regardless of result. Participants were adults in primary care at the Boston Health Care for the Homeless Program who smoked five or more cigarettes a day. Both arms were offered five tobacco coaching sessions and 12 weeks of varenicline. The primary and secondary outcomes were cotinine-verified 7-day point prevalence abstinence at 12 and 24 weeks.

Of 400 planned participants, 137 were randomised — 69 to incentives, 68 to control. Seven-day abstinence at 12 weeks was 4.4 per cent in the control arm and 5.8 per cent in the incentive arm (p = 0.71); at 24 weeks it was 4.4 per cent against 7.2 per cent (p = 0.48). Both arms cut daily cigarette consumption significantly from baseline at 12 and 24 weeks, with no difference between them.

The qualitative interviews (n = 17) locate the failure precisely. Participants described discouragement at how hard a negative cotinine test was to achieve, and resentment that cutting down earned nothing — the contract recognised only full abstinence.

Limits: the trial randomised a third of its target, so power is inadequate and the 5.8 against 4.4 per cent gap supports neither efficacy nor futility. Both arms received varenicline and coaching, so incentives were layered on active treatment. The outcome is point prevalence, not sustained abstinence, and the setting is a single programme in a single city.

Why it mattersThe real finding sits in the interviews: by making complete abstinence the only trigger, the design priced out the step most reachable in this population — going from twenty cigarettes to ten — and people who cut down without reward did not come back. On evidence, a randomised trial this under-recruited is feasibility data; with both arms near 5 per cent, pharmacological treatment plus coaching is already close to its ceiling here, which points at environment and comorbidity rather than motivation. The cost falls on the incentive budget itself: at up to $70 per visit for 12 weeks, money left unspent is the result, because a contract that pays only for abstinence generates almost no payments in this population. Paying for reduction would require rewriting the terms first.

11/25 JAMA Psychiatry (paper) ↗

Thirty animal studies: modulating SV2A moves kindled seizure severity by 2.16 standard deviations

JAMA Psychiatry published the meta-analysis online on November 25, 2026 (DOI 10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2026.3971). Blasco MB is first author and Mizrahi R corresponding author, at the Integrated Program in Neuroscience at McGill University, the Clinical and Translational Sciences Lab at the Douglas Research Centre, the Douglas Mental Health University Institute and McGill's Department of Psychiatry.

The underlying question is one of measurement. Synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2A (SV2A) is the tracer target most widely used as a PET proxy for synaptic density, and PET studies have consistently reported SV2A deficits across schizophrenia-related disorders. But SV2A also participates in calcium-dependent neurotransmitter release and may regulate the excitatory–inhibitory balance of a circuit, so a lower signal could mean fewer synapses or a circuit under strain.

The design is a systematic review and meta-analysis. EMBASE, Medline and PsycINFO were searched to December 30, 2025 for animal studies measuring SV2A expression or modulating it pharmacologically or genetically in kindling models — kindling being the experimental protocol in which repeated subthreshold stimulation produces lasting increases in excitability. The review was registered in PROSPERO, two reviewers handled selection, extraction and risk of bias with the SYRCLE tool, and random-effects models using restricted maximum likelihood pooled seizure severity and the odds of reaching kindling criteria, with meta-regression for moderators.

Thirty studies were included: seven measuring SV2A, two genetic models and 22 pharmacological modulation studies, with one study counted twice. Kindling in wild-type animals consistently raised hippocampal SV2A expression, mainly in GABAergic neurons, and genetic models with reduced SV2A function were more susceptible to kindling. SV2A-targeting interventions were associated with lower seizure severity (Hedge g = −2.16, 95 per cent CI −2.95 to −1.37, P < .001, I² = 82.3 per cent) and lower odds of reaching kindling criteria (odds ratio 0.04, 95 per cent CI 0.02–0.08, P < .001, I² = 0.4 per cent).

Limits: every study is animal work, and kindling is an experimental model rather than human epilepsy or psychosis. Heterogeneity on the severity outcome is high at I² = 82.3 per cent, so the pooled effect hides differences in species, model and dosing; the near-zero heterogeneity on the odds ratio cuts the other way, hinting that those studies share a protocol and possibly a bias. The result supports reading SV2A as an activity-regulated marker, not as a treatment target.

Why it mattersIf SV2A rises after repeated excitation as compensation, then a lower PET signal is not only fewer synapses but possibly failed compensation — the same number, two readings, and two different drug programmes behind them, one replacing synapses and one tuning excitability. On evidence, 30 animal studies with preregistration, duplicate screening and a risk-of-bias tool is well-conducted preclinical work; species and model heterogeneity are wide and there is no human data at all. The cost is already being paid inside industry: SV2A PET is a standard enrolment and stratification tool in central nervous system pipelines, and the price and availability of a scan decide whether it stays in research or becomes part of clinical triage.

11/25 PLoS One (paper) ↗

Sleep-time heart rate variability in 823 health workers moves with burnout and explains almost none of the variance

PLoS One published the study on November 25, 2026 (DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0359429). Pandya LS is first author and Mason AE corresponding author, with affiliations at the Osher Center for Integrative Health at the University of California, San Francisco, the Shu Chien-Gene Lay Department of Bioengineering at UC San Diego, the Department of Psychology at Yale and the Department of Psychiatry and Behavioral Sciences at UCSF.

The hypothesis being tested is already on the market: burnout is measured by self-report, so a passive physiological marker would give workplace interventions an objective outcome, and earlier studies pointing at low heart rate variability were small and cross-sectional. This one followed 823 health care workers longitudinally, pairing sleep-based HRV from a consumer wearable with the abbreviated Maslach Burnout Inventory and its three subdomains — emotional exhaustion (EE), depersonalisation (DP) and personal accomplishment (PA).

Data ran from April to December 2020, during the pandemic. Linear mixed-effects models adjusted for sex, age, weekday versus weekend and sleep length. The expected directions largely held: greater EE went with lower deep-sleep rMSSD (−0.417, 95 per cent CI −0.674 to −0.160, P = 0.001) and with lower REM rMSSD (−0.663, −0.977 to −0.348, P < .001) and HF-HRV (−1.594 × 10⁻⁴, −2.859 × 10⁻⁴ to −3.294 × 10⁻⁵, P = 0.013); lower PA went with lower deep-sleep rMSSD (−0.235, −0.440 to −0.030, P = 0.024).

Two findings refuse to cooperate. Greater depersonalisation tracked higher deep-sleep rMSSD (0.538, 0.285–0.792, P < .001) and higher REM rMSSD (0.369, 0.059–0.679, P = 0.020), the opposite of prediction, and the authors offer no mechanism. And the share of variance in HRV explained by burnout symptoms was very small, small effect sizes notwithstanding. Aggregated EE did climb 3.3 per cent in July and 6.1 per cent in September, but the absolute change between first and last measurement was negligible.

Limits, as the authors state them: an exploratory analysis with no preregistered primary outcome, one occupational group during one pandemic period, a device that is not research grade, and an unexplained reversed association for depersonalisation.

Why it mattersConsistent direction with negligible variance is the signature of a marker that differs on average between groups and cannot resolve one person: turn it into an employee screening programme and the false positives drown the signal. That also explains a commercial pattern — wearable makers prefer selling a stress score to selling a diagnosis, because a score needs no sensitivity or specificity. On evidence, a larger sample, a longitudinal design and adjustment for sleep length beat the earlier cross-sectional work; exploration, a single occupation and consumer hardware cap the claim, and the authors themselves ask for a preregistered replication with prespecified outcomes. The cost falls on the monitored: employers buy the devices, employees wear them, and this dataset says an individual reading is not yet enough to support a personnel decision.
