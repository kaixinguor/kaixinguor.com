11/21 07:15 UC San Diego (Newswise) ↗

Six weeks after the midterms, confidence that US elections are free and fair jumps from 49 to 66 per cent — Democrats 93, independents 66, Republicans down from 54 to 46

The University of California San Diego released the survey at 07:15 Beijing time on November 21, 2026. It comes from the university's Center for Transparent and Trusted Elections, co-directed by Thad Kousser, a professor of political science, and Lauren Prather, an associate professor in the School of Global Policy and Strategy. Fieldwork deliberately waited until November 9, nearly a week after the November 3 midterms, so that results and the first political reactions had time to settle.

The headline comparison is 66 per cent against 49 per cent. After the vote, 66 per cent of Americans said the midterms were free and fair; in September, when the same panel was asked whether they expected that, only 49 per cent said yes. September's wave had 5,016 respondents; 3,430 answered the post-election wave.

The recovery is entirely asymmetric by party. Confidence that votes nationwide were counted as intended reached 93 per cent among Democrats and 66 per cent among independents, while Republicans fell from 54 per cent before the election to 46 per cent after it. On the longer series, confidence stood at 77 per cent immediately after the 2024 presidential election and had fallen to 60 per cent by January 2026, with declines in all three party groups.

Other items in the survey: by a margin of 3 to 1, Americans want elections run primarily by state and local authorities rather than by a federal government with more power over administration; more than 70 per cent of Democrats, Republicans and independents support barring election officials and election workers from participating in prediction markets such as Kalshi or Polymarket, and large majorities would also bar candidates and campaign staff, though only a plurality would bar the general public; majorities in all three groups worry that AI-generated deepfakes affected the midterms.

What the design can and cannot support: this is a recontact panel, so it tracks the same people over time rather than comparing strangers, which is a real advantage over a single cross-section. It is still not randomised, so no single cause can be isolated, and “confidence” is a self-reported attitude, not a behaviour. The centre's work is partly funded by the Election Trust Initiative. Kousser attributes the rebound to wide margins, high turnout and losing candidates acknowledging results quickly. Prather points out that confidence usually rises for the winning side and falls for the losing one; here Republicans fell only modestly while Democrats and independents surged, which suggests how a result is communicated and accepted may matter as much as the result itself.

Why it mattersWhat changes here is the timescale attached to a trust crisis. Election confidence has been written as a slow decline, 77 per cent down to 60, which implies a slow remedy such as civic education. A move from 49 to 66 per cent in six weeks looks more like a fast-updating flow than a hard-to-reverse stock, and if that reading holds, the order of investment changes: fast counts, losing candidates who concede, and visible procedure buy more than another public campaign. The evidence is moderate to good — a within-person panel beats a cross-section, but it is observational, and nobody randomised who conceded when. The fact that Republicans moved down while everyone else moved up is the warning inside the number: party identity scores the same fact differently. The cost lands on election administrators, who are being asked to buy speed and transparency at the precise moment that deepfakes and prediction markets push new trust costs onto the information side, which no election office controls.
Another angle Follow the money to platforms and regulators: broad agreement that officials should not bet on elections, and broad worry about synthetic audio and video, suggests the public wants new tools fenced by procedure rather than another trust initiative.

11/20 University at Buffalo (EurekAlert) ↗

Three experiments, more than 1,000 people: framing a platform as serving one purpose makes people more willing to use it to reach an old friend

The University at Buffalo released the study on November 20, 2026. “When less is more: Unifinal framing promotes intention to use social media for social connection” appears in the Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, volume 128 (DOI 10.1016/j.jesp.2026.105013). David Lee, an associate professor of communication, is an author with Daniel Kulesza, a graduate student in the same department.

The team ran three experiments with more than 1,000 participants in total. The manipulation was light: some participants were led to think of a platform as serving one main purpose, others as serving several at once. The researchers then measured how appropriate the platform seemed as a place to reach out to a friend not spoken to in a while, and how willing and how likely participants said they were to do it.

The direction was consistent. When a platform was framed around a single purpose, it looked like a more fitting place to reconnect, and stated willingness and likelihood both rose. The authors explain this through ambiguity of meaning rather than feature overload: a place that simultaneously delivers news, shopping, entertainment and professional networking gives people no comfortable script for why they would do something private there.

What it does not show: all three are laboratory framing experiments with self-reported intentions as outcomes, not counts of messages actually sent. The figure of more than 1,000 is the total across three studies, each of which was smaller, and participants were mainly in the United States. Lee's design advice is specific — give reconnection its own visible entry point, such as a prompt to greet someone you have not spoken to, rather than burying it in the feed — and his advice to users is to give reconnection a dedicated moment.

Why it mattersLoneliness interventions assume that easier connection is better connection. This suggests the obstacle may be clarity of purpose rather than convenience: the more goals a tool carries, the harder it is for a user to justify using it for something personal. That changes the product question from “what else can we add” to “what is this for”. The evidence is middling: a lab manipulation with intention measures gives a credible direction but no magnitude and no durability, and convincing a platform to change an entry point needs a field experiment with messages sent and conversations held as outcomes, not willingness ratings. The cost sits with the platform, and it is small in engineering terms but awkward strategically, because a dedicated reconnection door runs against the logic of bundling everything.

11/20 Cell Reports (paper) ↗

302 postmortem brains, three regions: the methylation signature of PTSD scatters more between individuals than depression's, and sex, race and age at trauma each pull their own way

Cell Reports published the study on November 20, 2026. Frances Champagne of the University of Texas at Austin is corresponding author, and the work was supported by the National Institute of Mental Health. The team used 302 postmortem brains from the Lieber Institute for Brain Development, matched to donors' life histories and medical records so that diagnostic criteria could be assigned.

The groups were 101 people with PTSD, 100 people without psychiatric disorders and 101 people with major depressive disorder. For each brain, three regions were profiled for DNA methylation — the prefrontal cortex, the amygdala and the hippocampus. Methylation does not change the genetic sequence; it works like a dimmer switch on how much of a given stretch of code gets read.

Three findings stack up. First, methylation patterns varied more across brain regions in PTSD than in major depression. Second, they varied more between individuals with PTSD. Third, at the cellular level, people with PTSD showed a relative reduction of oligodendrocytes in the amygdala, particularly among those exposed to trauma in adulthood. PTSD was associated with more pronounced methylation differences in the prefrontal cortex, while depression was associated with them in the amygdala, and these molecular patterns tracked differences in the mix of cell types present.

Within the PTSD group, the molecular variation was driven by sex, by race, and by whether trauma exposure happened in childhood or adulthood. The qualifiers matter: postmortem tissue cannot establish whether methylation contributed to PTSD, resulted from it, or travelled alongside it, and the study lacked detailed information on symptom type and severity — precisely the information that individualised stratification would need.

Why it mattersThis unsettles the assumption that PTSD has one brain signature. If the molecular picture is mostly heterogeneity, then a single biomarker intended to screen everyone will probably fail, while stratification by sex, trauma timing and symptom dimension has a better chance — which bears directly on who gets enrolled in the next round of trials and helps explain why PTSD biomarker findings have been hard to replicate. The evidence is substantial for its class: 302 brains, three regions, two comparison groups. It is still observational, postmortem and without follow-up, and one step short of clinical use: someone has to find a measurable proxy in living people, whether through imaging or a signal accessible in peripheral tissue. The cost falls on biomarker companies and payers, because a test that works only for a subgroup has a very different business model from a test for everyone.

11/21 00:00 Wake Forest University School of Medicine (Newswise) ↗

1,470 primary care clinicians: 87.3 per cent recognised opioid misuse in a patient, 61.2 per cent recorded it — a 26-point gap inside the same practices

Wake Forest University School of Medicine released the survey at 11:00 US Eastern time on November 20, 2026 — midnight in Beijing on November 21 — and the paper appeared the same day in JAMA Health Forum. Robert W. Hurley, professor of pain medicine and anesthesiology and director of the pain outcomes lab, is corresponding author. The work was funded by the National Institutes of Health HEAL Initiative and the National Institute on Drug Abuse.

Fieldwork ran in April and May 2025 through a national online panel of 1,470 US primary care physicians, nurse practitioners and physician assistants who had prescribed opioids in the previous year. The questionnaire was refined with feedback from clinicians affiliated with the American Medical Association's Substance Use and Pain Care Task Force, participating in a personal capacity.

The core result is the distance between two self-reports from the same people: 87.3 per cent said they had identified opioid misuse in at least one patient in the previous 12 months, and 61.2 per cent said they had recorded it in a patient's medical record. That 26-point gap is not a difference between clinicians who notice and clinicians who do not; it sits inside the same practices, and it looked similar across physicians, nurse practitioners and physician assistants, and across practice settings.

Why leave it out? The most commonly cited concern, chosen by 38.5 per cent, was that recording misuse could lead a patient to be judged or treated differently, followed by time pressure and the difficulty of fitting documentation into the workflow. But clinicians who reported barriers were no more likely to have recognised misuse without documenting it than those who reported none. The authors also stress that misuse is a behaviour, whereas opioid use disorder is a diagnosis, and the two should not be conflated.

The limits: the study relies on clinicians' recollections rather than a review of records, so it cannot say how often misuse actually went unrecorded; an online panel may not represent all US primary care clinicians; and the survey may have missed other terms clinicians use to document misuse.

Why it mattersThe medical record is not only a clinical document; it is the substrate for surveillance and research. Misuse that is recognised and not written down does not exist for the next clinician who sees the patient, or for the public health systems that count on records. That reframes the problem: it is not that clinicians fail to notice, but that noticing does not become transferable information, so the fix is not another education session but cheaper documentation — structured fields, a short template — plus decoupling the act of recording from how a patient might then be treated. The evidence is moderate: self-report and cross-sectional, enough to establish that a gap exists, not enough to size it. The next step is to compare what clinicians say against what the electronic record actually contains. The cost lands on the next physician, who repeats the history, and on public health surveillance, which undercounts and therefore under-resources.

11/21 06:45 Rutgers University (Newswise) ↗

Healthy volunteers, two spelling tasks: an EEG cap plus a local language model nearly doubles sentence-building speed, and approaches triple when the model may rephrase

Rutgers University released the work at 17:45 US Eastern time on November 20, 2026 — 06:45 in Beijing on November 21 — and the paper appears in IEEE Transactions on Biomedical Engineering, volume 73, issue 8. Jiazhen Hong is first author; Laleh Najafizadeh, professor of electrical and computer engineering and director of the Integrated Systems and NeuroImaging Laboratory, led the research with Weinan Wang, and Pradyumna Rao is also an author.

The system is called ChatBCI-Assist. A user wears an EEG cap while rows and columns of a keyboard and phrase list flash rapidly on screen; when the row or column containing the intended choice flashes, the brain produces a P300 response about 300 milliseconds later, and the electrodes read it, letting the system infer the selection from the timing. What differs from a conventional speller is a fine-tuned language model running on the local machine, proposing words and phrases from what has already been chosen and from the conversation so far. The user can reject a suggestion and keep spelling letter by letter.

Performance was measured two ways. In copy spelling, where a sentence must be reproduced exactly, ChatBCI-Assist was nearly twice as fast as a conventional dictionary-based predictive system. In semantic spelling, where only the meaning has to be conveyed and the model may reword it, speed came close to three times the baseline. Participants also reported lower workload and found the intent-based mode easier to use than the dictionary-based one.

What the release does not support: every test was with healthy participants, with no patient data at all, and clinical testing with Shan Chen, a physician-scientist at the Rutgers ALS Multidisciplinary Clinic, is still being planned. The language model runs locally rather than through a cloud service, on the argument that messages about health, physical needs and private life should not leave the machine, and so that the system works without a reliable connection. Rutgers Technology Transfer has filed a provisional patent application. The paper is peer reviewed, but the release gives neither participant numbers nor effect sizes.

Background worth keeping in view: people with advanced ALS usually retain much of their cognition while losing motor control, and eye-gaze devices are the common route — a specialised camera tracks eye movements, and the device becomes hard to use once reliable eye control goes.

Why it mattersThe real shift is not speed but the endpoint. Treating communication rate, rather than spelling accuracy, as the clinical outcome changes what counts as success. Intent-level input finishes a sentence with fewer selections, at the price of not saying the exact words, and in situations such as consent to treatment or a legal statement that price has to be paid with an auditable confirmation step, or the speed is bought with blurred responsibility. The evidence is thin: healthy participants, laboratory tasks, no sample size, no effect sizes, no patients. Getting from two or three times faster to usable in daily life needs field work on fatigue, the cost of errors and training burden. Who pays is also unanswered — family out of pocket, insurance, or an assistive-technology programme — and that, more than accuracy, will decide whether this replaces eye gaze or sits beside it.
