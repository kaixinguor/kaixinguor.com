10 项研究、3 万余名罪犯的数据：被认为出于道德动机的犯罪，对惩罚的反应更弱

《PNAS》2026 年 11 月 16 日在线发表（DOI 10.1073/pnas.2624057123），作者是加州大学圣迭戈分校 Rady 管理学院的博士生 Raihan Alam 与管理学助理教授、心理学家 Tage Rai。这组工作一共 10 项研究，核心主张是：当人出于「纠正一件错事」的道德感去违法，惩罚的威慑力会比出于物质利益时更弱。

第一层证据来自联邦犯罪数据：样本为 3 万余名罪犯、29 类犯罪。研究者比较的是各类犯罪「被认为有多强的道德动机」与再逮捕率的关系——道德动机评分越高的犯罪，再逮捕率越高；物质动机评分越高的，再逮捕率越低。这里有一处必须写清的口径：动机评分来自研究参与者对各类犯罪动机的评估，不是罪犯本人的陈述，所以这一层只能算相关。

第二层是实验：422 名参与者想象自己为支持某个信念而堵路，或为拿钱而堵路，前者对后果的顾虑更少；换成罚金、人身伤害或社会谴责，模式不变。第三层是动真钱的经济游戏：一人故意独吞或经随机分配拿走全部奖金，另一人可以冒着被罚钱的风险把奖金重新平分。在没有罚金时，两种情形下平分的意愿差不多；一旦可能被罚，参与者去纠正「故意独吞」的意愿比纠正「随机分配」高约 20 个百分点。

还有一个容易被忽略的对照：作者把核心研究也在有轻罪或重罪前科的人群中做了一遍，结果与一般人群相似。他们据此认为，惩罚失效的原因不太可能是「罪犯不会算账」，而是我们对动机的假设错了。研究部分由 Alam 的美国国家科学基金会研究生奖学金资助；作者提到修复性司法可能是一条路，但明确说这项研究没有直接检验它。

看点这条真正改变的是一个默认解释：惩罚没用，过去常被归因于冲动、自控差或不会权衡后果；这里给出的替代解释是「道德动机根本不进那张成本收益表」。判断它站不站得住，要看三层证据的强弱——3 万人的部分靠外人对动机的评分，是相关；422 人及后续实验是想象情境，外部效度有限；动真钱的经济游戏效应最硬，但金额小、场景单一。三点实践含义：其一，对这类犯罪继续加刑期，边际威慑可能接近零，作者自己说得很清楚，「要报应就罚，要改行为就未必」；其二，筛查与干预该针对动机（羞辱、报复、维权），而不是笼统加码；其三，前科人群结果相似，说明问题不在「罪犯这一群人有什么缺陷」，这条对去污名化的政策讨论比效应量更有用。缺口也明确：10 项研究里没有一项是真实的量刑实验，修复性司法只是假设。

11/16 Princeton University（EurekAlert） ↗

同一批公告牌歌曲唤起的自传体记忆，在人与人之间比预想的更像：题材与年代能预测你想起什么

《PNAS》2026 年 11 月 16 日在线发表（DOI 10.1073/pnas.2536885123），作者为普林斯顿大学研究音乐与记忆的团队。方法是给参与者播放 30 秒的公告牌热门歌曲片段，题材覆盖流行、乡村与嘻哈，年代分两组：参与者自己的青少年时期，或他们父母青少年时期。听完让参与者描述被唤起的记忆，再用自然语言处理工具分析 1,000 多条记忆描述。

结果：同一题材或同一时期的歌，在不同听者身上唤起的记忆更相似——记忆描述按题材与年代聚集。为了排除「是歌词在起作用」，团队又做了两项研究：一项用代表歌词的单词做提示，另一项用歌词的故事梗概做提示，两种提示唤起的记忆都没有出现同样的聚集。

作者的解释落在「脚本」上。记忆研究通常把个体自传体记忆与集体记忆（对 9/11 这类公共事件的共同回忆）分开，音乐提示的是被忽略的中间层：日常的、感觉很私人的记忆，其实由文化习得的脚本塑造——暑假公路旅行、家庭婚礼，以及「什么音乐属于那种场合」。脚本在记忆形成时起作用，在提取时也起作用；即使是没听过的歌，单靠题材也可能唤起熟悉的脚本，于是不同的人报告出相似的记忆。

口径要一并读：新闻稿没有给出参与者总人数、效应量和记忆编码的具体判据，也没有说明样本来自哪些文化背景。测量对象是听者写下的记忆文本，不是记忆准确性或生动性的客观测验，所以「相似」是由文本相似度定义的，不等于记忆内容真的相同。

看点这条最有用的部分在方法，不在结论。「私人的记忆」可以被题材与年代预测，意味着自传体记忆的测量里混进了一个长期没被控制的变量：文化脚本。三个直接后果——用音乐做线索的怀旧疗法与生命回顾，被唤起的可能有一部分是脚本而不是个人经历，这会系统性地把「共同记忆」误当成「这个人的记忆」；用音乐做记忆线索的试验需要按年代与题材分层，否则组间差异可能只是选曲差异；反过来，如果脚本真的在编码与提取两端都工作，它也能被利用：给创伤记忆找一条不同的脚本，可能比反复提取更容易改写法。要给这条结论定级，需要的是同一批人、同一批歌、跨文化样本，加上对记忆内容的人工编码（而不是只看文本相似度）——目前都没有。

222 人的 3 期试验：氘代赛洛新辅助治疗抑郁，6 周 MADRS 多降 7.6 分、效应量 0.65

Helus Pharma 于 2026 年 11 月 16 日公布 3 期 APPROACH 试验的 topline 结果，同日 Psychiatric Times 做了报道。APPROACH 是多中心、随机、平行分组、双盲、安慰剂对照的辅助治疗研究：意向治疗人群 222 名成人，符合 DSM-5 重性抑郁障碍、筛查与基线 MADRS 总分均不低于 24，基线分别为 32.7 与 33.8；受试者全程维持原有抗抑郁药的稳定剂量。给药方式为两次口服、间隔三周、在监督下进行。

主要终点在第 6 周：HLP003 组 MADRS 最小二乘均值下降 19.5 分，安慰剂组 11.9 分，差值 −7.6（P<0.0001，效应量 0.653）。起效也快——第 2 天就已分开，下降 16.1 对 11.0 分，差值 −5.0（P=0.0013，效应量 0.438）。关键次要终点第 12 周维持在 16.9 对 9.9 分（差值 −7.0，P<0.0001，效应量 0.581）。临床总体印象量表从第 2 天起优于安慰剂。

安全性方面，多数不良事件为轻中度、短暂，且集中在给药当天；公司称共 3 例与 HLP003 相关的严重不良事件，未发现新的安全信号。据 pharmaphorum 报道，其中一例是第二次给药 55 天后的自杀未遂，该受试者对药物无应答，当时正在服用带自杀警示的背景抗抑郁药。涉及自杀风险时，中国大陆可拨 12356 心理援助热线。

节奏上，APPROACH 是 PARADIGM 3 期项目的第一个试验，第二个关键试验 EMBRACE 预计 2027 年第四季度读出 topline，长期延伸研究 EXTEND 正在进行；公司希望 2028 年提交上市申请。目前公布的只是新闻稿口径的 topline，同行评议论文、完整安全性数据与开放标签数据都还没有。

看点−7.6 分、效应量 0.65 放在辅助治疗里属于大效应：现在这条路主要用非典型抗精神病药，效应通常更小，还要付体重增加与代谢紊乱的代价，所以这个数字如果站得住，门诊路径会真的变。但有三处要盯住。其一，12 周效应量降到 0.58，方向没变，强度在衰减，长期能不能维持要看 EXTEND。其二，给药当天的不良事件加上 3 例严重不良事件，决定了这种治疗必须有观察空间与陪护，成本结构更像「有监护的门诊给药」而不是「回家吃药」——对支付方，问题不是有没有效，是每次给药占多少人力与场地。其三，受试者是「对现有抗抑郁药反应不足」的人，基线 MADRS 33 分属于中重度，这个人群不能直接推到首诊患者。要判断它能不能改指南，缺的是独立复现（EMBRACE）、以缓解率和功能为结局的报告，以及和现有辅助药物做头的对比——目前一样都没有。

APA 发布职场 AI 七条实践：适用员工中不到 40% 知道公司有没有 AI 使用政策

美国心理学会（APA）2026 年 11 月 16 日发布《Deploying AI Into Workplaces: What Leaders Need to Know》，给出 7 条据称基于既有心理学研究的实践，用于组织评估与引入 AI 工具。指南由一个人机交互与临床、社会、法律、认知、工业与组织心理学专家组成的顾问小组参与制定，APA 强调它不判断组织该不该用 AI，只帮选择引入的组织做得更审慎。

报告引用的关键数字来自 APA 今年早前发布的 2026 年《Work in America》调查：在「该问题适用」的员工中，不到 40% 知道雇主是否有关于可接受 AI 使用的正式政策；同时，报告称感到雇主施压要求在工作中使用 AI 的员工比例在上升。APA 把这种落差称为「采用与政策的错配」。

七条里被点名的三条是：按潜在错误的代价匹配保障措施，并预想后果与情景；以公平的程序引入，包括咨询将要使用系统的人，以及受系统输出影响的人；保护人们发展深度专长、分享知识与从经验中学习的机会。APA 执行首席心理学家 Dennis P. Stolle 的原话是，「买一个 AI 工具然后叫大家用，不是稳妥的策略」，组织需要投资「将与技术共事的人」，而不只是技术本身。

口径：这是专业协会的实践指南，不是效果研究——没有干预试验、没有效应量、也没有对七条实践的独立评估；它的证据基础是 APA 自己的调查与既有文献。APA 的立场是，AI 带来的信任与判断问题「本质上是心理学问题」。

看点把「AI 采用」从采购问题改写成工作设计问题，是这份指南的真正动作，落点是三件可核查的事：谁被咨询、谁能保留专长、出错代价由谁承担。对组织心理学的读者，空白在测量——「不到 40% 知道政策」是自报，分母只算「该问题适用」的员工，既没有说清「知道」的判断标准，也没有分行业。要判断七条有没有用，需要的是引入前后的纵向数据：信任、离职意向、错误率与返工时间；这些数据现在不存在，指南也无法替代它们。对临床与 EAP 这一侧，指南没有覆盖的一层是：员工对 AI 的焦虑会先出现在咨询室里——「我用 AI 写了这封邮件，该不该说」——这类问题既不进政策，也不进培训，最后落在个体身上。

11/16 Lumos Labs（EurekAlert） ↗

801 人的公司试验：12 周 Lumosity 训练在 12 项认知测验上进步更大，数据以会议摘要形式发布

Lumos Labs 2026 年 11 月 16 日公布 BRIGHT-Mind 研究结果，并称将在 11 月 18 日波士顿举行的第 19 届阿尔茨海默病临床试验会议（CTAD）上报告。试验纳入 801 名 25 至 80 岁健康成人，居家训练 12 周，每天 15 至 25 分钟、每周至少 5 天；对照组同样每天花时间看有教育性的视频，属于主动对照，用来排除「只是更投入」或「期待效应」。

主要分析纳入 677 人。训练组在一套 12 项、参与者没练过的认知测验合成分上进步更大（p=0.00032）；公司用「大约相当于 6.7 岁年龄差带来的分数差距」来描述差异大小，并称各年龄段效果相似。全部随机到 Lumosity 的人里，83.6% 完成了 12 周内至少 13 小时的训练。

次要结局：「患者总体印象改善」（PGI-I）自评改善比例 70%，对照组 46%（p<0.0001）；Sheehan 功能损害量表上，训练组在包含工作或学业、社交生活与家庭责任的总体指标上改善更多（p=0.00080）。MRI 子研究有 220 名可评估者，在 60 至 80 岁组，训练组的「脑龄差」比对照组多改善约 1.5 年，脑室扩大更少，前扣带、中颞回与前额皮质的皮层厚度保持得更好。两组都没有报告相关不良事件。

口径必须写明：试验由厂商设计、执行，公司 CEO 是通讯作者；结果以会议摘要与公司新闻稿形式发布，没有同行评议论文；主要结局用 p 值与「年龄当量」表达，没有给出以标准差为单位的组间差与置信区间，也没有随访数据说明停练之后还剩多少。

看点这项设计比多数脑训练研究讲究：有主动对照、用没练过的测验、800 人、12 周——所以它值得认真读，而不是当成广告跳过。但三个数字要先对齐：677/801 才进主分析，完成度与排除规则没公布，而「每天 20 分钟、每周 5 天、12 周」在真实用户里是极高的门槛（83.6% 完成 13 小时说明的是被随机进来的人，不是买产品的人）；p=0.00032 说的是「差异不是零」，不是「差多少」；「6.7 岁」是把测验分换算成年龄的类比，读起来直观，却不等于临床或生活功能上的意义。下一步需要的四样东西很具体：以标准差为单位的组间差、停练后的随访（6 至 12 个月后迁移是否维持）、独立复现，以及真实依从性数据。对雇主与支付方，真正的报销问题不是「练了有没有用」，而是「停下来会怎样、谁会坚持到 12 周」。

社会与行为
11/16 University of California San Diego (EurekAlert) ↗

Ten studies and 30,000 offenders: crimes seen as morally motivated respond less to punishment

The work appeared online in PNAS on November 16, 2026 (DOI 10.1073/pnas.2624057123), by Raihan Alam, a doctoral student at UC San Diego's Rady School of Management, and Tage Rai, a psychologist and assistant professor of management there. Across ten studies the claim is narrow: when people break the law out of a sense that they are righting a wrong, punishment deters them less than when they are chasing material gain.

The first layer is federal crime data: more than 30,000 offenders across 29 offence categories. The researchers related how morally motivated each category was rated to be with rearrest rates — crimes rated as more morally motivated had higher rearrest rates, those rated as more materially motivated had lower ones. One qualifier matters: the motive ratings came from study participants' assessments of offence categories, not from offenders' own accounts, so this layer is correlational.

The second layer is experimental. In one study, 422 people imagined blocking traffic either for a cause they believed in or in exchange for money; those acting for a cause were less deterred, and the pattern held whether the consequence was a fine, physical harm or social disapproval. The third layer used real money. One player ended up with the whole pot, either deliberately or by random allocation, and a second player could redistribute it evenly at the risk of a penalty. Without a possible penalty, willingness to redistribute barely differed; with one, participants were about 20 percentage points more likely to act when the unequal split had been deliberate.

One control is easy to miss: the core studies were also run with participants who had prior misdemeanour or felony convictions, and the results looked much like those from the general population. The authors read that as evidence against the usual explanation — that offenders simply fail to weigh consequences — and in favour of a wrong assumption about motives. The work was funded in part by a National Science Foundation Graduate Research Fellowship to Alam. The authors raise restorative justice as a possible route but state plainly that their studies did not test it.

Why it mattersWhat this displaces is a default explanation. When punishment fails, the fault has usually been placed on impulsivity, poor self-control or an inability to weigh outcomes; the alternative offered here is that moral motive never enters the cost-benefit calculation at all. Whether it holds depends on reading the three layers separately: the 30,000-offender analysis rests on outside ratings of motive and is correlational; the scenario experiments are hypothetical and limited in external validity; the economic game is the hardest evidence, but the stakes are small and the setting narrow. Three practical consequences follow. If the goal is retribution, punishment works, as the authors themselves say; if the goal is changing behaviour, added prison time may buy close to nothing for this class of offence. Screening and intervention should target the motive — humiliation, retaliation, defending a cause — rather than sentence length. And because participants with prior convictions behaved like everyone else, the problem is not a defect in "that kind of person", which matters more for stigma than for effect size. The gap is equally clear: none of the ten studies is a real sentencing experiment, and restorative justice remains a hypothesis.

11/16 Princeton University (EurekAlert) ↗

The memories a hit song brings back are more alike across listeners than we assume, and genre predicts which ones

The study appeared online in PNAS on November 16, 2026 (DOI 10.1073/pnas.2536885123), from a Princeton team that studies music and memory. Participants heard 30-second clips of Billboard-topping pop, country and hip-hop, drawn either from their own teenage years or from their parents' teenage years, and were asked to describe the memories the clips brought up. The team then ran natural language processing over more than 1,000 of those descriptions.

Songs from the same genre or the same period reliably evoked similar memories: the descriptions clustered by genre and decade. To rule out the lyrics as the driver, the researchers ran two further studies, cueing one group with single words representative of each song's lyrics and another with summaries of the lyrics' story. Neither cue produced the same clustering.

The explanation the authors offer is scripts. Memory research usually separates individual autobiographical memory from collective memory — the shared recollection of public events such as 9/11 — and music, they argue, exposes a neglected middle ground. Everyday memories that feel intensely private are shaped by culturally learned expectations about what happens in particular settings, such as a summer road trip or a family wedding, and about which music belongs there. Scripts operate when a memory is formed and again when it is retrieved; even an unfamiliar song may summon a familiar script through genre alone, which is why different people report similar memories.

Read the qualifying details alongside. The release gives no total sample size, no effect size and no coding criteria for the memory descriptions, and says nothing about which cultures the listeners came from. What was measured is text that listeners wrote, not accuracy or vividness on an objective memory test, so "similar" is defined by textual similarity and does not mean the remembered content was the same.

Why it mattersThe useful part here is methodological, not the conclusion. If private-feeling memory can be predicted from genre and decade, then autobiographical memory measurement carries a variable that has rarely been controlled: cultural scripts. Three consequences follow. Reminiscence therapy and life review that use music as a cue may be eliciting part of a script rather than a personal episode, which systematically mistakes shared memory for this person's memory. Trials using music cues need to stratify by era and genre, or a between-group difference may just be a difference in the playlist. And if scripts work at both encoding and retrieval, they can also be used: giving a distressing memory a different script may be easier than repeatedly retrieving it. Settling the claim needs the same people hearing the same songs across cultures, plus human coding of memory content rather than textual similarity alone. None of that exists yet.

A 222-patient phase 3: adjunctive deuterated psilocin cut MADRS by 7.6 points more than placebo at six weeks, effect size 0.65

Helus Pharma released topline results from the phase 3 APPROACH trial on November 16, 2026, and Psychiatric Times reported them the same day. APPROACH was multicentre, randomised, parallel-group, double-blind and placebo-controlled, testing HLP003 as an add-on: 222 adults in the intention-to-treat population with DSM-5 major depressive disorder, a MADRS total of at least 24 at screening and baseline, and mean baseline scores of 32.7 and 33.8. Everyone stayed on a stable dose of their existing antidepressant throughout, and dosing was two supervised oral sessions three weeks apart.

At the six-week primary endpoint, the least-squares mean MADRS reduction was 19.5 points on HLP003 against 11.9 on placebo — a difference of −7.6 (P<0.0001, effect size 0.653). Separation came early: by day 2 the reduction was 16.1 against 11.0, a difference of −5.0 (P=0.0013, effect size 0.438). At the 12-week key secondary endpoint it held at 16.9 against 9.9 (difference −7.0, P<0.0001, effect size 0.581), and Clinical Global Impression scores favoured the drug from day 2 onward.

On safety, most adverse events were mild to moderate, transient and confined to the day of dosing. The company reports three HLP003-related serious adverse events and no new safety signals. As pharmaphorum reported, one of those was a suicide attempt 55 days after the second dose, in a participant who had not responded to the drug and was taking a background antidepressant carrying a suicidality warning. In mainland China, the 12356 mental health hotline is available for anyone at risk.

On timing, APPROACH is the first of three studies in the PARADIGM programme. A second pivotal trial, EMBRACE, is expected to report topline results in the fourth quarter of 2027, a long-term extension called EXTEND is running, and the company has said it hopes to file for approval in 2028. What exists publicly today is a press-release topline: no peer-reviewed paper, no full safety dataset, no open-label data.

Why it mattersA difference of −7.6 points with an effect size of 0.65 is large for an adjunctive treatment. The current route is mainly atypical antipsychotics, which tend to deliver less and charge for it in weight gain and metabolic risk, so if this holds the clinic pathway really does change. Three things to watch. First, the effect size falls to 0.58 at 12 weeks — same direction, less force — and durability beyond that depends on EXTEND. Second, dosing-day adverse events plus three serious events mean the treatment needs observation space and a companion, which makes the cost structure closer to supervised outpatient dosing than to a pill at home; for a payer the question is not whether it works but how much staff and floor space each dose consumes. Third, the participants were people with an inadequate response to their current antidepressant and a baseline MADRS in the mid-30s, a population that does not map onto first-episode patients. To change a guideline it still needs independent replication in EMBRACE, remission and functioning as outcomes, and a head-to-head against existing adjuncts. None of those is available yet.

11/16 American Psychological Association (EurekAlert) ↗

APA publishes seven practices for AI at work, as fewer than 40% of affected workers know whether a policy exists

The American Psychological Association released Deploying AI Into Workplaces: What Leaders Need to Know on November 16, 2026, setting out seven practices it describes as grounded in established psychological research and emerging evidence on AI. The guide was developed with an advisory panel drawn from human-technology interaction and from clinical, social, legal, cognitive and industrial-organisational psychology. APA is explicit that it does not tell organisations whether to adopt AI, only how to do so more deliberately if they choose to.

The headline number comes from APA's own 2026 Work in America survey, published earlier this year: among workers for whom the question applied, fewer than 40% knew whether their employer had an official policy on acceptable AI use. At the same time, the share reporting pressure from their employer to use AI at work is rising. APA calls this the adoption-policy mismatch.

Three of the seven practices are named in the release: match safeguards to the stakes of a potential error and think through consequences and scenarios; introduce AI through a fair process, consulting both the people who will use the system and those affected by its outputs; and protect opportunities to build deep expertise, share knowledge and learn from experience. "Purchasing an AI tool and telling people to use it without further direction is not a sound strategy," said Dennis P. Stolle, APA's executive lead psychologist for applied psychology. Organisations, he said, need to invest in the people who will work with the technology, not just the technology.

Read it as what it is: a practice guide from a professional body, not an effectiveness study. There is no intervention trial, no effect size and no independent evaluation of the seven practices; the evidence base is APA's own survey plus existing literature. APA's framing is that the trust and judgement questions raised by AI are, at bottom, questions of psychology.

Why it mattersThe real move is reclassifying AI adoption from a procurement problem into a work-design problem, which turns on three checkable things: who was consulted, who gets to keep expertise, and who absorbs the cost of an error. For organisational psychologists the gap is in measurement. "Fewer than 40% know the policy" is self-report, its denominator is only workers for whom the question applied, and neither the criterion for knowing nor any industry breakdown is given. Judging whether the seven practices work requires longitudinal data — trust, turnover intention, error rates, rework time — before and after adoption, and no such data exist; a guide cannot substitute for them. One layer the guide does not cover sits in the clinic and the EAP: the anxiety shows up first in a consulting room — "I used AI to write this email, should I say so?" — a question that enters neither policy nor training and lands on the individual.

11/16 Lumos Labs (EurekAlert) ↗

An 801-person company trial reports 12 weeks of Lumosity training beating an active control on a 12-test cognitive battery

Lumos Labs released results from BRIGHT-Mind on November 16, 2026, and says it will present them at the 19th Clinical Trials on Alzheimer's Disease conference in Boston on November 18. The trial enrolled 801 healthy adults aged 25 to 80 who trained at home for 12 weeks, 15 to 25 minutes a day and at least five days a week. The comparison group spent comparable time on educational videos — an active control, meant to absorb general engagement and expectancy.

The primary analysis covered 677 participants. The training group improved more on a composite of 12 cognitive tests participants had not practised (p=0.00032), a gap the company describes as about the size of the difference in scores between people roughly 6.7 years apart in age; the benefit was described as similar across ages. Among everyone randomised to Lumosity, 83.6% completed at least 13 hours of training over the 12 weeks.

On secondary outcomes, 70% of the Lumosity group reported improvement in concentration, memory and clear thinking on the Patient Global Impression of Improvement, against 46% of the comparison group (p<0.0001), and the Lumosity group reported greater cognition-related functional improvement on the Sheehan Disability Scale across work or school, social life and home responsibilities (p=0.00080). An MRI substudy with 220 evaluable participants found that in the 60-to-80 group the brain-predicted age difference moved about 1.5 years more favourably with training, with less ventricular enlargement and better-maintained cortical thickness in the anterior cingulate, middle temporal gyrus and prefrontal cortex. Neither group reported related adverse events.

The qualifiers belong in the same breath. The trial was designed and run by the vendor, its CEO is corresponding author, and the results were released as a conference abstract and press release with no peer-reviewed paper. The primary result is expressed as a p value and an age equivalence, without a standardised between-group difference in standard deviation units, without a confidence interval, and without follow-up telling us what remains after training stops.

Why it mattersThis design is better than most brain-training studies — active control, untrained tests, 800 people, 12 weeks — so it deserves a careful read rather than a reflex dismissal as marketing. But three numbers have to be aligned first. Only 677 of 801 entered the primary analysis, and neither the completion figures nor the exclusion rules are public, while "20 minutes a day, five days a week, for 12 weeks" is a very high bar for real users: the 83.6% who completed 13 hours are people who were randomised into a study, not people who bought a product. The p value says the difference is not zero, not how large it is. And "6.7 years" is a conversion of test scores into an age analogy — intuitive to read, not a statement about clinical or everyday function. Four things would settle it: a between-group difference in standard deviation units, a follow-up at 6 to 12 months to see whether transfer persists, an independent replication, and real-world adherence data. For employers and payers the reimbursement question is not whether training works but what happens when it stops, and who makes it to 12 weeks.
