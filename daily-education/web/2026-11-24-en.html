Policy & Regulation

政策与监管
11/23 16:46 China Education Daily ↗

Beijing publishes a draft textbook regulation: eight chapters, 67 articles, and a legal regime for the book in every classroom

At 16:46 on November 23 the Ministry of Education posted a notice seeking public comment on a draft Textbook Management Regulation, with a deadline of December 23, 2026. Comments go by post to the ministry's legal affairs department in Xicheng district or by email to jcfz@moe.edu.cn, marked "Textbook Management Regulation consultation". China Education Daily carried the notice on page one of its November 24 edition and People's Daily on page four the same day. The draft runs to eight chapters and 67 articles, with an accompanying explanatory note.

It defines a textbook as "a teaching book written around talent-training objectives, reviewed and published under prescribed procedures and used by universities, secondary and primary schools", and brings seven activities under the regime: planning, writing, review, publishing, distribution, selection and use. A National Textbook Committee under the State Council steers the whole field, and provincial governments set up their own coordinating bodies. Vocational colleges and universities "shall establish a school textbook work leadership body and designate a dedicated department" responsible for planning, writing, review, selection and use (Article 10). Planning is layered to match: the national committee coordinates the national plan, the ministry drafts plans for school, special, vocational and university core courses, provinces draft regional plans and universities draft their own (Article 7).

Three articles touch money. Article 27 sets textbook prices at cost plus a thin margin, with primary and secondary prices fixed by the provincial price authority together with the publishing regulator. Article 26 requires primary and secondary textbooks to be printed to a higher standard than ordinary publications. Article 41 makes the state the provider of free textbooks in compulsory education and encourages free supplementary teaching resources. Article 29 bans commercial advertising, disguised advertising, bundled workbooks and other goods in the publishing, printing and distribution chain, and Article 31 requires primary and secondary textbooks to reach schools before term starts.

Quality control and exit are the new hard edges. Article 36 orders education departments at every level to build a monitoring system, with schools, publishers and research institutes tracking quality and classroom effect, and makes the findings a reference for revision, selection and awards. Article 38 creates the exit: a title with political or value problems is suspended immediately, and one that is poor in quality, slow to update or badly rated by teachers and students "is no longer included in the selection scope". Article 47 requires the ministry to build a textbook database and publishers to report data within 60 days of publication. Training and selection are also pinned down: textbook-use training must be written into teacher training plans (Article 35), and selection results must be publicised and filed (Article 33).

The penalties are specific. A textbook containing prohibited content brings the editor-in-chief a warning and a fine of RMB 50,000 to 100,000, with a possible ban from textbook work; writing and review violations bring confiscation of illegal gains plus a fine of five to ten times those gains; publishing unreviewed textbooks or publishing primary and secondary titles beyond one's licence brings suspension, withdrawal from sale and recall, with the same five-to-ten-times multiplier above RMB 10,000 of turnover; unlicensed publishing carries a fine of up to RMB 250,000 where gains are under RMB 50,000. The commencement date is left as a blank placeholder.

LookThree prices move here. The first is the price of the book: cost-plus pricing, provincial price-setting and free provision in compulsory education together turn the school textbook into a publicly priced, publicly purchased good, so publishers compete on catalogue and support services rather than margin. The second is the nature of "selection": Article 38 converts selection from a one-off approval into continuous assessment, and a backlist title becomes a liability that must be maintained every year — maintenance paid for either inside a capped price or by the provincial budget that buys the free copies. The third is the school's own exposure: vocational colleges and universities must staff a standing textbook body, publicise selection results and absorb training into teacher development, none of which comes with new posts, so the cost lands on registry offices and on teachers' continuing-education hours. The most underrated clause is Article 47. A 60-day data filing requirement builds a traceable record for every title; without it, the monitoring in Article 36 and the exit in Article 38 have nothing to run on.
Another angleArticle 2 limits a textbook to a "teaching book", and no chapter addresses digital textbooks; free digital material appears only under "supplementary teaching resources" in Article 41. For educational publishers that leaves digital products classified as supplements, outside both the review channel and the free-provision budget line. Whoever first gets digital content recognised as part of a textbook will be the one with a budget heading to sell into.

11/23 20:47 gov.cn ↗

Four ministries let kindergartens enrol two-year-olds — but only where three-to-six places are already covered

At 20:47 on November 23 the State Council website carried a CCTV report on a joint plan from the National Development and Reform Commission, the Ministry of Civil Affairs, the National Health Commission and the Ministry of Education. The "elderly and children" service-system plan (Fagai Shehui [2026] No. 1623) has State Council approval, is dated November 9, 2026, and is addressed to provincial governments.

The 2030 targets are numeric: nursing-care beds to reach 73% of elderly-care capacity, community elderly-care facilities to cover 70% of the population, and the enrolment rate for children under three to rise six percentage points over 2025. On the elderly side the plan also targets 2,000 publicly run facilities built long ago and now dilapidated for renovation and upgrading.

The section that matters for schools is the childcare network. Prefecture-level centres and centres in populous counties are to be built out until prefecture-level coverage is complete. Kindergartens with the capacity are to be renovated and extended to run classes for children aged two to three. A "15-minute childcare circle" is to be laid out, providers are to be encouraged to reach rural areas with childcare points in larger villages, and employers are encouraged to run childcare on their own sites with professional partners. "Combined elderly-and-childcare" models are permitted where safety allows.

Money and supervision are both specified. Kindergarten classes that qualify are to be included in the affordable-childcare subsidy, tax and utility-price preferences apply, and local governments are encouraged to offer stable operating subsidies; funding channels are the central budget, local resources and local government special bonds. Article 11 divides supervision: "on the condition that places for children aged three to six are secured", education departments take charge of planning and of vetting the conditions under which a class opens, while health departments set service standards and staff qualifications and handle filing and professional guidance. On staffing, the plan calls for stronger disciplines in elderly and childcare studies, a training system for nursery teachers and infant-development guides, a clear route for professional titles, and practical training partnerships between vocational colleges and care providers.

LookWhat actually changes is a kindergarten's right to enrol downwards, and that right comes with two conditions attached. The first is the guarantee that three-to-six-year-old places are protected, which makes the policy school by school: a city or township kindergarten losing pupils can fill empty rooms with two-year-olds, while one that is still oversubscribed cannot. For cities short of kindergarten places it adds almost nothing; for districts with falling rolls it is the remedy. The second is dual supervision: education departments control siting and opening conditions, health departments control standards, qualifications and filing, so one class sits in two registries. The budget line changes too — subsidy money shifts, in places, from pre-school funding to the affordable-childcare subsidy, which means two applications and two reporting chains. The real cost lands on staffing: ratios for two-year-olds are tighter than for three-to-six, nursery teachers are the binding constraint, and the plan's answer is vocational training partnerships. A number worth watching: a six-percentage-point rise in the under-three enrolment rate is a hard target, but the 2025 baseline is not in the published summary, so the actual number of new places will only appear in provincial plans.

Vocational & Careers

职教与就业
11/23 18:52 Xinhua ↗

A data-sector teaching committee takes shape: seven programmes, and a 2030 target for the AI-era skills pipeline

At 18:52 on November 23 Xinhua published a Beijing dispatch by reporter Gao Kang: a national vocational education teaching committee for the data sector has been set up in Beijing and has held its first plenary session, according to the National Data Administration. The session approved the committee's charter, its 2026-2030 development plan and its priorities for 2026-27.

Its work is organised into seven programmes: reform of key teaching elements, research on industry demand and occupational competence, programme layout and construction for data-related vocational majors, industry-education collaboration in training, competition-based learning and quality improvement, ecosystem building, and international exchange. The 2030 objective is a working model for training high-skill data workers suited to the AI era, with three systems in place — teaching standards, resource development and teacher training — inside an ecosystem coordinated by government, guided by industry and joined by companies.

A second instrument started the same day. The National Data Administration and the Ministry of Education launched a cluster training programme for high-skill data workers, using those seven levers to strengthen hands-on training in the combined use of data and artificial intelligence. CCTV reported on November 24 that the vocational system already offers more than 100 majors in the digital economy and digital technology, spread across more than 12,000 programme sites. Those two numbers are the starting point: the majors exist, but they are scattered across 12,000 sites, and standards, resources and teachers all have to be aligned.

The administration's own emphasis is on conversion — pushing the national data system, companies, research institutes and vocational schools into closer contact so that reforms, industrial scenarios and case studies become teaching resources, and supporting schools and firms that want to go further together.

LookA teaching committee produces standards; it does not allocate resources. Of the seven programmes, only two change what a school can do: programme layout, which decides who may open or close a data major, and teaching standards, which fix hours, workshop conditions and teacher qualifications. The 12,000 sites are a legacy stock. The committee can designate some of them as hosts for cluster training, and the rest will face compliance pressure once standards rise — the same sequence as the earlier double-high plan: set the standard first, then use projects to concentrate money in a few schools. For schools the cost sits in two places. Workshops need computing power, de-identified real business data and software that can actually run a scenario, none of which is covered by per-student funding. And teachers: most data-major lecturers came over from computer science or statistics with little industry practice, so industry-education collaboration stays on paper unless companies commit engineers to timetabled teaching. For students the signal is blunter — standards will sort data degrees, and which institution you choose will matter more than which major.

Technology & Product

技术与产品
11/24 13:50 China News Service via Chinaqw ↗

Twelve art schools pool one national laboratory as shared kit for more than twenty campuses

At 13:50 on November 24 Chinaqw carried a China News Service dispatch from Shanghai by reporter Xu Jing: a university alliance of digital-arts laboratories has been established at Shanghai Theatre Academy. The alliance is meant to connect research, training, creative practice and commercial translation into a single chain across art schools.

Twelve art institutions are the founding members: Shanghai Theatre Academy, Shanghai Innovation Institute, Shanghai Conservatory of Music, the Central Academy of Drama, the Central Conservatory of Music, the Central Academy of Fine Arts, Communication University of China, the National Academy of Chinese Theatre Arts, the China Conservatory of Music, the China Academy of Art, the Beijing Film Academy and the Beijing Dance Academy. Fudan University, Harbin Institute of Technology, Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications and St Petersburg State University are among more than ten participating institutions at home and abroad. Industry support comes from Hunan Performing Arts Group, the Dashijie performing-arts experiment zone, Chuangda Chankong, Shanghai Guo Pai, Shanghai Instrumentation, Alibaba Cloud, Volcano Engine, SenseTime and the IEEE.

The key sentence on governance comes from Shanghai Theatre Academy's party secretary Xie Wei: the academy is willing to treat its national-level digital-arts innovation laboratory as the alliance's shared technical platform and its translation hub, sharing resources, research topics and talent development with members. President Huang Changyong released a four-point working consensus on behalf of the member schools, including a commitment to reproducible, verifiable and iterable artistic research and to keeping technology in the service of humanistic judgment.

The delivery list is a sharing agenda: joint research and co-built projects, with professional datasets, technical tools, experimental methods, findings and production cases opened to members. A "young creation and experiment" forum ran alongside the launch for young scholars, postgraduate students and creators, covering shifts in creative practice, virtual and physical staging, and human-machine co-creation.

LookFor an art school, buying digital kit alone is the worst deal going. Motion capture, virtual production and render farms are expensive and used for only part of the year; a single campus buying a single set may run it below thirty per cent utilisation. Pooling it through one national laboratory saves the duplicate purchases but creates scheduling, maintenance and cross-campus billing — and the alliance's documents do not yet set rules for any of the three, which is what will decide whether the platform is a hub or a showroom. The harder constraint is people: teachers who understand both the technology and the craft are scarce, and equipment can be shared while posts cannot. That the first forum targets postgraduates and young creators is an admission that the first cohort fluent with these tools comes out of graduate study, not the undergraduate classroom. With Alibaba Cloud, Volcano Engine, SenseTime and the IEEE on the industry list, course standards risk being shaped by the tools on offer; and once datasets and tools are genuinely shared, copyright, credit order and commercial boundaries have to be settled in the first joint projects, or sharing stops at the case-study stage.

11/23 17:31 CFi.cn (HKEX filing) ↗

China Education Group guides FY2026 profit down 21-36%, with income tax up as much as 86%

At 17:31 on November 23 China Education Group Holdings (00839.HK) published a supplementary inside-information announcement elaborating on the FY2026 trading update it issued on November 13. The company expects net profit attributable to owners of about RMB 630 million to 770 million for FY2026, against about RMB 977 million in FY2025 — a fall of RMB 207 million to 347 million, or roughly 21% to 36%.

Impairment is the largest single item, and it moves the other way. The group expects non-cash impairment of non-current assets at two cash-generating units in Sichuan and Henan, net of deferred tax, of about RMB 1.06 billion to 1.14 billion; after minority interests, the attributable impairment is about RMB 780 million to 840 million, below the RMB 1.091 billion booked in FY2025, a decrease of RMB 251 million to 311 million. The smaller impairment therefore offsets part of the profit decline rather than causing it, as the announcement states explicitly.

Three operating factors all worsen. Administrative expenses are expected to rise by RMB 150 million to 190 million, or 13% to 16%, over the FY2025 figure of about RMB 1.159 billion, driven by higher indirect taxes and staff costs and by extra depreciation and utilities as new campuses and buildings come into use. Gross profit is expected to fall by RMB 60 million to 100 million, or 1.5% to 2.5%, from about RMB 3.926 billion, because spending on teaching staff and teaching input as campuses and programmes expand outpaces revenue growth. Chinese corporate income tax within the tax charge is expected to rise by RMB 250 million to 290 million, or about 74% to 86%, over the FY2025 figure of about RMB 339 million, mainly from higher current tax.

The announcement also fixes the calendar and the board: audited FY2026 results are expected on or around November 26, 2026. It is signed by company secretary Chen Yuanfeng; the executive directors are Yu Kai and Wang Rui, and the independent non-executive directors are Gerard A. Postiglione, Rui Meng and Wu Jianbing. The figures rest on the board's preliminary assessment of unaudited consolidated management accounts and may be adjusted by the auditors.

LookThe line to watch is not net profit but income tax. A rise of 74% to 86% means RMB 250-290 million more paid, roughly the size of the entire profit decline of RMB 207-347 million. Whether that is a one-off catch-up or a change in the applicable rate decides next year's base; if it is the latter, the tax holiday for private higher education is over and the valuation model has to be rebuilt. Second, the administrative-expense line includes depreciation and utilities on new campuses and buildings, costs that do not come back out — the marginal cost of expansion is rising, and a 1.5-2.5% fall in gross profit shows revenue growth is not converting. Third, the geography of the impairment: writing down two units in Sichuan and Henan is a markdown on enrolment and fee prospects in those provinces, and although impairment is non-cash it encodes management's view of future cash flow. For how the institutions actually run, a falling bottom line is first absorbed by capital spending and teaching posts — the two slowest-moving variables in teaching quality. The audited results on November 26 will give revenue and enrolment, which is what settles whether volume is being bought with price.
