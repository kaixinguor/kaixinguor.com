11/22 09:27 China News Service Hunan ↗

Hunan's five departments issue an AI-in-education plan: 18 tasks, and a system spanning every stage by 2030

At 09:27 on November 22, China News Service's Hunan desk published a breakdown of the plan under the headline "18 tasks in focus: how Hunan will push AI plus education over the next five years". According to the report, the provincial departments of education, development and reform, industry and information technology, science and technology, and data jointly issued the Hunan action plan for AI in education for 2026-2030. Its 18 tasks set a five-year path toward a system that by 2030 covers every stage of schooling, links in-school and out-of-school learning, and brings the province's overall level into the national front rank. Hunan Daily carried the same announcement on page two the same day, with a subtitle naming the university computing alliance and the six application scenarios.

The tasks fall into four ascending blocks: computing and platform foundations, AI literacy for everyone, AI talent cultivation, and full-scenario application. The computing section is the most concrete. The National Supercomputing Centre in Changsha becomes the collection point, the provincial scheduling platform the hub, and a "Hunan university computing alliance" pools campus capacity into one reserve. The province will explore a cost-sharing mechanism and require education computing projects to pass review and filing, so that capacity is built jointly rather than separately. On the platform side, the Xiangjiao Cloud management platform is to achieve "four integrations" with the national smart education platform, a provincial guide for digital campuses is to be issued by level and type, and the programme pushing smart teaching terminals into primary and secondary schools continues. A separate safety paragraph names model hallucination, algorithmic bias and information cocoons as risks, and sets up filing, monitoring, early warning and emergency response for AI products entering schools.

Two hard constraints sit in the literacy and talent sections. For students, AI general education is to be built by stage with a "universal plus distinctive" curriculum, and universities are to make AI a public compulsory course. For teachers, the focus is lesson preparation, tutoring and teaching research, with nationwide and provincial training programmes expected to reach every teacher, AI competence written into teacher training, certification and pre-service preparation, and a new AI category added to provincial teaching achievement awards. On talent, AI subjects enter the provincial list of subjects industry urgently needs; universities with the capacity are encouraged to prioritise smart chips, quantum technology, brain-computer interfaces, large-model algorithms, embodied intelligence and space intelligence, add AI master's and doctoral programmes, and build "AI plus engineering, agriculture, medicine and humanities" clusters. Vocational education is directed at job families including AI application development, agent development and operations, and data governance and annotation.

Application is organised into six scenarios: smart learning, smart teaching, smart assessment, smart management, smart research and smart creation. Smart teaching proposes a three-way "teacher-student-machine" model and AI-assisted team teaching; smart assessment proposes developmental evaluation built on digital profiles plus AI-assisted question setting and marking, subject to human-machine review. The funding paragraph calls for stronger coordination across departments, more money and pilot programmes: existing funds are to be pooled, qualifying projects encouraged to seek local government special bonds, and the provincial Jinfurong fund used to bring in private capital, with selected regions, universities and schools allowed to move first. The report does not give a five-year spending total, a launch date for the computing platform, or a number of pilot schools.

LookThree ledgers actually move here. The first is the computing budget: shifting from separate builds to shared capacity means a university data centre must clear provincial review first, equipment purchases become service payments, and the provincial ledger moves from capital spending to operating spending — which then sits in the annual base. The second is the degree ledger: authority to add AI master's and doctoral programmes rests with the State Council's academic degrees committee, so the province can only build the direction, recruit the team and then apply; that is why the "urgently needed subjects" list comes first in the text. The third is the financing ledger: pooled funds are existing money already committed elsewhere, special bonds must be repaid with interest, and whether an education project generates cash flow decides whether this is construction money or future debt service. The most underrated of the 18 tasks is training every teacher in AI. National and provincial programmes reach teachers on the payroll; the rural and small schools with the biggest gaps are exactly the hardest to schedule, and the cost lands on teachers' holidays and on county in-service training schools.
Another angleMaking computing a utility — used "like water and electricity" — swaps one-off hardware purchases for recurring service fees: cheaper for universities, rigid for the provincial budget. And writing AI competence into teacher certification changes who may enter the profession, which forces normal universities to rewrite their programmes for the same cohort of students — a schedule far harder to align than any of the 18 tasks.

11/22 20:17 Xinhua ↗

South Korea's answer to AI cheating: 30,000 aprons, 111 candidates caught, and a minister gone

At 20:17 on November 22, Xinhua carried a Beijing dispatch by reporter Zhang Jing. AI-assisted cheating has become frequent enough in South Korea that universities are issuing their own countermeasures, and according to Korean media reports on November 22, Kyung Hee University in Seoul prepared about 30,000 disposable white non-woven aprons for exams held on November 21 and 22. Candidates had to wear one; those who refused were not allowed to sit. They could bring no electronic devices and no earplugs, and candidates with long hair had to keep their ears exposed — measures aimed at miniature earpieces and cameras.

Other universities chose differently: metal detectors, tighter checks for AI glasses, and network signal blocking. The education ministry said it had formed an anti-cheating emergency response team with the Korean Council for University Education and universities that set their own papers, to coordinate prevention and follow-up investigations. That is a mechanism built after the fact — it took shape only once the exams were over and the argument had started.

The report traces the incident from its start to its end. On November 15 a candidate who had scored highest on the certified tax accountant examination admitted using AI to cheat and turned himself in to police. In the CSAT — the Suneung, held on November 19 — the education ministry caught 111 candidates carrying smart devices. And because the handling of cheating caused confusion, education minister Choi Kyo-jin resigned on November 22.

The resignation itself was reported by Xinhua's Seoul desk (reporters Chen Yi and Ji Xinlong) and carried by The Beijing News. Choi said in a statement that multiple changes to exam rules just before the test had inconvenienced the public, candidates included, and that as the minister in charge he could not escape criticism that preparation had been inadequate; President Lee Jae-myung accepted the resignation. About 550,000 candidates sat the exam. The rushed measures also included checking candidates' glasses, putting stickers over clothing buttons, centrally issuing some stationery, and sending a top-level disaster alert to every phone in the country on exam day to find handsets that had not been surrendered — some of them published only a day or two before the test. On November 21 Lee said a person reporting a cheating case would receive 100 million won (about US$74,000), calling exam cheating "as serious as corruption by public officials" and promising that cheats would be pursued to the end.

LookThis is exam administration being outrun by technology, and the cost allocation is unusually legible. Kyung Hee's 30,000 aprons are disposable consumables — a few yuan per candidate at the scale of a test centre — but they replace detection with a dress code. A garment that cannot hide a device substitutes for a screening technology nobody can validate, which makes it the cheapest and bluntest fix available. The price falls on candidates: no earplugs, ears exposed, no personal stationery, with both exam-day experience and the cost of appealing a penalty rising. The emergency response team is worth noting for a different reason: it is a post-hoc body, which suggests Korean exam security has long depended on each university improvising until something broke. The lesson for China is timing. The 2027 national postgraduate entrance exam runs on December 19-20, and the Ministry of Education held its exam-security video conference on November 13; rules that land a week before the test reproduce exactly this disorder in any jurisdiction. The 100 million won bounty creates its own moral hazard, which is why officials had to offset it with the clause letting a confessing cheater claim the money.
Another angleTreating AI cheating as a character problem means buying detectors, adding invigilators and printing aprons for every exam, forever. Treating it as a test-design problem is the only route to changing question formats and assessment — and Korea's lesson is that when the countermeasures arrive is itself part of governing capacity.

The people who teach robots: a new occupation with a million-plus gap and no training path

At 08:40 on November 23, People's Daily Online republished a feature from People's Daily Overseas Edition by reporter Dou Hao. It opens in a training hall at the Zhejiang Humanoid Robot Innovation Centre in Haishu district, Ningbo, where Jiang Zebin watches data scroll as a wheeled, dual-arm humanoid rehearses raising an arm, waving and reaching. In September, the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Security and other departments published the eighth batch of new occupations, and "embodied-AI robot application technician" was formally included.

The official definition is specific: using teleoperation rigs, spatial digitisation tools and simulation platforms to collect and process multimodal interaction data, fine-tune and adapt models, deploy and debug hardware and software on site, and monitor and optimise performance. Jiang works on teleoperation, turning algorithms that work in a lab into motions that hold up in the real world. A grasp that looks like it takes seconds, he says, may need hundreds of data sets collected, cleaned and labelled before it can be fed to a model.

The results are measurable. NAVIAI WA2, the wheeled humanoid he trains most, reached 99% success on same-type bin picking and 97% overall in a project with a large international logistics company, and ran for three days without a fault at the Stuttgart logistics fair in March. The centre, jointly built by the Ningbo city government and Professor Xiong Rong's team at Zhejiang University, employs 184 people in the role and more than 200 including interns. Jiang graduated in a robotics-related field from the Technical University of Munich and returned to China at the end of 2023.

On supply, the report is blunt: one survey puts China's embodied-AI talent gap in the millions, and as a new occupation the job "has no established training path yet" — the first cohort taught themselves. Policy is moving alongside. In June, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology and the State-owned Assets Supervision and Administration Commission launched a joint programme of real-world training for humanoid robots and embodied intelligence, with a target of putting 10,000-unit-scale deployments to work by the end of the year; Zhejiang's human resources department and the provincial trade union federation have jointly launched demonstration training for the role. The report does not give the basis for the million-plus estimate, the status of a national vocational skill standard, or how order-based classes would connect to the vocational major catalogue.

LookBeing added to the occupation list settles status, not supply. The skill set spans computer vision, reinforcement learning, electrical fundamentals and on-site fault diagnosis — each of them larger than the class hours any existing vocational programme can spare. The real training cost currently sits in two places: company induction, where a newcomer spends months in a training hall generating hundreds of data sets, and provincial demonstration programmes paid for by the human resources department and the trade union. The million-plus figure has no published methodology, but one inference is safe: if a single centre can only produce people in the low hundreds, closing a million-plus gap cannot come from training schemes. It comes from splitting the job — collection, annotation, deployment, operations — and the segment that vocational colleges can actually absorb is the middle one. For families, the appeal is starting pay and industry momentum; the risk is how fast the skills decay. Someone who can tune teleoperation today may find the model doing it next year.

A trade society parks its industry-education council inside a fashion institute

At 21:30 on November 22, The Paper reported (reporter Cheng Ting) that the China Society for International Trade held its 45th anniversary conference and an industry-education and international cooperation forum in Beijing on November 22, during which it formally established a professional committee for industry-education integration and international cooperation. The committee was approved by the society, is a second-tier body within it, and has its secretariat at Beijing Institute of Fashion Technology.

The numbers are specific. The committee is chaired by He Shien, a vice president of the society, with nine vice chairs and 25 founding members drawn from universities, international organisations, industry alliances and companies. The secretariat handles daily liaison, research programming and events, and works with the institute on governance, research and training, drawing on the school's strengths in textiles, apparel and fashion design to connect the committee's agenda with teaching and research capacity.

International work already has a vehicle. The institute signed a cooperation framework agreement with University College of Future in Malaysia, setting up a standing mechanism for practice-based teaching and cooperation on joint disciplines, joint programmes and student internships; that platform is one of the channels the committee will use. Forum topics included the overseas hiring needs of Chinese companies, the articulation between vocational and degree education, joint programmes between institutions, and overseas practice bases.

The committee's own framing, as quoted, is that industry-education integration is moving from project-by-project cooperation between schools and firms toward an ecosystem coordinated by government, universities, companies and industry, and that companies going abroad need internationally capable graduates. It plans regular cross-disciplinary research sessions, a "visible path from research to employment", and matchmaking between institutions and companies using the society's platform and overseas partners. The report does not disclose the committee's funding, its annual research output or its first-year work plan.

LookThe combination of a second-tier learned-society body and a university secretariat effectively moves the running cost of an industry organisation into one college's administrative budget: liaison, research, events and foreign visits all need staff, while the society supplies standing and contacts. For the school it is a good trade — what a traditional strength like textiles lacks is not research topics but an interface for going abroad. For companies it is a cheap way to meet future hires early. Three things are worth watching: the balance of academics and companies among the 25 founding members, which decides whether the agenda is scholarly or recruitment-driven; whether the "visible path from research to employment" is backed by credit recognition and curriculum mapping, without which it stays a seminar series; and the sequence — the Malaysia agreement came first and the committee second, which suggests the first year is about giving existing cooperation an institutional shell rather than launching anything new.

A normal university turns its physics and chemistry departments into two engineering schools

At 13:13 on November 22, Xinhua's Beijing channel published a Capital Normal University announcement: on November 21 the university held an interdisciplinary development forum and an inauguration for its new School of Physics and Optoelectronic Engineering and School of Chemistry and Materials Engineering in the laboratory building's lecture hall. Both schools were unveiled that day.

President Fang Fuquan, an academician of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, framed the change: upgrading the physics and chemistry departments into schools is how the university serves national strategic needs and Beijing's four-centre functions and advances its double first-class construction. The two schools, he said, should activate basic research, train outstanding science teachers and pursue a path of interdisciplinary innovation in science and engineering distinctive to a normal university. Beijing's education commission director Li Yi said the departments had deep roots in training basic-education teachers and in terahertz optoelectronics and optoelectronic materials, and that the new schools should explore a new-engineering training model of their own while linking to innovation resources in Haidian and Fangshan.

The unveiling line-up and follow-on actions were published together. Academician Sun Changpu, Beijing Institute of Technology physics dean Yao Yugui, Li Yi, party secretary Cao Wenjun and Fang unveiled the physics school; researchers Liu Yunqi and Li Yadong of the Institute of Chemistry, Chinese Academy of Sciences, joined for the chemistry school. Deans Zhang Liangliang and Tian Yang presented their plans, and the schools signed cooperation intents with companies on site, with representatives from Quantum Emergence (Beijing) Technology and BOE Technology attending. Parallel forums covered quantum science, advanced optics, synthetic chemistry, new energy materials and electrochemical storage.

LookA normal university starting engineering schools runs into two hard constraints: students and staff. Physics and chemistry at such universities have long been organised around producing secondary-school teachers; turning them into engineering schools means moving the curriculum toward optoelectronics, materials and storage, importing lab equipment and industry instructors — while the establishment quota and senior academic posts do not grow just because the sign over the door changed. That is the expensive part of "interdisciplinary". The second constraint is admissions: which major codes the new schools use, how many students they take in year one, and whether they enter the double first-class subject portfolio will decide whether they become a second growth engine or another faculty under employment pressure. The information in the ceremony is who showed up: the city's education chief, two CAS academicians, BOE and a quantum startup — government access and industrial scenarios placed on the same stage. What was not disclosed is the value of the cooperation intents, the number of joint labs and the size of the industry-instructor pool, which are the actual tests of whether this is ceremony or mechanism. For Beijing's schools there is a slower effect: if the training of physics and chemistry teachers tilts toward engineering, the supply of secondary-school science teachers will show it in three to five years.

Industry & Capital

行业与资本
11/22 21:27 The Paper ↗

CATL's zero-carbon student contest closes with 10 finalists and a club in more than 100 universities

At 21:27 on November 22, The Paper reported (reporter He Liping) that the final of the first national university zero-carbon science and technology innovation contest was held in Ningde, Fujian, on November 21, organised by the Chinese Society for Environmental Sciences, the talent office of the Ningde party committee and CATL. Ten teams reached the final.

Both top prizes have deployment numbers attached. A Jilin University team won for an industrial wastewater purification technology that works in cold climates and is already used at nine companies. A Shanghai Jiao Tong University team coupled PEM electrolysis, solid-state hydrogen storage and fuel cells to convert between hydrogen and electricity in both directions, addressing the timing mismatch that renewable output creates for the grid.

The contest sits inside a corporate talent programme. It is part of CATL's Qingning initiative, a public-interest programme for zero-carbon talent in universities that the company launched on January 10 this year around "one set of courses and textbooks, one contest, one hundred communities", building a system that spans coursework, competition and peer networks. The Qingning club now covers more than 100 universities. The report does not disclose the programme's annual spending, how the courses enter university curricula, or what happens to the winning projects next.

LookA company-run contest should be read across three ledgers. The first is recruiting: one national competition does résumé screening, technical interviewing and employer branding at once, cheaper than campus tours and better at finding people who can actually build. The second is curriculum: "one set of courses and textbooks" entering universities means company standards entering the classroom, and three unresolved questions — who writes them, who owns the copyright, whether they carry credit — decide whether they are teaching material or an extended brochure. The third is durability: a club in 100-plus universities depends on an annual budget line that usually sits under brand or ESG spending, the first thing cut when capital expenditure tightens. For students the gains are real project experience and a hiring channel; for universities the risk is treating an industry contest as the whole of practice-based learning, since a contest tests whether something can be built while a degree programme must answer whether the graduate will still be capable in four years. Zero carbon has an extra wrinkle: it carries both the national dual-carbon target and the fortunes of the new-energy industry, and when the enthusiasm cools, the club is the first thing to idle.
