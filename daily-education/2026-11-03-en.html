Ministry of Education’s Fujiang programme launches in Guangxi: 14 cities, 20 counties, 60 schools

The Ministry of Education launched its "Fujiang" programme for AI literacy in primary and secondary schools in Nanning, making Guangxi the first province to run it. The programme covers 14 prefecture-level cities, 20 participating counties and 60 schools.

Its stated aim is to build AI literacy among teachers and students in central and western China: tiered training for local administrators, researchers, principals and front-line teachers, a cohort of "seed" AI educators, and lesson materials adapted to ordinary classrooms, with resources pushed toward border, ethnic-minority and rural schools.

The design leans toward border schools, schools in ethnic mountain areas and schools along the canal, and builds a four-tier chain running from the region to the city, the county and the school. Chen Qiuyan, deputy director of the ministry’s basic education department, called for practice rooted in classrooms; Guangxi’s education chief Han Liu said the region wants to distil a replicable "Guangxi model".

Two details stand out. The programme is framed as literacy, and its money goes into training and lesson materials rather than hardware. And 60 schools across 14 cities works out to fewer than five per city — a pilot footprint, not a rollout.

Why it mattersThis is a teachers-first pilot. The hard part of putting AI in classrooms is not compute; it is whether teachers can use it and dare to. Splitting the trainees into administrators, researchers, principals and teachers admits that school-level adoption needs an owner at every layer. Sixty schools says the goal is to test a mechanism — the four-tier chain, seed teachers, reusable lessons — not coverage. The real test comes in two years: whether those 60 schools produce lessons that travel, and whether the seed teachers still use AI after the programme ends. Training without deposit is just software procurement with a teacher attached; lessons that survive are the only evidence that "AI plus education" reached the grassroots.

11/03 03:55 Guangming Daily ↗

Nationwide AI training for teachers: 81% say it saves time, but the barrier is confidence

The Ministry of Education recently convened a national meeting on AI for teacher development and called for full coverage of AI training. In April, a five-ministry "AI plus education" action plan set out to define a standard for teachers’ AI literacy and to add AI to teacher qualification exams and certification.

Demand is well documented: the 2025 national survey of primary and secondary teachers’ digital literacy found 81% say AI saves them preparation time. Liu Jia, a professor at Yangzhou University, found teachers accept "workload-reducing" uses such as marking and learning analytics most readily; training gets harder once the task involves building interactive environments or coordinating multiple agents.

Supply is catching up. On October 10 the national smart-education platform relaunched its teacher development centre with an AI literacy section split into concepts, ethics and hands-on tools; offline training runs through the national teaching programme, cascading from province to school. Wang Lei, head of the ministry’s teacher affairs department, said the biggest obstacle for many teachers is not the technology but "the hurdle in their heads" — fear of being replaced, or of being left behind.

Classrooms are already improvising. Jin Wenyang, a physics teacher in Tianjin, packages assessment analysis into a fixed workflow: upload the data, generate a class dashboard, then produce an individual plan for each student — with every plan reviewed by a teacher before it is sent. Mo Weihong in Xiangtan takes the opposite order, feeding school requirements and her own past lesson plans into a model so it learns her approach before generating new material.

The boundary is being argued too. Zhang Weichun of the China Association for Educational Technology says dependence "is not about using it a lot, but about not knowing when and how" — letting students work out a locus on paper before showing an AI animation is not the same lesson as playing the animation first. Liu Jia recommends approved-tool whitelists for schools, under a principle that AI assists but does not replace, and tools do not overstep.

Why it mattersThe problem this training addresses is not the teachers who cannot use AI but the two ends: those afraid to try and those who overdo it. The 81% figure validates the workload case, but there is a capability gap between reducing work (marking, analytics) and teaching with AI (designing lessons, judging students). The first standardises; the second depends on professional judgement. Adding AI to teacher certification writes that gap into the entry requirement. Three things to watch: what the standard actually tests and who administers it, whether training hours count toward continuing education, and whether school whitelists turn into a new procurement list.

11/02 China Education Daily ↗

First forum on running schools abroad: an annual reporting system, and a push for "small, light" campuses

The 2026 China International Education Conference held its first dedicated forum on overseas campuses, hosted by Hebei University of Technology and attended by more than a hundred university international officers, under the theme "openness for renewal, cooperation for reach".

Xi Ru, a second-level inspector at the ministry’s international cooperation department, said overseas campuses are an important part of education opening-up, with platforms such as the Overseas Excellence Engineer Academies and Luban Workshops already established. Next, the ministry will explore an annual reporting system and monitoring indicators, and promote a model she described as "small and beautiful, benefiting people, asset-light", so that institutions expand in an orderly way.

Cases spanned Central Asia, ASEAN and Europe, mixing universities and vocational colleges: Northwest A&F University’s Tashkent branch, Hebei University of Technology’s Finland campus, and Luban Workshops. Astana International University described its "two-pillar" cooperation with China.

A China-Finland dialogue closed the forum, with LUT University’s head of educational development and front-line teachers discussing student growth, staff development, cross-cultural understanding and what actually happens inside a campus.

Why it mattersAnnual reporting plus indicators moves overseas campuses from an approval regime toward registration with evaluation. The old problem was never opening a campus; it was that nobody kept the books afterwards — how much went in, how many students enrolled, where graduates went, where the faculty came from. The value of reporting is not another form but the first basis for comparing quality across institutions. "Asset-light" is the realistic part: building a campus abroad costs hundreds of millions of yuan, while renting space, partnering and exporting courses is what most institutions can afford. For universities this shifts internationalisation from building campuses to building curricula; for regulators, how the indicators are defined matters more than the rule itself.

11/02 Nansha District Education Bureau ↗

Guangzhou’s Nansha names two unlicensed tutoring cases: 1,340 yuan confiscated, 1,608 yuan fined

On November 2, the Nansha District Education Bureau in Guangzhou published two typical cases of illegal off-campus tutoring, both investigated and penalised by the district’s integrated enforcement bureau.

In the first case, a cultural consulting company ran tutoring without approval. Under articles 17 and 42 of the interim measures on administrative penalties for off-campus tutoring, it was ordered to stop, had 1,340 yuan of illegal gains confiscated and was fined 1,608 yuan.

In the second, an education consulting company ran tutoring and an unlicensed tutoring institution. Under the same articles it was ordered to shut the institution and had 2,536 yuan of illegal gains confiscated.

The bureau said it will keep regulating tutoring providers and consolidating the results of the sector clean-up.

Why it mattersThe two penalties add up to less than 5,000 yuan, but the notice itself is the point. One case combines confiscation with a fine, the other confiscation with closure; the penalties scale with illegal gains, which is the proportionality principle in practice. For a consulting firm, the real cost is not 1,608 yuan but closure and the public naming. It is also a reminder that tutoring under the label of "cultural" or "education consulting" is what enforcement now looks for — a business licence is not a school permit. Watch the density of enforcement: monthly district notices would mean routine; a handful a year still means demonstration.

11/02 China Education Daily ↗

National embodied-AI industry-education consortium forms in Harbin: 513 members across 29 provinces

A national consortium for industry-education integration in embodied AI held its founding meeting in Harbin, led jointly by Northeastern University, Harbin Vocational and Technical College and Leju Robotics of Shenzhen.

The 513 member institutions come from 29 provinces, regions and municipalities. Officials from the Ministry of Education, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, and provincial and municipal education departments attended, alongside colleges, companies and industry associations.

The meeting published a joint programme for building talent-training and assessment systems for embodied AI, plus a textbook system, covering standards, practical courses, teacher development and teaching resources.

Outside the hall, Leju set up working scenarios for data collection — medicine sorting, a logistics line, home services — bringing real production steps into the venue. Harbin Vocational and Technical College said it will work on joint programmes, shared curricula, shared faculty, shared facilities and joint technical work.

Why it matters"Consortium" has been used loosely for years. Whether one is real comes down to three things: textbooks developed together, training facilities shared, and jobs that companies actually pay for. This meeting delivered the first — a textbook and assessment system. Five hundred and thirteen members across 29 provinces is scale, not achievement. The skills gap in embodied AI is genuine: robotics firms need people who understand mechanics and models and can debug on site, a profile traditional majors do not produce directly. What to watch: whether the consortium turns real work such as data collection into course modules, and what share of its first graduates land jobs in the field.

11/02 China Education Daily ↗

Hefei’s five industry-education consortia report: 90% job-match rate in the Visionox track

The Hefei (Xinzhan) high-tech industry-education alliance convened a review of five municipal consortia covering information technology application, smart internet of things, high-end equipment manufacturing, new energy storage, and new displays and integrated circuits. Each presented results and, item by item, its shortfalls and plans for dedicated funding.

The most concrete numbers came from the display and chip consortium: Hefei Vocational and Technical College and Visionox, partners since 2020, report that more than 90% of graduates in mechatronics and mechanical design go into matched jobs, and that 25% of them have become technical leads or front-line managers. Their joint "Visionox field engineer class" is now in its third cohort, with company executives and engineers teaching in person.

The energy-storage consortium focused on research collaboration: the college and Gotion High-Tech built a joint innovation platform and a micro-scale materials research centre, run on a principle of problems set by industry, projects as drivers, real work assessed by the market. Companies’ genuine needs enter the college’s research priorities, and teachers take on commissioned projects.

The shortfalls were tabled too: progress differs across supply chains, and some consortia still see shallow company involvement, with cooperation stuck at project level rather than embedded in daily teaching and production.

The Hefei (Xinzhan) alliance joined the ministry’s first batch of national city-level industry-education alliances in 2023 and runs on a "one body, two parks, three platforms, four alliances" structure.

Why it mattersThe 90% is not the story; the shortfall list is. Industry-education deals are easy to sign and easy to forget; putting five consortia on one stage to present results and name their gaps changes the test from "is there cooperation" to "how far did it get". A 90% match rate and 25% becoming team leads are verifiable outputs — but from one company and one programme, so they do not generalise. The dividing line is the word "embedded": if cooperation stays at classes and internships, it is a project; only when company needs enter teaching plans, teacher evaluation and school budgets does it become an institution. Watch how the dedicated money is spent — on equipment it is a one-off; on curriculum and faculty it might leave something behind.

88th China Education Equipment Exhibition closes: 700+ exhibitors, 29.6% in research and technical services

The 88th China Education Equipment Exhibition closed on November 1 at the Changsha International Convention and Exhibition Centre after a three-day run, hosted by the China Educational Equipment Industry Association with the Hunan education department and Changsha’s municipal government.

Its theme was "AI leads high-quality development of education equipment", with displays of AI, big data, cloud computing and virtual reality in smart classrooms, lab teaching, campus management and personalised learning. Academic sessions, buyer-supplier matching, product launches and demonstrations ran alongside.

Of more than 700 exhibitors, companies in scientific research and technical services accounted for 29.6%, and those in information transmission, software and IT services for 13.9%. The organisers read the mix as a shift "from scale expansion to value deepening".

Why it mattersPut 29.6% and 13.9% together and the supply side has already changed: fewer boxes, more software and services. That is good for schools — hardware is bought once and depreciated over three years, while software is paid for continuously — but it raises new questions about budget lines, data ownership, and whether data can leave when a vendor does. The theme moved from "digitalisation" to "AI leads", which is vendors following the policy vocabulary. The real sorting comes next year: when local budgets tighten while AI features are still demos, the survivors will be the companies that embedded AI in everyday teaching rather than selling it as a standalone exhibit.

Fifteen listed education companies report 18.775 billion yuan, 8 growing and 10 profitable

Shanghai Securities News’ tally of third-quarter reports shows 15 listed education companies in China posting about 18.775 billion yuan of revenue for the first nine months of 2026.

Eight of them grew revenue and 10 stayed profitable. Several are shifting toward efficiency in existing businesses and looking for a second growth curve.

AI is both the main tool for cutting costs and the main thread for new growth, but the pace of implementation differs widely and the results show up unevenly in earnings.

Why it mattersEighteen and three-quarter billion yuan across 15 companies averages 1.25 billion each; with 10 profitable and 8 growing, the sector has moved past the stage where telling an AI story lifts the stock. AI revenue for education companies comes from roughly three places: hardware and software sold to schools, courses sold to individuals, and internal cost cuts. The first two depend on budgets and household spending; the third shows up only in the expense ratio. So the metric that verifies "AI delivery" is not the number of launch events but gross margin and revenue per employee. Watch two things: whether operating cash flow improves — prepayments are the lifeblood of education firms — and whether R&D intensity rises with AI spending. Growth with falling margin and rising R&D means AI is still a cost line.

11/02 10:21 Duozhi ↗

Tokyo’s Gecipe closes a 660 million yen Series B: 600,000 lessons and a 96% renewal rate

Gecipe, a Tokyo-based immersive-education startup, completed the final close of its Series B, taking the round to 660 million yen (about 27.82 million yuan). Investors include existing backer Nippon Venture Capital and new entrants Kyoto Capital Partners, Colopl Next, SBI Okasan Alternative Investment, Resona Bank and the Japan Finance Corporation.

The money goes into expanding the immersive-education business, hiring, organisation building and preparation for an IPO. Founded in 2018, the company builds education services on esports and virtual worlds.

Its core product, eSpi!, teaches conversational English inside game scenarios, so learners speak while cooperating in play. Launched in 2020, it passed 600,000 cumulative lessons by September 2026, with a renewal rate above 96% and more than 95,000 reviews averaging 4.8 out of 5.

Beyond consumers, Gecipe has begun selling to schools and local governments, offering digital English programmes to municipal schools; several local authorities have adopted it.

Why it mattersThe informative numbers are not the 660 million yen but the 96% renewal rate and 600,000 lessons — retention is the hardest part of any education product, and using game cooperation instead of vocabulary drills turns practice into a social act. What it is doing next matters too: moving from consumers to local-government and school procurement. That route carries lower gross margin but steadier cash flow and cheaper acquisition; for a company preparing to list, public-sector revenue is a valuation anchor. The risk sits in the same place: Japanese education digitalisation follows fiscal cycles, so a tighter municipal budget slows growth quickly. Six hundred and sixty million yen is not a large round in Tokyo; what it tests is whether the model can cross the line from "fun but temporary" to durable.
