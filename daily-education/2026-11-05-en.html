Ministry warns 2027 gaokao applicants: four scam types, one official channel

Registration for the 2027 national college entrance exam has begun, with many provinces running their online windows from mid-to-late October into early November; arts and sports candidates register on the same schedule. On November 4 the Ministry of Education sorted through recent years of registration-season fraud and published a warning.

The warning separates the scams into four. First, claims of an "internal re-registration" or a purchasable quota. Second, text messages saying a review failed, with links to pages that imitate the official portal and ask for ID numbers, bank card details and one-time codes. Third, demands to send fees, activation charges or expediting charges to a private account. Fourth, fixers who offer household registration, school roll or social insurance paperwork for a large fee and promise eligibility or bonus points.

On re-registration the notice is explicit: some provinces state in writing that late applications are not accepted and no second round is arranged, while others allow a tightly limited round set by the provincial exam authority. Those exceptions share four features — fixed timing, restricted eligibility, a single entry point, published conditions.

Review results and payment status can only be checked in the provincial exam authority's own system. Education departments and exam bodies do not send standalone text messages containing unfamiliar links about a failed review. Applicants who use false information or forged documents to obtain eligibility or preferential treatment are recorded in the integrity file and have registration, admission or enrolment cancelled.

Why it mattersRead this as a map of channels rather than a warning. Four scams sit on four steps — eligibility, review, payment, bonus points — and each step is meant to have one door, the provincial exam authority's site. Why now? Because registration is one of the few high-stakes procedures families complete themselves, which makes the information gap wide and anxiety easy to price. For schools the lever is the form teacher: whether a student who gets a suspicious text asks first or clicks first depends on whether the school pushed the official link and the re-registration rules into every class group. For parents, what actually protects them is not memorising four slogans but knowing that the registration fee goes to exactly one public account.
The other angleLook at admissions governance and the warning has a second face: the verification chain keeps lengthening. Household registration, school roll and social insurance are checked jointly by police and education departments and then published. Each added step becomes another selling point for a middleman. What shrinks the fraud market is not the frequency of warnings but the openness of results — once a parent can look up the status and the basis of their own record, "inside information" loses its buyers.

11/04 Ministry of Education ↗

University presidents' forum in Hong Kong: 49 leaders, 12 agreements

The World Summit of University Presidents, initiated by Hong Kong University of Science and Technology and Fudan University, met in Hong Kong on November 4 under the theme "reshaping higher education: innovation leads the future". Education minister Huai Jinpeng gave the keynote, "the changing times of universities and their future mission", and proposed a standing high-level dialogue platform, open and trusted cooperation, and joint projects aimed at global challenges.

Attendance was 23 presidents from universities including Cambridge, UCLA, Sydney and the Technical University of Munich, and 26 from Peking, Tsinghua, Hong Kong, the Chinese University of Hong Kong and Macau, 49 in all.

The forum issued a list of ten issues its participants consider urgent and ran three strands — the university in the age of AI, the university and society, the university and civilisation. Twelve institutions signed cooperation agreements: Shanghai Jiao Tong, Nanjing, Southeast, Zhejiang, Central South, HKUST, Barcelona, Monash, Nanyang Technological, Warwick, Newcastle and Khalifa.

Side events covered a life-sciences open alliance forum for young scholars and an AI open alliance forum, which published ten open questions in life sciences and five technology lists in AI. Ban Ki-moon, the eighth UN secretary-general, and the president of UNESCO's general conference spoke by video.

Why it mattersForums like this produce text and bilateral agreements; they do not move a quota or a budget. The agenda is the signal. Putting AI inside "the university and society" and "the university and civilisation" instead of giving it a technology track of its own says that presidents are thinking about disciplinary structure and evaluation, not compute purchases. Six of the twelve signatories are mainland institutions, and the usual forms are joint training and research collaboration, which pay off over years — count dual-degree programmes and joint labs in five years rather than photographs now. For Hong Kong the summit is a way to turn "international higher-education hub" into an annual mechanism. For mainland universities the real barrier is not signing but whether credit transfer and degree-awarding rules can be made to fit.

11/05 China Education Daily ↗

Canadian Teachers' Federation names four AI risks in K-12, from data to the digital divide

Canada's teachers' federation has published "Protecting students, educators and public education in the age of AI", examining the risks that arrive with artificial intelligence in primary and secondary schools and arguing for a human-centred floor, China Education Daily reported on November 5.

The first risk is over-appropriation of student and teacher data. The report wants children's data classified as sensitive, a ban on using it to train models without explicit permission, school AI systems designated high-risk products with mandatory risk assessment before deployment, and a national independent regulator.

The second is erosion of young people's reasoning: the answer is to prioritise AI literacy, train teachers specifically, build curricula by stage, set safety standards and fund tools that fit real classrooms. The third is mental health, where the report singles out companion-style products that use emotional manipulation to keep adolescents engaged, and proposes legislation against manipulative design for minors plus more human counselling capacity.

The fourth is the digital divide. Underfunded schools can afford only free versions with lower accuracy; training data skews to English, which affects French-language and Indigenous students; and marginalised students are more exposed to profiling and algorithmic discrimination. The remedies are public digital learning platforms, hardware and connectivity for poorer districts, and multilingual, Indigenous-appropriate resources.

Why it mattersThe report's contribution is to cut "AI in schools" into four objects that a regulator can actually hold: data, cognition, mental health, equity — each matched to a tool, whether classification, high-risk designation, procurement standards or a public platform. For Chinese readers the lesson is less the conclusions than the method of proof: it writes the accuracy gap between free and paid versions straight into the digital divide, an entry that most local procurement budgets never carry. A school on a free model saves money and pays in learning quality. To act on any of this, a district first needs an inventory of AI products in its schools with a risk grade attached; otherwise headteachers decide on the strength of a vendor demo, and the demo is always the best version.

Nantong's industry-education consortium gets a governing council: 20-plus joint programmes, 8 training bases

On November 3 the Nantong Economic and Technological Development Area's "three new, two high" advanced manufacturing city-level industry-education consortium held the founding meeting of its governing council at Jiangsu College of Engineering and Technology, with about 40 people attending from the college, the development area party committee, the municipal education, industry and human resources bureaus, member units and partner companies.

Since being named a Jiangsu pilot consortium in 2024, the group has mapped the local industrial base, run order-based classes at scale and built more than 20 jointly designed programmes and eight training bases. The college describes the council as the start of "a new stage of operating as a real entity".

The meeting read out and seated the council, badged the order-based and field-engineer classes, and saw partner companies donate equipment and software. Beijing Zhiqi Lanmo presented its "gangketong" platform, and the four parties — government, industry, employers and colleges — discussed cluster training for skilled workers and the transfer of research results.

A separate session looked at building an "industry-education brain". The college says the next step is joint programme design, shared curricula, shared faculty and shared facilities in service of regional manufacturing.

Why it mattersMoving from pilot to council changes who decides. Twenty-plus programmes and eight bases are the stock; the council now has to allocate the flow — who pays for the next equipment purchase, which programme expands first, who carries the depreciation on donated machines. Donations are welcome, but they can lock a college's maintenance budget to one vendor's brand, an item that rarely appears in integration stories. Order-based classes and field-engineer classes were badged on the same day, and they differ in how they weed: one recruits to a post, the other screens for capability. If the council meets without controlling money, "real entity" stays three words in a document.

Guangxi's fourth skills competition closes: 116 teams, 981 competitors, 33 events

The fourth Guangxi vocational skills competition closed at the Nanning International Convention and Exhibition Centre on November 3 after two days of competition. A total of 116 teams and 981 competitors took part across 33 events.

Half the events are new technology: drone systems, agricultural drone piloting, robot system integration, industrial robot maintenance, industrial vision maintenance, service robotics, intelligent connected vehicle assembly and servicing, AI trainer, HarmonyOS application development, IoT installation and commissioning, and smart security systems.

The other half are the old trades: bricklaying, tiling, welding, electrical work, refrigeration and air conditioning, freight forwarding, restaurant service, hotel reception, childcare, elder care, nursing assistance, pattern-making, agricultural and food inspection — plus four noodle events for local specialities.

Teams came mostly from cities and colleges, with a minority from employers such as China Petroleum No. 6 Construction, Liuzhou Iron and Steel, and a Nanning hotel group. Gold, silver and bronze medals were awarded in every event, and the full list was published by the regional human resources department.

Why it mattersMore than ten of the 33 events touch AI, robotics or intelligent vehicles, and not one of bricklaying, welding or tiling has disappeared: the event list is a snapshot of demand, with new lanes added and old trades retained. Nine hundred and eighty-one competitors spread across 116 teams is 8.5 per team, which tells you the pipeline still runs through colleges and municipal delegations rather than employers. For a school, the prize is not the medal but whether an event's scoring rubric can be turned into a course. The assessment criteria for an "AI trainer" event, item by item, describe what an employer means by someone who can do the job — far more useful to a teaching group than a medal in a cabinet.

World MOOC conference: a digitalisation index covering 101 countries, and ten AI course awards

The 2026 World Conference on MOOCs and Online Education opened at Vietnam National University in Hanoi on November 3 under the theme "reordering intelligence: the future of education for human-machine coexistence". Vietnam's deputy prime minister, UN under-secretary-general and UN University rector Tshilidzi Marwala, and UNESCO's assistant director-general for education spoke at the opening.

Tsinghua president Li Luming, who chairs the Global MOOC and Online Education Alliance, and Vietnam National University president Bui The Duy released the World Higher Education Digitalisation Development Report 2026 and its companion index, built on data from 101 countries.

Four plenary tracks covered university transformation, future talent and capabilities, AI ethics and governance in education, and open cooperation. The conference debuted a concept it calls "reallocating intelligence".

Awards went to ten alliance courses and ten AI-special courses; about 39 universities, companies and organisations brought cases to an exhibition. Some 300 delegates from 35 countries and regions and 142 institutions attended. The alliance admitted the University of Johannesburg and Queen Mary University of London, and Hong Kong University of Science and Technology will host the 2027 edition.

Why it mattersPut 101 countries beside ten AI course awards and the competition in MOOCs has shifted from how many courses are online to how intelligent and comparable they are. For Chinese universities the useful part is the index's construction: if it ranks on access rates, completion and credit recognition, investment will follow those numbers; if it counts courses, it is a showcase again. Thirty-nine exhibitors and 300 delegates is a modest footprint, yet the report, the awards and the council meeting all land in the same week, which is how international-organisation conferences work — the output is standard-setting, not product. With the 2027 edition in Hong Kong, Asia-Pacific participation should rise, and credit-recognition talks may move faster there.

Kaiwen Education to list a subsidiary: 241.3m yuan appraised, 613.8m across three

Kaiwen Education (002659) filed a progress announcement on November 5. To speed up asset disposal, rebalance its portfolio and reduce the drag from underused fixed assets, the company will transfer 100% of three subsidiaries through a public listing on the Beijing Equity Exchange, as approved by its board on September 14 and by an extraordinary shareholders' meeting on September 30.

The appraised values are 241.3093 million yuan for Kaiwen Hengxin, 185.9057 million for Kaiwen Renxin and 186.5685 million for Kaiwen Camping, a combined 613.7835 million.

The Haidian district state-owned assets regulator has issued approval 海国资发〔2026〕207号 and an asset-valuation approval 海国资发〔2026〕208号, allowing the company to list Kaiwen Hengxin at no less than the approved appraised value of 241.3093 million yuan.

Kaiwen will proceed first with Hengxin and list the other two "when the timing allows". The notice also warns that the buyer cannot yet be identified and that completion and timing remain uncertain.

Why it mattersThree subsidiaries worth 613.8 million, and the first to go is the largest at 241.3 million. This is not a fire sale but a three-step disposal: get the approvals and the sanctioned valuation in hand, then use the first listing's result to calibrate the price of the other two. Kaiwen's core business is in Haidian — private schools plus education services — while the three subsidiaries sit in education technology and camping. Selling the heavy assets and keeping the operation improves the balance sheet and cash, but consolidated revenue shrinks with it. For parents and enrolled students the question is continuity: after the operating entity changes, do fees, staffing and student records carry over? None of that is in the announcement; it will show up in the school's licence amendments and its own notices. For peers, the case offers a pricing reference — the value of an education subsidiary ends up locked twice, first by state approval, then by what the exchange actually clears.
